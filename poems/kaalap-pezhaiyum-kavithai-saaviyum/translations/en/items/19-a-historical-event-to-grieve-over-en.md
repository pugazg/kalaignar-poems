# A Historical Event to Grieve Over!

<!-- scan 99 -->

That Senguttuvan was a son born of the Chola line,
and that his mother
was Narchonai, daughter of Karikala Chola —
this is what *Silappathikaram* tells us.

What does the *Pathitrupathu* decade say?
That the maiden's name
was Manakkilli, daughter of the Chola.

Since it is natural enough
for one person to bear two names,
there is no need to magnify this
and become confused.

Greater confusions than this
raise their heads in Tamil history.
There are three —
first, second and third Karikalas;

and the victories won by those three
collide with one another
until we struggle to know
which victory belonged to whom!

As though there were another Kuttuvan,
there is one called Velkezhu Kuttuvan;
yet scholars say he is no other man —
only another name
for Cheran Senguttuvan himself!

In England, from Henry the First
to Henry the Eighth,
however many Henrys may come,
time and history remain exact.

Here, because literature and inscriptions
are what have come down to us as history,
I would say that only as far as possible
have we been able to research
and fix the dates with certainty!

<!-- scan 100 -->

The peerless warrior who went to the Himalaya and won;
the Chera who, whenever the occasion came,
added glory to the Tamils;

the great king who held honour alone
to be the good life,
the valiant Tamil who stood like a great mountain
to guard his people —

“there is none who can defeat him,” people said;
“whoever comes against him will fall,”
the words rang out;

and as Senguttuvan's fame spread everywhere
like a full, resounding note,
some kings who ruled the earth
boiled over with jealousy!

Like embers that refuse to die,
jealousy and unbearable irritation —
seeing a Tamil's greatness,
these arise in another Tamil himself;

and among those kings,
one who sprang up so
was Palaiyan of Mogur.

To finish his enmity
and send the Chera to the burial pit,
Palaiyan made new plans,
wove wicked plots,
prepared for war,
and had the war-drum beaten.

On the frontier of his country
was a chieftain called Arugan;
thinking the time had come,
he leapt upon Palaiyan
in support of the Chera.

Palaiyan's anger against Arugan
was a thousand times fiercer
than even his anger against the Chera;
he rose first to destroy him.

Escaping that assault,
the chieftain Arugan resolved
to bring Palaiyan down
and throw an unforgettable challenge
at his honour;
he joined the Chera army
and broke Palaiyan's fort!

Senguttuvan, who appeared
like a lion-bull at the battle-front,
shattered Mogur and roared —

<!-- scan 101 -->

“Wherever you may be,
I shall not let you go!”
He surged forward
and captured Palaiyan too.

He cut down completely
the neem that stood
as Mogur's guardian tree;

and, ordering the elephants of Mogur,
now bound under his rule,
to drag away the felled tree,
that incomparable Tamil king
returned to his capital.

As soon as he returned,
Arugan moved to fulfil his own desire —
to settle his score with Palaiyan.

To make a hauling-rope
for tying and dragging the guardian tree,
he summoned the warriors
who obeyed his commands;

he ordered the hair of the women
of Palaiyan's palace to be cut,
twisted into a rope,

and told them to bind the guardian tree
with those ropes and drag it.

Hearing this,
the Chera spat fire at Arugan:
a stain had fallen
upon the victory he had won.

“Using my support, Arugan,
you have settled your own enmity.

To show the anger you bore toward Palaiyan,
you sent forces
and took your revenge;
yet —

to bind and drag
the guardian tree of an enemy land,
against all culture,

you ordered the hair
of the women of Palaiyan's family to be cut.
You may call it an act of revenge —

<!-- scan 102 -->

No! No!
A base act!
A base act, I call it!”
said the Chera.

He was deeply troubled;
not only the guardian tree —

he released Palaiyan too
from captivity,
and only then eased his grief.

Among the Tamils of the Dravidian people
there was valour, there was wisdom,
there was ability whose fame scented all eight directions;

there was the force of a storming tiger in war,
and an honour that would never show its back
to an enemy!

There was fearlessness
that never left them for even an instant,
meeting death in battle
with a spear borne in the chest;

there was a summit of devotion to art
that crossed the surging seas;
there was the strength of principle
that would not let its stand collapse —

but,
but,
alas —
among the Tamils,
that one thing alone,
unity of the people,
was absent.

Is that not why
this event of history
must make us grieve so deeply!
