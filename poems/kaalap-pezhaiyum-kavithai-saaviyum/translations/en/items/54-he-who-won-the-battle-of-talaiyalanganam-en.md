# He Who Won the Battle of Talaiyalanganam!

<!-- scan 271 -->

He who won
the battle of Talaiyalanganam!

I had written:

“Since some Pandyas
have already found a place
in many of my portraits in writing,

rather than narrating
that Pandya history
again in full detail,

let us continue
with the history of the Pandyas
who created a golden age
in the Last Sangam period
and afterwards.”

Seeing that,
some younger brothers
became angry:

“No matter —
however many times you write it,

ancient Tamil history
never tires us when we read it!

Even if many years have passed,
write it again —

do not pass beyond
our request.”

They pierced me
through the telephone
with those words.

Finding pleasure
even in that trouble,
I begin again.

★ ★ ★

From my youth itself,
I had a fascination
for the names Anbumagan
and Nedunchezhiyan.

At a time
when they had not yet
entered the movement directly,

I, as a young man,
was already involved.

Accepting the invitation
to the anniversary
of *Murasoli*,
which we had issued
as a handbill in Aarur,

both of them captivated me that day
with speeches matched in splendour!

<!-- scan 272 -->

Like Anbumagan
and Nedunchezhiyan,
I too wanted to change my name
and fulfil a long-held desire.

On the advice
of dear elder Annal Thango,

I sought Anna's permission
to change it to
“Arutselvam.”

He said:

“The name has already
become known and spread;

so do not change it now.”

I thought it over too.

Because my name
was common to people
of every religion,

and was not the name
of any particular deity,

I decided
not to interrupt matters now
with a change.

So “Karunanidhi”
remained unchanged;

and I too remained
unchanged in the Kazhagam.

In thought as well,
strong and clear,

having acquired
in the Erode and Kanchi camps
the temper to struggle,

I became
a “permanent fighter.”

Even when those
who grew the party with us
later stood apart,

I have wished them
life with dignity and distinction.

So too was the friendship
of Nedunchezhiyan,
who captivated my thought.

When I heard
that he had died,

I left the capital Delhi
that very night,

and with a heart broken apart
hurried toward his home.

A crowd without principle
surrounded the doorway,

mocked me,
raised shouts:

“Have you come now?”

Little men
with hateful hearts
like Kutilan,

barked like curs
and tried to leap forward
and bite.

<!-- scan 273 -->

Without knowing
the depth of our friendship,

they shouted thanklessly
and babbled.

Even there,
I showed patience,
paid my final respects,
and returned.

Though Navalar
had gone over
to the enemy camp,

my heart held
an incomparable affection

for the individual friend
Nedunchezhiyan,

and for that Tamil name.

So whenever the names
Nedunchezhiyan,
Karikalan,
Senguttuvan

appear in the pages
of Tamil Nadu's history,

I want their honoured names
to find a place
in my classical-Tamil verse.

To fulfil the request
of the younger brothers —

instead of a veena
plucked with the cry
“Let them return again” —

here are the extended lines
of verse,

in two parts
inside the Chest of Time!

★ ★ ★

Chera,
Chola,
Ezhini,
Thithiyan,
Irungo Venman — five;

with Porunan
and Erumaiyuran joining them,

seven armies
stood on the battlefront.

Young-bull Nedunchezhiyan
set out to meet them,

saw the battlefield,
won it,

seized the enemy drums,
fulfilled his vow,

and became
king of kings —

so sings the rare poet Nakkirar,
with delight,
in *Akananuru*!

“As though sun and moon together
had fallen upon the earth,

the great Chera and Chola kings
fell upon the battlefield” —

through a *Puram* poem,
poet Kalladanar
praises the learned young Pandyan!

<!-- scan 274 -->

“For one man to fight another,
and for one of the two to fall,
is natural!

But that one man
fought without fault
to bend and subdue seven,

that all seven lost,
and he alone won —

I call this
a wonder in this world!”

So poet Idaikkunrur Kizhar
shaped in writing
the victory of the young Pandyan.

Unable to bear the grief
of Tamil kings
colliding among themselves,

poet Kudapulaviyanar called it:

“Talaiyalanganam,
where Tamil leadership
was thrown into confusion.”

The broad-shouldered Pandyan
won in the end;

so the poet immediately
longed to embrace him.

His heroic chest,
spread wide
like a hunter's tiger-trap —

“I wish to rush forward
and embrace it,”

cried that poet
in expansive Tamil,

and embraced
the pearl-garlanded chest!

See how that Tamil poet
describes
the battlefield of Talaiyalanganam!

Arrows flew
against fierce foes;

suddenly
the enemy elephants fell.

When warriors
cut with swords
the trunks of fallen elephants
along with their mouths,

those severed pieces
lay scattered across the field,

looking,
it is said,
like ploughs
along cultivated land!

Many commanders
died and fell.

Beside them,
their sons too
lay dead.

Old women,
with wrinkled brows,

stood around,
wept and lamented;

<!-- scan 275 -->

and yet took pride
that they had borne
such warrior-sons:

“What victory
could be greater than this?”

Mangudi Maruthanar,
great poet
of Nedunchezhiyan Pandyan's court,

marvelled at the skill
of the guardian-king
who came crowned
with battlefield victory.

He rejoiced —

but when he saw
the king surrounded
by an unending desire
for battle,

he grew weary of it!

To reduce
the great Pandyan warrior's
thirst for war,

Mangudi Maruthanar
composed the work
called *Maduraikanchi*.

In a *Puram* poem
that Maruthanar sang:

“O Pandyan,
whose elephant-army
cuts through the field
like a wooden ship
cutting through the sea!

Behind it,
your spear-bearing warriors
cut off the heads
of crowned kings
and make them the hearth;

in a furnace
where blood-water boils,

they cook the flesh
of those killed in war,

stirring the food
with shoulders as paddles,

and conduct
a battlefield sacrifice!”

After praising him
in many such ways,

Maruthanar praises
his enemies as well:

“Though they received
the name ‘foes’
and were defeated by you,

because they fell
bearing honour-wounds,

they live forever
in the world of warriors!”

Thus he raised up
even those who opposed the king
and scattered pearls of verse.

“Let the king now
leave war behind
and live while sword-warriors
praise him!” he said.

<!-- scan 276 -->

“Stand as a generous giver
and give life
to those who have nothing!”
he said.

“Drink the clear liquor
given by bangle-wearing women;

let all life hold
pleasure and peace;

shine so that the world
may praise you!” he said.

“Know this:
those who guard good conduct
are the ones who live exalted;

those without it
are not to be counted
among those who live
in the world!” he said.

As the gathering
of Tamil poets praised him,

as every Tamil letter
honoured him,

the man of Talaiyalanganam lived!

Receiving *Maduraikanchi*,
which Maruthanar composed
and gave him,

the great king lived!

And when Nakkirar
sang and gave him
*Nedunalvadai*,

the young Nedunchezhiyan
lived long
and planted enduring fame
upon Tamil soil!

★ ★ ★

**Note:**

*Akananuru* — poem 36 — sung by Nakkirar.

*Purananuru* — poem 25 — sung by Kalladanar.

*Purananuru* — poem 76 — sung by Idaikkunrur Kizhar.

*Purananuru* — poem 19 — sung by Kudapulaviyanar.

*Purananuru* — poems 24, 26 — sung by Mangudi Maruthanar.

(The collection of ideas contained in the poems composed by these distinguished poets about Pandyan Nedunchezhiyan, who won the battle of Talaiyalanganam, forms one part of the compilation of Pandya history.)
