# The Iron Pillar and the Wings of Flies!

<!-- scan 211 -->

The iron pillar
and the wings of flies!

In the age of Aditya Chola,
the one who ascended the throne
as his son
was Parantaka I.

He had twelve wives —
and their sons were:

Rajaditya, Gandaraditya,
Arikulakesari, Uttamasili
and Arinjaya.

Of these,
Rajaditya died
in the battle of Takkolam;

therefore Gandaraditya,
who came after him,
received the crown.

He married Sembiyan Mahadevi,
daughter of Malavaraiyan,

and had Uttama Chola as his son.
That son also had another name:
Madhurantakan.

When Gandaraditya died,
Madhurantakan was still a child;

so his younger brother Arinjaya
took responsibility for rule.

But he too
met an untimely death.

Arinjaya's son,
Parantaka II,
next came to the throne.

He was granted
the distinction *Rajakesari*.
Though still young,
in war —

<!-- scan 212 -->

he faced Veera Pandya
at a place called Sevur,
won,

and, says the Thiruvalangadu copper plate,
sent the Pandya to the land of death;
that charter sings his praise.

Among the wives of Parantaka II,
two are especially mentioned.

One was Vanavan Mahadevi,
daughter of the Malaiyaman.

She was the one
who mounted the funeral pyre
with her husband.

She was not one
who deceived anyone by saying,
“I thought I would mount it.”

We too say
that such a custom
does not accord with rational thought;

then, now, and always,
we have never supported it.

Before Vanavan Mahadevi
and her life-partner Parantaka II
lost their lives,

Rajaraja —
the Chola prince,
pure gold,
who would become a cause
of the Chola empire's rise —

sat upon the lap
of his mother Vanavan Mahadevi
and played by whirling a sword.

Another wife of Parantaka II
was Cheran Mahadevi;

the town named after her
is “Cheranmadevi”!

The jewel-womb of Vanavan Mahadevi,
mother of Rajaraja,
Aditya Karikalan
and Kundavai Nachiyar,

was delighted to see
the Chola tiger-flag
fly across the world.

Knowing that the strength
of her own children
was the cause,

she became the very grammar
of the Kural:

“Greater than at the moment of birth
is the mother's joy
when she hears her son called a worthy man” —

<!-- scan 213 -->

and that mother
stood amazed at herself!

Vanavan Mahadevi's eldest son
was Aditya Karikalan.

Yet while his father,
Parantaka II,
was still alive,

he became the victim
of a criminal conspiracy —

a consequence of the question:
who had the right to rule the land?

Once Aditya Karikalan was killed,
Rajaraja had the right
to come next to the throne
and be crowned according to order.

So a slander arose against Rajaraja:
that he himself,
eager to rule,
had arranged the conspiracy
and committed the killing.

That accusation vanished at once,
like dew before the sun;

but the country,
the source says,
came to understand what had happened.

In truth,
the deceased son of Parantaka II,
Aditya Karikalan,
had been the one entitled to rule.

But Madhurantakan,
who had received only the title
“Uttama Chola,”

regarded taking life
as though it were a game
and, utterly unlike his own name,

completed a violent conspiracy
one day, it is said.

He became an example
of how, for some,
name and deed do not match —

and then joined
the assembly of the “worthy”!

<!-- scan 214 -->

There are also those
who construct another account:

that the charge upon Uttama Chola
was empty blame,

and that his own close relatives
were responsible
for Aditya Chola's murder.

Whatever caused
Aditya Chola's end,

whoever carried out
the conspiracy may remain a mystery;

but no one who argued against it,
the source says,
denied that conspiracy
had indeed taken his life.

Those who had thought,
“Now Rajaraja will be king,”

those who had immersed themselves
in sweet dreams,
reeled in disappointment.

But Rajaraja calmed everyone.
Though he himself suspected
whether Madhurantakan too
had a role in Aditya Karikalan's death,

he let that very paternal uncle
hold the sceptre.

See, then,
how fitting the name
“Arulmozhi Devan”
was for him!

Thus Aditya's grave itself
became the throne
upon which Madhurantakan
sat wearing the crown.

Yes — conspiracy had won!

The innocent people murmured,
“Alas — fate has won!”

and shed floods of tears.

Yet through all this,
not even a sesame-point of disturbance
stirred Rajaraja's heart.

Will an iron pillar
break merely because
the wings of flies strike it?

Rajaraja took responsibility
with this vow:

“I will remain prince
and watch over this government.”

And every step
his paternal uncle Madhurantaka took —

<!-- scan 215 -->

he watched
without allowing his eyes
to tire even for a moment.

Had he not fully understood
his uncle?

Had that uncle not,
it is said,
planned to make the crown his own
and brought Aditya Chola's life
to an end?

Thus, like the saying
“Murugan seated upon the peacock,”

Rajaraja sat
in the responsibility of prince,
while in his mind
Madhurantaka Chola himself
was that peacock beneath him.

When even great families
see contests
for leadership of a household,

could there be no contest
for leadership of a vast empire?

Rajaraja handled precisely that
through intelligence and tact,

through faith in the maxim
“those who endure shall rule the earth,”

and, above all,
through fear that confusion
inside the palace
would open the way
for outsiders to enter.

Even in youth
he understood the proprieties,
conducted himself accordingly —

and later
he ruled the country!
