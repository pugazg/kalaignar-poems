# He Is Young; He Is a Son of Tamil!

<!-- scan 173 -->

He is young;
he is a son of Tamil!

That was an age
when even a tender shoot,
still too young for school,

would rise with a spear
that leapt forward,
with a sword
that gathered up fury,

crying,
“I will pluck out
and cast away the foe!”

It was an age
granted to the Tamils
like gold —

an age when many poets
nurtured the Sangam!

Warriors went
to the battlefield
like surging tigers;

auspicious women
stood blessing them.

Even when the wind blows,
Tamil valour comes
and strikes within it!

The sea-wave rises high —
in it the Tamil's stature
rises and shines!

The smoke of *akil* wood
spreads fragrance —
in that too
Tamil's fame is fragrant!

A lovely moon appears
among the clouds —
in it appears
the treasure of a Muttamil epic!

Even King Ashoka,
who ruled the great land
praised by the world,

spoke of those
outside his rule

<!-- scan 174 -->

as the mighty people
of the southern country.

Among those broad-shouldered
peoples of the South
was one division,
the valiant Kosars!

They had the power
to bring forth courage,
and were famed
for truthfulness joined with purity.

Leaving Tulu country,
they stopped their wandering
as far as the eastern sea
and, history says,
settled in the regions
called “Niyamam” and “Sellur.”

“● South of Sellur —
where the great sea roars —
at Niyamam,
the fierce-eyed Kosars,
their faces scarred
by iron weapons...”

★ ★ ★

This is the message
given by *Akananuru*.

It introduces
two lion-like Tamil warriors,
one called “Anni”
and one called “Minjili.”

Joining those two names
comes a young Tamil woman —

though she was a maiden
at the age when her eyes themselves
became weapons of war
that felled young bulls,

she was a virtuous,
sacred young woman!

Many longed
to embrace her.

Some Kosars
asked her father
for her hand;
he refused.

The beautiful maiden
called Anni Minjili
was a woman of chastity
that breathed fire,
like the warrior Anni
who died in battle!

She was firm
like the warrior Minjili
who defeated and killed
Aay Eyinan
and made Nannan's town
his own!

<!-- scan 175 -->

“Like a picture of the moon —
like long-eyed *kendai* fish
moving through water —

a parrot-girl who moves with grace,
with eyes fit for good union —”

thinking so,
some Kosars came sweetly seeking her.

Her father asked,
“What do you think my daughter is?

She is sugarcane, man!
I will not agree
to place her
in the hand of a savage monkey!

Do you know
where all her thoughts rest?

Upon the man of Azhundur!
Tithiyan,
the guardian of that town,
is her guardian too!

Do not come again.
Understand what I have told you
and act accordingly — now!”

History says
that even among Kosars
renowned for truthfulness,
there lived some
with hard hearts.

They devised a plan
to seize that maiden.

Many fertile fields
where bean crops grew
were the property
of the Kosars settled at Niyamam.

One cow alone
was the cherished family property
of Anni Minjili.

With a dreadful plan,
some Kosars drove that cow
into their bean field
and let it graze.

If a hungry cow
is given a crop-field for pasture,
will it run away in fear
without rushing in to eat?

Like the belly
of a cruel ruling class
that swells by gathering
and devouring together
all the labour
of the working class,

that cow's stomach swelled
until there seemed
no room left within it!

<!-- scan 176 -->

“If the old man
who said, ‘No maiden for you,’
is made into a man
with no eyes,

then the peahen
will have no protector!
All of us can share
that peerless beauty.”

So plotted those men
of degraded nature,
working their trickery.

They put Anni Minjili's father
in the accused's cage
and told the story
of the cow grazing
in the bean field that day.

The Kosars of the village council asked:
“Tell us — what is the truth?”

The old man's face
darkened like a rain-filled sky.

“I will not ask,
‘Who saw my cow graze?’

Mine is a lineage
that will not tell a lie
no matter who opposes us.

It is true:
my cow grazed
the bean crop!” he said.

He admitted the offence.

Therefore the council
of the Kosars pronounced judgment:
“Gouge out his eyes!”

The next moment
they dragged away
Anni Minjili's father —

that ripe old man, poor man —
and tore out both his eyes.

“Have you lost your sight,
Father?

I will not leave
these sinners unavenged!”
cried the daughter,
raising the sound
of a war-drum.

“They destroyed my eyes
so that they could steal you!

Who has the courage
to approach my daughter,
who carries chastity
as a sword?”

Before her father
could finish speaking,
the woman standing like a leader
answered.

<!-- scan 177 -->

“Until the lives
of those who blinded
my father, dear as life,
are gone —

I will not eat
from an eating vessel;
I will not change my clothes.

Until vengeance is complete,
there will be no hunger,
no sleep for me!

I will finish the feud!
I will give the enemies
as sacrifice
for my father
who has lost his sight!”

Anni Minjili's vow
reached Tithiyan of Azhundur
the very next day.

Hearing of the sorrow
that had struck the maiden,
he grieved
and longed to see her.

As soon as the news
entered his ears,
he saw “Anni”
standing before him
like a tongue of red flame.

“Delight of my eyes!
One who fits my very thought!

I know neither the method
nor the path
by which to place medicine
upon the wound in your heart,”
he grieved.

“You have cavalry
in your fort, king!

You have shoulders
like a mountain,
my dear!

If it is true
that you love me,
Anni Minjili,

set out without delay
for war against the Kosars!

They have made my father
one who lives in darkness —
those ignorant villains
must be brought down.

I have come seeking
broad-shouldered Tithiyan,
the hero who will hack apart
those eye-gouging snakes!”
she said.

“See —
my sword has risen
from its sheath!

Know, beloved,
that my vow will not die!

<!-- scan 178 -->

Only after I return
having taken the lives
of those who injured
your father — my father-in-law —

will these hands
touch you!”
roared the man of Azhundur.

The war-host set out!

The bodies
of the wicked Kosars
were shattered —

they fell dead
like a long wall
that cannot stand.

Her vow was fulfilled
as she desired.

She leaned upon the shoulder
of the returning Tithiyan!

An *Akananuru* poem
is the basis for this.

★ ★ ★

Note: (*Akananuru* poem 262, sung by Paranar. The characters who appear in this historical incident are not imaginary. There may be chronological contradictions and differences of place. When the literary evidence is compared, this is, in fact, a moving portion of Chola-period history that really occurred.)
