# The Undying Art of Sculpture and the Beautiful Art of Painting!

<!-- scan 236 -->

The undying art of sculpture,
and the beautiful art of painting!

After Rajaraja's father,
Parantaka II,
died naturally;

after his elder brother Aditya too
fell through conspiracy;

Madhurantakan — rightly or wrongly,
a man spoken of as connected with it —
wore the crown
and ruled until his death.

Only after those three
did Rajaraja become king
and receive the crown.

So whatever else we may say,
in CE 985
his age must have been
somewhere between forty and fifty!

Only after he too
had ruled for thirty years
did Rajendra Chola
assume royal responsibility;

scholar R. Rajamanickanar,
after research,
writes that Rajendra's age then
was fifty.

Even for him,
all that is known
is that the year he came to rule
was CE 1012.

He was born
on the Tiruvathirai star-day
in the month of Margazhi —

but in that family
of kings among kings,
there is no other detail!

For the father,
Sadhayam;
for the son,
Tiruvathirai —

only those are discovered
as the days on which
they “descended” into this world!

<!-- scan 237 -->

Only after son Rajendra
ascended the throne
did father Rajaraja
die naturally;

but even that day,
when we speak of history,
was forgotten.

The heart cannot help
but become wounded!

        Fortunately,
our ancestors forgot the dates,
but did not forget the news!

Is merely naming
a month and a star enough?

Every month,
days and stars
come and go.

Never mind —
        our old palaces
are historical splendours
that forgot birthdays and death-days,

places that became hidden confinements,
burying them out of sight!

Very well, let it go.
History at least
has reached us as a torn fragment;

let us take comfort
that even that fragment
did not fall into destitution,
and continue!

★ ★ ★

In Rajaraja's time,
Chola country,
with Thanjavur as capital,

extended north to the Tungabhadra,
northeast to Vengi country,

south to the central part of Eelam,
and through what are today
South Arcot, Tiruchi, Thanjavur and Pudukkottai.

It formed the ancient Chola mandalam;
and to avoid administrative difficulties,
many “valanadus” were created.

The subdivisions of a valanadu
too were called “nadus” —

the list runs on:
“Pappa Nadu,”
“Uratha Nadu” —

<!-- scan 238 -->

A group of ministers
assisted the king.

Government officers belonged
to divisions called
“Peruntaram” and “Sirutaram,”
with officers of the next rank beneath them.

They did not receive
a separate cash salary;

lands were granted to them,
and the yield from those lands
served as their pay.

To honour the honesty,
integrity and rare service
of certain officers,
lands were sometimes given
tax-free.

Each region called a “nadu”
was under the supervision
of a guard-officer.

An order was issued
to measure and account
for all the lands in the country,

classify them as forest,
upland and cultivated field,
and regulate land tax;

it was put into practice immediately.

There is a note,
the source says,
that an inscription found at Karur
names an officer as
“Ulagalavitta Tiruvadigal Sattan”!

Lakes that appeared like seas
too were gifts of grace
from the Chola kings!

Assemblies were formed
in every village;

four kinds of local councils
were constituted here and there
through elections.

Because high-caste domination
entered among them,
those local bodies were called:

“Chaturvedi Mangala Sabha,”
“Devadana Councils,”
“Brahmadeya Sabha,”

and “village councils
to which non-Brahmins were elected.”

I am not building a story;
I say that the researcher
shows inscriptional evidence!

<!-- scan 239 -->

The Uthiramerur inscription too
claims a share in this
root-level local-government system

for Parantaka Chola,
who had ruled sixty years
before Rajaraja.

Committees were formed as well
to conduct public affairs.

If there was even a drop of stain
in conduct, character,
integrity or honesty,

a person's name could not even
be written upon the *kudavolai*;

he would immediately
lose eligibility.

Only the fit
would be chosen;

the unfit
would be cast aside.

There were committees
for the lake,
for cultivated fields,
for accounts,
for the sluice —

and only those without blemish
would bear responsibility
in the committees
through which people performed civic duties.

The source also presents Rajaraja
as the first to group local bodies
into unions
and conduct orderly administration.

Rajaraja lived
with the principle
that every religion was acceptable.

Though he had a special devotion
to Saivism,
he embraced people of every faith
and took delight
in supporting good people
whatever their religion.

His elder sister Kundavai Nachiyar herself,
at a place called Dadapuram,

for Saivites,
Vaishnavites,
and Jains —
three communities —

<!-- scan 240 -->

personally led the building
of three temples
so that each might find fulfilment.

★ ★ ★

“I shall follow my father's path
without straying
and win a place
in the minds of the Tamil people” —

with that vow,
Rajendra Chola
bowed at the feet of his forebears
and took up the sword.

He became the living form
of Ayyan Tiruvalluvar's two Kurals:

“The good a father does his son
is to make him stand foremost
in the learned assembly,”

and:

“The good a son does his father
is to make people say,
‘What penance did this man's father perform
to gain such a son?’”

Seeing his son Rajendra rule in that way,
the great warrior Rajaraja
lost his heart in delight.

The mountain-sized support
the two gave
to art and literature
stood on one side.

Though it also formed
a fortress for theism,

the work they did
to make the arts of dance and song
into undying sculpture
and beautiful painting

was work praised by the world.

Those works are
Thanjavur Rajarajeswaram
and, its equal,
Gangaikonda Cholapuram!
