# What Prize Is Fitting for the Beauty of a Simile!

<!-- scan 289 -->

What prize is fitting
for the beauty of a simile!

When a king sets out
with armies for war,

besieging the enemy's
capital fortress
is called *uzhinjai*.

Holding fast to one's own fort
and resisting the siege
is called *nochchi*.

When two armies
face one another
and wage battle,
that act is called *thumbai*.

Whoever wins,
the victory is called *vaagai*!

These, we may say,
are the names
the poets gave

to each stage
of the warfare
of the Three Crowned Kings.

There was a king
named Pandyan Ukkira Peruvazhuthi.

Let us see,
in this part
of Pandyan history,

the event in which
he went with a sky-rising army

to wage siege-war
against the fortress
of “Kaanapper Koyil,”

and how that affair
was brought to its full stop.

Was it the enemy's wonder?
Or the poet's wonder?

Read —
and we shall know!

★ ★ ★

Chinna Marudu and Periya Marudu
ruled the Sivaganga country —

two lion-hearted great warriors
who bloomed as red-blood flowers
in the history of red soil.

At Kalaiyar Koyil,
where statues stand for both,

in ancient Tamil Nadu
there was another name:

<!-- scan 290 -->

“Kaanapper.”

Even now,
it is a land of *Maravars*
that launches wars of honour!

There,
a minor Vel chieftain
named Vengai Marban

built a fortress
of towering fame

and issued a challenge:
who could conquer it?

One might pierce an atom
and enter inside it —

but to enter
that fortress
was impossible,

and warrior after warrior
stood bewildered!

At that time,
the king ruling Pandya country
was Ukkira Peruvazhuthi!

A lion
upon the battlefield!

Strong enough
to strike elephants
and toss them about!

For enemies
to escape his sword
and remain alive
was rare — rare indeed!

With overflowing strength
and a learned artistic heart,

he gained fame,
the source says,
by compiling
the poems of *Akananuru*.

The presentation-festival
of the good verses
of the undying lamp,
*Tirukkural*,

is also said
to have taken place
in his presence!

One day,
Avvai saw him seated
with Cheraman Marivenko,

strung and placed upon them
a garland of refined Tamil verse,

and blessed there
the unity
of the Three Crowned Kings.

The fish-bannered king
Ukkira Peruvazhuthi

was lover
to a fish-eyed maiden.

One day,
while reading
a bedchamber epic
with that beloved,

she hung a question mark
from the corner of her eye
and said:

“My lord of my eyes!
Clear one doubt for me.”

<!-- scan 291 -->

“What is it, my dear?
What doubt do you have?

Are you asking me
how you came to possess
such softness,
like the feather
of that swimming swan?

Or do you wish to know
how honey came
to your lips,

though bees never
brought and poured it there,

yet I scoop it up
and drink it?

Was your face made
by looking at a red lotus?

Or, my radiant one,
was the red lotus itself
created only after looking
at your face —

is that your doubt?

Unable to bear
the two round globes,

will your waist,
writhing like the worm
upon a hook
cast for a kendai fish
in a pond,

continue to stand?

Or will your shining
ivory thighs
strike it
and hold it up?

These are all
the doubts growing
in your mind,
are they not?”

So asked Vazhuthi.

Before him,
she bowed her head
with modesty,
like a curved ray,

and said:

“I did not raise a question
so that you might write poetry
upon me and win a prize!

Tell me:
in delightful Tamil,
what does *eyil* mean?”

“Ah, foolish peahen!
Do you not know even this?

To stop an enemy army,
we build a fort
around the capital —

that is called *eyil*,”
said the king!

“I know that too.
But there is one thing!

There is said to be
an *eyil*
that no one can break;

and a king,
bubbling with pride,

is said to boast
that even ‘this man’
cannot come near it.
Do you know of that?”

<!-- scan 292 -->

When she said
“even this man,”

she pointed at Vazhuthi
and held him tight!

“That news
too has reached my ears.

The fort is said
to have been built
at a city called Kaanapper.

Our court poet
Aiyur Moolankizhar
has made a rare description
of it!

I shall tell it to you.
Listen:

in the order of years,
it deserves first place!

A long moat
deeper than the depths
of the earth;

high walls
rising to touch
the blue sky;

upon them,
missile-platforms
working swiftly
like the stars
crowding that sky;

and a guard-forest
so dense
that even sunlight
finds no place to enter;

surrounded as well
by countless little
supporting forts —

such is the Kaanapper *eyil*,
which will not move
even if a thousand elephants
butt against it!

Many times
I have rejoiced
hearing our poet
describe it
in poetic Tamil,”
said the king!

If, after laying siege,
one goes down
into the moat
surrounding the fort,

defying the resistance,

stones from slings
and rain-showers of arrows
fall from the walls above.

If one gets past those,

boiling oil
and molten lead
come rattling downward!

<!-- scan 293 -->

If one overcomes all that
and begins climbing
toward the top
of the fort,

the ladders are smashed.

Even after that,
metal-bird machines,
with beaks,

stab at the crown
of one's head.

Escaping all of those,
one must still descend
inside the fortress
on the other side!

“The Pandyas can see
enemies far away;

you bring foes
like tigers inside cages.

Yet nearby,
in the city called Kaanapper,

Vengai Marban
has built a fortress,
raised his sword,

and cries so loudly
the town shakes,

saying no one,
with any army,
can come near him!

After he has indirectly
mocked our courage,

would it not be shameful
for us to remain saying,
‘What is that to us?’

The doubt
that had lain for many days
at the bottom
of my heart's depth

I have brought
to its full stop today!

For a king
who can confront
even a rutting elephant
and split its forehead,

what is the wall
Vengai Marban has built —

the Vindhya mountain range?

Even if the moat
he has dug
is a deep ocean,

for my Pandyan
it is merely
a distance to cross!

Then why,
seeing the arrogance
of Vengai Marban
who issues the challenge,

should he lie quiet?”

<!-- scan 294 -->

“Let the king
clear this doubt.
After that,
we may issue the decree,”

she said,
sulking!

The Pandyan
caught the woman
who tried to move away,

held her tight,
interlaced their legs,

and said:

“Let morning blossom!

I shall order
our infantry,
elephants,
horses and chariot-force

to set out
toward the Kaanapper *eyil*.

Leave your worry!

Now,
take away this hand
that is trembling.”

She took it away!

Within the blink
of an eye,
the sulking was forgotten —

and union continued
until the rays
of morning spread!

The next day,
just as he had promised,

the ranks of the army
set out
so that Pandimadevi's wish
might be fulfilled!

They struck
the heavily guarded fortress
called Kaanapper *eyil*,

waged a fierce war,

and brought the siege
to its end.

Under the determined attack
of Ukkira Peruvazhuthi,

the defences
of Vengai Marban's fort
were shattered!

The Pandyan king
returned to tell
the victory-news

to the queen
who had placed
the victory mark
upon his forehead
and sent him forth.

Upon the wounded chest
of the king,

she gave pressing comfort
again and again
with the mound
of her own body!

Every honour-wound
from which blood still seeped

received her kisses
and healed completely!

★ ★ ★

Some days later,
Aiyur Moolankizhar
came to the Pandyan court.

<!-- scan 295 -->

“King!
What greatness —
that enemies who did not respect you
now respect and praise you!

O king
who knows nothing
but victory
when you enter the field!

From today,
let the epics sing you
as:

‘Ukkira Peruvazhuthi
who crossed the Kaanapper *eyil*!’

Let the poetry-stage
praise you!

As long as the sun exists,
let that name endure!”

So spoke the poet.

“Thank you, poet!”
said the king with humility,

and asked him:

“Vengai Marban,
who has been diminished
in this war —

will he,
spitting sparks of anger,

make another battlefield
to recover the Kaanapper *eyil*?”

The answer
Aiyur Moolankizhar gave

presented Mother Tamil
with a rare simile!

What was it?

If one drop of water
falls upon iron
heated by a smith
in the furnace-fire,

can that water
ever be recovered?

(In the same way),

the Kaanapper *eyil*
that Ukkira Peruvazhuthi
has conquered

has become
like a drop of water
absorbed by forge-heated iron!

“Vengai Marban
stands grieving in his heart,
speaking of your greatness,”
said the poet.

What prize
could the Pandyan give
worthy of the beauty
of that simile
bestowed by the golden poet?

I say
he must have struggled
not knowing!

Is there an error
in my thought?

You who know
the taste of Tamil —
tell me!
