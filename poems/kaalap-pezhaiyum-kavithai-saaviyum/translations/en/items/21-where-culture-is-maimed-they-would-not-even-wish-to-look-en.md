# Where Culture Is Maimed, They Would Not Even Wish to Look!

<!-- scan 107 -->

While still a sturdy young bull,
he escaped in anguish
with his ankle scorched
in the fire lit by certain wicked enemies;

he became famed for the Kallanai,
and built embankments for the Kaveri;

with Uraiyur and Poompuhar
as two incomparable capitals —

that Chola king was Karikalan.
His daughter,
the beautiful princess Aathimandhiyar,

was herself a queen among poets,
and a great queen of dance
upon the stage.

Nalankilli, Nedunkilli and Mavalathan —
three brothers she had;

though Aathimandhi laboured hard
so that no cloud of civil strife
should form among them after Karikalan,
it was of no use.

Though they took the Tamil way
as their path,
they acted without unity,
walking the way Tamils so often walked.

To make her own path
a path of love,
Aathimandhi, queen of Tamil art,
fell in love with the broad-shouldered Chera,
Aattanathi,
master of the flourishing art of dance.

Because that truth remained locked
inside the palace,
many princes from near and far
came to see her as a prospective bride;

<!-- scan 108 -->

she did not wish even to look
at any one of them.
Beginning with her confidante,
and when Karikalan —
whose fame surpassed the sweep of the sky —
asked whom else she desired,

she recited, as though from memory,
a poem beginning
“Mallaar kuzhiiya,”
whose sense was:

“In festivals where warriors gather and dance,
and in *thunankai* dances
where men embrace courtesans,

I have not seen him.
I too am a woman skilled in the art of dance;
likewise,

the man who captured my heart
so that these bangles loosen upon my arm
is himself a master of that art.”

Hearing this,
Karikalan sent word of the marriage
of Aathimandhi and Aattanathi
to all the kings
and conducted the wedding.

But one or two villains,
who had waited many days,
were stunned as though a cotton pod had burst,
and joined together to devise a plan
to cut their life of love short halfway.

When someone in the house next door,
or the house opposite,
is eating a sweet jackfruit segment,
how can hearts bitter as *etti* fruit
simply stand and watch?

And so,
those whose nature was like a honey-sting
fixed a day
to cut their love apart with a sword;

<!-- scan 109 -->

they chose the day
and did the work of jackals.

After finishing dance practice,
the two lovers danced and rejoiced
until the festival of the Kaveri's fresh flood ended;

later they read together
the midnight bedchamber epic,
and, kissing that thornless rose,

embraced and fell asleep in exhaustion.
Then the plotters bound Aattanathi with rope,
lifted him away;

though Aathimandhi woke and chased them,
they kicked her aside,
and into the Kaveri,
roaring as it ran with an “O!”

they flung him —
like a ball swinging at the end of a rope.

Those nameless, worthless husks
escaped,
leaving the daughter
of the earth-ruling king in torment.

She of the cool, flawless moon-face
ran along the wave-beaten riverbank,

burning for him,
crying “Aththaan! Aththaan!”
until her heart seemed to burst.

He, caught within the rope
in the river's whirl,
thrashed like a splinter
caught in a cyclone.

When the news reached King Karikalan,
he stood bewildered,
not knowing how to console
his precious Aathimandhi.

But she,
calling and calling for her man,
ran along the riverbank —
ran and ran.

She danced a dance of frenzy,
danced and danced;
like a lily-stalk falling into flame,
she withered and withered —

<!-- scan 110 -->

and in that midnight hour,
an old woman who rowed a boat upon the river

hurried in distress
to the maiden Aathimandhi,
who had collapsed upon the bank,
and turned her face to see.

Lightning struck — “paleer! paleer!”
Thunder whirled and cracked — “suleer!” —
like a lash of the whip.

To Aathimandhi,
who opened her eyes only halfway,
the old woman said:

“Are you not, child,
the one who sang to my granddaughter,
your friend Nilavazhagi,
when so many came to see you as a bride:

‘Even in the festival where wrestlers gather,
even in the *thunankai* where women embrace,
nowhere have I seen the man worthy of me;
I too am a woman of the dancing-floor,
and the lord of high worth
who loosened the bright paired bangles on my arm
is himself a man of the dancing-floor.’?”

Hearing the old woman recite
her own poem,
her own pure Tamil song,
without rejecting a word,
Aathimandhi marvelled;

she sobbed,
and the tears poured from her eyes —
one might say they conquered the Kaveri itself.

Filled with compassion,
the old boatwoman said affectionately
that her own name was “Kaveri”;

a smile blossomed
in her toothless, hollow mouth,
and she told them
that she had safely sheltered Aattanathi
in her hut —

<!-- scan 111 -->

news that filled everyone nearby with joy.

She handed Aattanathi,
son-in-law of world-ruling Karikalan,
back to Aathimandhi,
who ruled through Tamil song.

The two danced in joy,
and sang in reverence
to the old boatwoman.

Aattanathi–Aathimandhi
is the very epic that declares
that art and love
were already a settled culture
among the Tamils of that age!

**Note:** Aathimandhiyar and Aattanathi
were not merely people of good conduct;
our culture is such that
where even that conduct would be maimed,
they would not wish even to look.
