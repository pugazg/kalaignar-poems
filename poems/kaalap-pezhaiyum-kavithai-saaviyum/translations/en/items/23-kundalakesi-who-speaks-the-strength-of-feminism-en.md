# Kundalakesi, Who Speaks the Strength of Feminism!

<!-- scan 117 -->

Kovalan, great lord among merchants;
his beloved companion,
Madhavi, the truthful mistress of dance;

Kannagi, queen among women,
who came to him
as the sacred lamp of life;

and, born in their line,
the woman of the noble clan of language,
of inexhaustible fame —
Manimekalai,

an unattached ascetic
who gave no room in human society
for anyone's reproach —

with these come the sounding pair,
*Silambu* and *Mekalai*;
with them shines as a twin
*Seevaka Chintamani*;

there is the rare Tamil literary work
*Valayapathi*;
and *Kundalakesi*,
about the woman
who brought patriarchy down.

Jainism, Buddhism,
Saivism and Vaishnavism —
when great conflicts arose among them,

though the poets nurtured by the Sangam,
and learned masters
joined to every art,
needed time
to come to honour equality,

before that time arrived,
alas,
Tamil land had lost
two of the Five Great Epics.
What a wonder!

How did *Valayapathi* bend away from us?
How was the form of *Kundalakesi* broken?

Did the termite called time devour them?
Or did the carelessness of the Tamils
stand as the cause?

For more than sixty years
this humble servant searched,
wandering without tiring —

<!-- scan 118 -->

and afterwards,
a torn leaf came into my hands.
In it was a favourite son
of the palace preceptor,

one who had made murder,
robbery, rape and abduction
into the arts by which
he spent his life.

When he was captured,
his hands and feet shackled,
and dragged through the street
to be kept under guard,

she saw him —
the maiden bearing two hills
upon her breast,

the virtuous woman called Kundalakesi,
whose other name was Pathirai.

Her father,
the great minister,
would call her “Amudham” — nectar —
pouring out his love.

That nectar came to desire poison:
she fell and lost herself
in the beauty of the beast
who passed before her in chains.

She bowed at the feet
of her father the minister,
won his favour,
and had the king's punishment
set aside.

Pathirai,
whose heart was stolen
by the glitter of the serpent,
became the maiden of his bedchamber;

together the two
drained deep
the drink of pleasure's nectar.

One day,
in a lovers' quarrel in the bedchamber,
while they held each other close,

“You are a thief, aren't you?” she said —
not as a harsh word,
but as a word of love.

Black anger rose in him,
though he did not show it;
he embraced her
until her full breasts pressed deep,

and said,
“Beloved, come;
shall we go see
the nearby mountain peak?”

When he called,
she clung to him and went;

the two winged birds
reached the summit —

<!-- scan 119 -->

“Shame! Shame!
You mocked me as a thief;
for that I am going
to push you from this peak —
you will be shattered!”
he said.

Within a few moments
that woman,
whose heart was a garland
of cool flowers,
understood the truth.

Deceit and intrigue,
she resolved,
must be defeated
not with a sword,
but by deceit itself.

“Life of my life!
Rare medicine of my being!
Before you take away my life,
let me have a little time
to worship my husband
and rise,” she asked.

The robber —
the man without virtue —
gave it to her.

With great skill
she acted out the sham drama
of a woman bound by devotion,
full of worship toward her husband.

When half her circling was done,
she planned
to take the sinner's life;

while he waited
for the other half of the circuit,
she pushed him
from the mountain rock's summit.

That great sinner died.

Though the literature of *Kundalakesi*,
which declared the strength of feminism
against arrogant patriarchy,
no longer exists,

this Kundalakesi
remains as its grammar —
its living rule.

She went on
to spread Buddhist doctrine,
winning many debates,

and later
brought her life
to fulfilment.
