# The Mother Full of Dignity and the Stainless Son!

<!-- scan 148 -->

The mother full of dignity
and the stainless son!

All the beauty of nature —
as darkness and light;

as sky and cloud;
as sun and moon;

as sea and land —
thus appearing

in unlike pairs,
two by two,

and then seeming
to mingle without distinction —

not taking on some giant form,
but taking graceful form and smiling;

with beauty that sways like waves,
with hair flowing long like a waterfall,

with two buds
upon the front of her body,
with skin like silk,

with coral-red lips,
and teeth like a string of pearls —

whatever further beauties
might add beauty to her beauty,

all those beauties
arrayed together and fashioned
into a woman's form —

<!-- scan 149 -->

when we call that green parrot Madhavi,
and speak of the girl born to her

with the many-skilled Kovalan,
who shared her as half his life,

what words could there be
to celebrate the beauty
of their daughter Manimekalai,

even if ranks upon ranks of poets
were summoned to praise her?

Not only they —
even the Tamil they had learned
would stand astonished
before such immense beauty!

★ ★ ★

In that splendid household
where joy poured like a waterfall,

the lovers' quarrel
that arose between Madhavi and Kovalan
at the Indra festival

left no place thereafter
for them to come together again
in life as wife and husband;

Madhavi became
like a world without night,
her heart broken,
and turned renunciant.

Even after deciding
that household life was no more for her,

as though a thorn
had pierced her heart,

a young bull,
intoxicated with love like liquor,
crossed into the life
of Madhavi's daughter Manimekalai;

Madhavi withered,
not knowing what to do.

Declaring that his very heart
loved Manimekalai,

Udayakumaran,
the Chola prince,
wrote a letter to Mekalai:

“O coloured statue worthy of worship!
O nectar of heaven!
I shall come this evening
to meet you;

O jackfruit ripened at the root,
I come to place my request
before you face to face.”

<!-- scan 150 -->

He sent that letter to Mekalai —
and Mekalai's body trembled!

Before he could arrive,
she longed to alter
her bodily beauty completely,
and did so;

made up in an illusory guise
like her Kali-like companion
named Kaya Chandikai,

she came and stood before Udayan
inside a darkened hall —
in the corridor where food was served.

Though the prince
had learned the many arts,
in his haste he failed to discern

the marks that distinguished
Kaya Chandikai from Mekalai.

He came near Manimekalai,
who was there giving food
to people without support;

he called her “Beloved,”
he wept and spoke to her;
he asked,
“Why have you become so dishevelled?”

Just then
the true husband of Kaya Chandikai
arrived there.

In an unexpected calamity,
her husband Vidyadharan,
thinking that his own wife
had entered into an improper relationship
with the prince,

violently pulled Manimekalai aside;
then, drawing the sword
already fastened at his waist,

he cut off the prince's head
before anyone could stop him,
and escaped.

“I have ended the story
of that rogue Udayakumaran
who played with the chastity
of my wife Kaya Chandikai!”
he roared.

Poor Manimekalai fainted!

<!-- scan 151 -->

When Sembiyan Nedumudikilli
heard the news,

he did not let his heart convulse
because his only son's life
had ended in this way.

“The punishment
I myself ought to have given —
as though the country itself had given it,
someone has given it,”
he said, consoling himself.

Nor did it stop there.
In the confusion of mind
that made him think
Manimekalai herself
was the cause of all this,

he imprisoned her too;
his wife's words,
it is said,
were one cause of that as well.

Then Aravana Adigal —
a man of discipline
whom wise elders and learned people
revered and worshipped —

advised the king.
Listening to him,
the king not only released Manimekalai,

but also enabled her once more
to continue the humanitarian work
of relieving the people's hunger.

Mekalai,
who had become a mother of compassion,
was welcomed by Ilangkilli,
king of Kanchi;

he even built for her
the Buddhist pedestal
that had lived as her dream.

Is not the history
of Madhavi and Mekalai —
that mother and daughter
full of dignity —

a stainless and glorious history
that proclaims the greatness
of the honour-filled Tamil land?
