# A Noble Friendship Higher Than Life Itself!

<!-- scan 167 -->

A noble friendship
higher than life itself!

Pisiranthaiyar —
that concentrated blaze
of classical Tamil,
who stood like a lamp of wisdom
to the Pandyan Arivudai Nambi —

held for Kopperuncholan,
king of the Chola country,
an affection no words
could ever weave.

One day,
in his birthplace,
the village called Pisir,
in a grove dense with shade,

the poet of principle
longed to fulfil a desire:

to compose a poem
about his life-friend,
the Chola king.

Making a swan-cock
the centre of his imagination,
the poet shaped a lovely poem
in sugar-sweet Tamil!

“O swan-cock!
O swan-cock!”
he called,
opening the living heart
within him,

while on one side
he held down the storm in his mind
that he still had not gained
a chance to see
Kopperuncholan,
the king Perungokilli.

He spoke from the fullness
of his breast:

“O swan-cock!
O swan-cock!

from the tip of maiden Kanyakumari,
with your beak
that has pecked the *ayirai* fish,

you fly very high
through the sky,
travelling toward
the snow-mountain-filled north;

may your journey triumph!

<!-- scan 168 -->

On the way you pass,
my darling bird,
there lies the capital
of the Chola who subdues his foes!

If you and your mate
go that way,
you will see in the Chola's eyes
the light of affection!

If you tell him,
‘We belong to Pisir,
the town where the poet Anthaiyar lives,’

the king himself
will run to welcome and honour you,
and delight your mate
with many ornaments!”

★ ★ ★

Whether the swan-cock,
after hearing Anthaiyar's words,
ever went to the place
where the Chola king lived or not,

the poet, content
that he had carved
the greatness of his life-friend,
recited the poem
to his beloved wife
and rejoiced.

“What a beautiful imagination,
that swan-cock!” she said,
serving rice to him
and to their grandchildren.

“Look here —
two rows of *mullai* flowers
are smiling!” he said.

Opening her bow-like brows,
his wife asked,
“Where?”

“The maiden's milk-white smile —
and here,
the smile of this white rice —
those are the *mullai* flowers
I meant!” he said.

“Mind what you say;
the children are beside us!”
she warned.

“Our son's diamond statues
are not yet old enough
to understand such words!

<!-- scan 169 -->

Our grown son
has gone to the fields
with his wife;
it will take time
for them to return.
Do not fear!” he said.

“They will come now;
do not start cuddling me,”
she warned.

At once the poet
closed his mouth —
with the rice she had served!

It caught in his throat;
he choked and struggled.

The woman sprang up,
tapped her husband
on the head,

and rubbed his chest
with her hand;
he embraced her —
she slipped away!

Like anklet-bells
ringing with concern,
the old lovers
burst into delighted laughter.

Sweet as the base
of a sugarcane,
the grandchildren babbled,
clapped their tiny hands
and laughed too.

Then, as though a sudden barrier
had been thrown across
joy pouring like a breached tank,

a piece of grave Tamil news
fell upon their ears.

The sons whom Uraiyur king
Kopperuncholan cherished as life itself,
it said,
had raised an army against him;

and the Chola king,
who composed poetry
in parrot-sweet Tamil,
was plunged into great sorrow
by the deed of his children;

and the man
whose heart was a white flower,
seeing no other way,
had taken the warrior's sword
in his hand
and risen to enter the field.

Hearing this strange news
of battle breaking out
between father and sons,
Pisiranthaiyar said

<!-- scan 170 -->

that the happiness in his mind
had become like a possession lost,
and with deep anguish
set out for the Chola country.

★ ★ ★

He heard that the learned men
of the town had gathered
in a public village council
established by Kopperuncholan.

His heart beating
with an agitation beyond words,
he went there
to learn whether
the smoke of war
had been put out.

The great poets
Poththiyar, Boothanaganar,
Naththathanar
and Pullattrur Eyittriyanar

leapt from the council,
ran to him,
embraced Pisiranthaiyar
against their chests
and rejoiced:

“Come! Come!”

“My limbs tremble,
my heart trembles —
that is how I have come, sir!

What happened
to the war that arose
between the lion
and its cubs?”

The poet stood there
like a question mark.

“Nothing ended in disaster;
listen!

Something else arose,
and this great man
leapt like lightning
and stopped it.

Asking,
‘Must the hen itself suffer
because the chicks trample?’

he cooled even Kopperuncholan
with tender Tamil verse
and became the reason
for the war being stopped.

This is Pullattrur Eyittriyanar!”
said Poththiyar,
introducing him.

Pisiranthaiyar leapt
with overflowing joy.

Then he returned home,
his happiness blossoming —

but a message waited there
that would turn his heart
into a storm.

★ ★ ★

<!-- scan 171 -->

The Chola king,
who had ruled the land
like the sun rising in the east,

unable to mend the heart
broken by the opposition
of the children born to him,

had gone to practise *vadakkiruthal*
and give up his life —

so the poet read
in a palm-leaf message
sharp as a blade.

“This very moment
I will leave,
go home,
take my farewell
and return.

We who have lived
all this time for friendship
will not be separated
in the world of death!

Tell the Chola
who has gone to sit facing north:
Pisiranthai will soon come
to sit beside him!”

Saying this,
the lion among poets
set out,

tears dropping
from his two eyes
like dew falling
from a leaf.

★ ★ ★

The pure-Tamil Chola
who had ruled through his appointed days,

his heart wrapped in fog
as he thought of the war
raised by his sons,

wished to die
through *vadakkiruthal*,
choosing that as the path
by which all anguish of mind
might disappear.

Poets living across Tamilakam
heard of it;

some, saying their own lives
were no nectar to be hoarded,
went and sat with him.

As the king's life,
resolved to depart,
slowly melted away
and his whole body weakened,

some of them looked
at the great Kopperuncholan
and said:

“O king of kings!

You say the master
of green Tamil verse,
Pisiranthaiyar,

holds a friendship with you
that never bends within,
even though you have never seen each other.

Your span of life
is about to end;
why has he not come?”

<!-- scan 172 -->

“He vowed that he would return
and give up his life beside you —
what became of those words?”

When mockery sounded in this way,
the king grew angry
at those who raised doubts
about the integrity
of his friend's friendship
even at the place of death.

“The friendship of Anthaiyar,
that gold who arose
in the little Tamil village Pisir
of the Pandya country,

is a pure friendship
that may not come forward
to show itself while life goes well,

but when one falls low,
it will run without hesitation
and stand beside him!

Learned men,
know this!”
answered the king.

The very next day
he gave up his life
and became a *nadukal*.

Pisiranthaiyar did come late;
there, beside his friend,
he too sat facing north
and gave up his life.

The poet Kannaganar,
turning tears into flowers,
strung a garland of verse
and placed it
upon those hero-stones.

Here it is:

“The worthy will always
stand beside the worthy.....
Those without true worth
will fall away in history
like dry leaves!”

Is not the friendship
of these noble men
a great friendship
higher than life itself!
