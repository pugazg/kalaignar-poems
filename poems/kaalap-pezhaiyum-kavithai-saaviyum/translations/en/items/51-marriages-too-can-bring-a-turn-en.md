# Marriages Too Can Bring a Turn!

<!-- scan 252 -->

Marriages too
can bring a turn!

Rajendra Chola Devan,
Rajendra Chola II,

made the battlefield of “Koppam” itself
his coronation hall
and put on the crown
as king of kings.

Immediately there,
he cut down
the commanders
of the opposing Chalukya army:

Jayasingan,
Pulikesi,
Dasapanmavan,
Araiyan,
and Asokamayan.

He also captured
the two royal women,
Satyavai and Sangavai.

And with the blood-fury
of vengeance still unappeased,

he took as his own
the enemy country's royal elephants —
Satrubhayankaran,
Karabhathiran,
Moolabhathiran —

as well as camels,
horses,
and other possessions
of the Chalukya ruler.

In CE 1054,
amid heaps of corpses,
he received the imperial crown
on this earth;

bearing the name
Rajendra II,
he lived in the world
for only nine more years,
the source says —

<!-- scan 253 -->

and died in 1063.

Those who came to rule after him
were his younger brothers,
Rajamahendran
and Veera Rajendran;

they too,
history says,
did not live long.

Thus after Veera Rajendran
died in 1070,

Athirajendra came to rule;
he too lost his life
after a short span,
in an internal rebellion.

Thus the Chola state,
which had risen in CE 850
stretching its arm toward the sea,

for two hundred years
performed service to the people,
service to God,

war-service in meeting enemies
upon the battlefield,

and the service of government;

with Athirajendra Chola
it set like the sun.

Yet after that,
history paints as a luminous picture

the fifty years
in which Kulothunga Chola
held the sceptre
under the name
“Chalukya Chola.”

A luminous painting, yes;

but even upon that Chola moon,
the small blemish
called “Chalukya lineage”

clung like the shadow
of the “old woman Avvai.”

Kulothunga pushed
that little defect away
and conducted
a rule of distinction.

In two hundred years
of Chola history,

who is this
“Chalukya Chola”?

<!-- scan 254 -->

Once that question
has found a place,
can we withdraw
without answering it?

Not merely victories in war —
agreements of reconciliation too;

through taking and giving women
in marriage,
through marital exchanges,

and by using the mind,
was not Rajaraja
an incomparable guide
in guarding firmly
the territorial expanse
of the Chola country?

Was it not he
who arranged,
in that age,

a dynastic mixed marriage
between his beloved daughter,
the younger Kundavai,

and the warrior Vimaladitya,
a son of the Chalukya lineage?

The son born to those two
was Rajaraja Narendra —

he was Rajaraja's grandson
through his son.

Ammangai Devi,
the second daughter
of Rajendra I,

was likewise Rajaraja's
granddaughter
through his son.

If the son born
to Rajaraja Narendra
and Ammangai Devi
was Kulothunga Chola,

would history hesitate even a little
to call it
the Chalukya-Chola lineage —

especially in the way
our social doctrine is developing?

In an age
when rubbish-heaps
of *shastras*
are multiplying?

★ ★ ★

Even in his crawling age,
Kulothunga was intent
on watching the path
along which he crawled;

<!-- scan 255 -->

so people said
he was a schemer
like his grandfather,

and would not,
even by mistake,
take a road that led to defeat.

In his youth,
everyone in the town
called him Rajendra.

When he became
prince of Vengi country,
he was given the name
Vishnuvardhana.

He had many other
special names:

Virudaraja Bayankaran,
Karikalan,
Manukula Deepan,
Ulaguyya Vandhan,
Parthagan,
Sungam Thavirtha Cholan —

many names,
and many titles won!

On the ninth day
of June, CE 1070,
he wore the crown
as Chola emperor!

Thus he also became
the first king
to bear the epithet
“Tribhuvana Chakravarti”!

He was crowned
at Gangaikonda Cholapuram,
made Kanchipuram
another capital,

and made Pazhayarai
and Tirumazhapadi
places suited
to his administration.

He declared
that Vikrama Chola,
the son who captivated his heart,

would stand beside him
and succeed him;

Kulothunga ruled
for fifty years.

Yet the victory he gained
in the Kalinga war
that gave rise
to *Kalingattu Parani*

<!-- scan 256 -->

seems to have been
the final victory
of his reign.

Vikrama Chola's rule too
was a rule that honoured
honesty and justice,

a rule that did not lose
what his father had left behind.

Even so,
the hold over Lanka was lost;

that may be called
the beginning of great loss,
the beginning of retreat.

The Perumal temple
at Mannargudi
was raised by King Kulothunga;

therefore,
the source says,
it is called
“Kulothunga Chola Vinnagaram”!

A copper plate
recording Kulothunga Chola's
grant of land
to the Buddhist vihara at Nagai

can still be seen,
the source says,
at the exhibition in Leiden
in “Holland.”

One final point:
as Dr. Rasamanickanar says,

after Gangaikonda Cholapuram
gained the status of capital,

Tillai — Chidambaram —
began to gain special prominence!

From the time
of Vijayalaya Chola
to the time of Rajaraja,

they regarded Tiruvarur itself
as especially distinguished!

Perhaps that is because
of the relationship
between the two towns!
