# The Field of Blood Itself Became the Coronation Hall!

<!-- scan 246 -->

The field of blood itself
became the coronation hall!

Rajaraja Chola
had the heroic son Rajendra,

and the grandson Rajadhiraja;

when all three,
together in one formation
or in separate formations,

fought in other lands,
they did not return
carrying upon their shoulders
only new victories.

Gold and wealth,
pearls and coral,
shining diamonds,

and still more costly things —
eye-catching jewels and ornaments —

came back in heaps upon heaps.

Not merely to raise
their own clan
or their sovereignty,

but so the Thanjavur tower
might rise high,

so the temple
at Gangaikonda Cholapuram
might climb toward the sky,

and for other constructive works,
Arulmozhi Varman
and his descendants
poured out that wealth.

Nor did the royal queens
fail to give gifts
to masters of dance
and musicians.

Meanwhile,
Rajadhiraja defeated
the Chalukyas
who had continued
to create obstacles —

<!-- scan 247 -->

seized their capital,
“Kampili,”

and in the battle of “Poondur”
on the Krishna River,
destroyed their army
root and branch.

In Rajendra's final period,
the Chalukya “Agavamallan,”
whose attempt at invasion
had not succeeded,

turned to deceit:
acting like a friend,
he began a treacherous plan
to roll up and seize the country.

He sent several envoys
to Rajadhiraja,
presenting them
as mind-bewitching magicians,

wrote on a palm leaf
that there was nothing
they could not accomplish,

and asked Rajadhiraja
to receive their “blessing”!

Because Rajadhiraja
was not one who believed
that sacrifices and *yagas* alone
would give victory,

or one who distrusted
his own courage and intelligence,

he understood the intrigue
and decided
that this was the fitting answer
to the enemy.

He seized two
of those who had come as envoys:

one he had made
to wear “five kudumis”;

another he had dressed
in an “Aravani” woman's costume.

He named the two
“Agavamallan” and “Agavamalli”

and sent them back
to Agavamallan himself!

Unable to bear
the rage and humiliation,
Agavamallan cried,
“War! War!”

and clashed with Rajadhiraja
at a place called “Poondi.”

<!-- scan 248 -->

The result:
Agavamallan fell,

Rajadhiraja seized Kalyanapuram,

and on that very battlefield
he put on the valour-filled title
“Vijaya Rajendra.”

A *veerabhishekam*
was performed there;

and a gatekeeper statue
was given as a symbolic prize
of victory.

Upon the pedestal
of that diamond-hard statue
were written the words:

“Gatekeeper statue
brought after conquering Kalyanapuram.”

The source says
the statue still stands
in the Thanjavur Great Temple!

Researchers say
that before this,
the statue had been
in the Airavatesvara temple
at Darasuram.

★ ★ ★

With the joy
of receiving the title
“Vijaya Rajendra,”

and with a little arrogance too,
Rajadhiraja failed
to approach even an enemy
with civility.

Making Agavamallan's envoys
wear five kudumis
and an Aravani disguise,

he behaved without refinement;

I would call that
“a stain”
on Chola history!

Agavamallan,
the Chalukya
who had been deeply humiliated
by Vijaya Rajendra,

swore an oath
and entered upon extreme schemes
somehow to fight him,
defeat him,
and kill him.

Thinking the task
must be carried out
without haste,

he waited until CE 1054
to avenge himself
upon Rajadhiraja.

<!-- scan 249 -->

Then Agavamallan the Chalukya,
watching for the right moment,
began the plot once more.

At the battlefield called “Koppam,”
on the right bank
of the Krishna River,

at what is today called “Chitrapur,”

he prepared a great army
and formed a ring
to bring down Rajadhiraja,
Vijaya Rajendra.

Thunderous war
on both sides!

All of Agavamallan's soldiers
surrounded Vijaya Rajendra,

determined to complete
the work of revenge;

they aimed only at him,
loosed arrows,
and hurled swords!

Rajadhiraja,
hero among heroes,
praised as a Vijaya of the bow,

was fighting seated
upon the royal elephant.

Under the rain
of repeated attacks,
he had to fall
from that elephant!

He collapsed,
he fell,

received the honour-wound
upon the battlefield,
met a warrior's death,

and became forever
one worthy of festival-fame.

He who had been
a field where courage grew,

a dwelling place
where victories lived,

courageous enough
to comb his hair
with the rib-bones of enemies......

he who was equal
to Indrajit and Abhimanyu,

the lion-dream of his enemies —

that rising storm
called Rajadhiraja —

<!-- scan 250 -->

when Vijaya Rajendra fell,
his younger brother
Rajendra Chola Devan

had no time even
to wipe the tears
from his eyes.

Like thunder,
lightning and storm
striking together,

he leapt upon the enemy —

hack-hack,
cutting off the arms of many;

thud-thud,
piercing through the army;

cutting the heads
of the enemy host;

crying out,
“Victory! Victory!”

and crash-crash,
bringing the Chalukyas down,

he fought like a battle-cyclone
and won.

Those who had brought down
Vijaya Rajendra
were brought down.

With the light of his eyes
nearly extinguished by grief,
he brought down those
who had brought down
his own brother,

and Rajendra Chola Devan
stood victorious
with a hundred arrows
across his body!

With those arrows
still lodged in him
on the field —

and with a strange delight
in looking upon them —

“Bring the crown!”
cried the Chola host,
again and again;

the battlefield surged!

Taking that very place,
where blood was swelling,

as his coronation hall,
Rajendra Chola Devan
completed the ceremony
of becoming king
of the Chola country!

This was a novelty,
the source says,
in the battlefield history
of India!

<!-- scan 251 -->

Rajendra Chola Devan,
who thus made history,

went from there
to Kolhapur,
planted a victory pillar there,

and only afterwards
returned to the capital!

A crown was placed
upon one man;

for that,
must thirty thousand human beings
meet their end?

A Jain ascetic,
walking beside the battlefield
and singing such a question,

presented a sight
that could turn
even the eyes of the merciless
into lakes and ponds!
