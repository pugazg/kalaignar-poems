# The Knife Belongs to the Enemy; The Blood Is What We Give!

<!-- scan 29 -->

Whether Stone Age man wished
to learn many new things or not,

Nature herself stepped forward,
like a mother feeding milk to her child,
and taught him many things.

He killed animals for food,
ate their flesh,
and wore the flayed skin as clothing —

was that not truly the result
of a command unfolded by his brain?
And for him to make the tools
with which to live by force,

was it not Nature's own kindness
that told him, “Take iron and stone”?

Without lifting even a splinter,
letting the belly grow,
living as income pours in —
that paradise of landed domination;

if today there is a poor working class
rising with a roar to make it weep,
though there was none then,
the reason is surely that here

there sounds the cry of an intellectual revolution
that breaks the condition of slavery!

To the poor and ruined who asked,
“Why? For what? How?”
and were told by a crowd of scoundrels,
“It is all God's command” —

because Bharathidasan,
king above kings among poets,
beat the war-drum of verse,
calling for the worker to be set against
the binding knot called God —

<!-- scan 30 -->

we now see softness and humility
in the mansions of the very wealthy!

Because kindness and culture
could be found at the beginning of the Stone Age,
men made battlefields and fought
in forests, on hills and uplands!

Even if one says the Golden Age was better,
the support of wicked lords and great men
must be searched for only with a magnifying glass;

and to eyes wearing a Vedic lens,
not even drops of sweat
can ever be seen.

Festivals, spectacles,
all kinds of amusements —
a condition that had found unity in difference
changed;

because difference and division
grew swiftly,
hostility put down roots
and sent out shoots as a poisonous tree.

They hacked at one another,
struck one another with stones,
and by the hundreds, according to the count,
fell as victims on both sides;

all the possessions of both sides
lay wasted, unusable.

In the darkness of folly,
the warriors left standing on both sides
lit the lamp of death,
knelt and touched the earth,
and began to pay silent homage
to the martyrs of heroic death!

As I write this,
a passage from Pandit Nehru's
book of world history,
which I have read many times,

shadows itself across my heart
and stirs many memories.

Here — Nehru says:

“If we look closely at history,
we can understand the evolutionary process
by which this world has grown and changed —
‘as grass, as shrub, as worm, as tree,
as many forms of vegetation,
as bird, as snake,

<!-- scan 31 -->

as stone, as human’.”

He calls Manikkavasagar's *Tiruvachakam* itself
as evidence and witness;

and Pandit Nehru — the Light of Asia —
after praising the power
of human reason,
says in that same book:

“If, as we saw in the World War,
what we call progress means
united brothers destroying one another,
then there is some fundamental error
in that progress.”

What medicine can possibly cure
the anxiety of people like him
who say this?

Carrying a bowl of medicine,
a young woman came there —

I can say firmly:
she was not that beautiful enchantress,
the “magnet stone”!

With her eyes opened wide,
she walked toward the wounded,

placing each step so gently it might ache,
her lifted foot almost blistering —
slowly she walked.....very slowly.

She turned over one or two bodies and looked.
Except for the bodies of warriors who had fled,

all the others lay fallen
as honour-filled warriors
welcoming death.

The young woman walked with the medicine bowl —
an upright walk;
if you call it a majestic stride,
a proud stride —

<!-- scan 32 -->

all that tract of land,
registered in the names of many,
had been swallowed by that sinful landlord;

he had taken a heap of money,
distributed portions,
and belched in satisfaction.

To hide the sin he had committed,
he babbled like a madman:
“I shall perform sacrifices and yogas,”

“I shall win; I have no fear.”

What should have become the possession
of those who had the right to it,
men of fox-like nature
made their own property,

leaving many good people
in the middle of the street.
Cursing him — “May he perish!” —

the whole village boiled up;
the Rising Sun too rose.

At the waterside,
as though lying there waiting for her,
was a stone used for washing clothes.

The peahen climbed onto it and said:
“Comrades, lend me your ears;
listen to the ideas I speak!

We are only a few,
people living in the deep forest!

Those who burn with irritation and envy toward us
are, like us, only a few!

If irritation meets irritation,
only more irritation will flare,
and nothing but our ashes will remain.

To provoke both sides
and make them clash
requires not even a drop of intelligence.

<!-- scan 33 -->

The landlord lies asleep
in his bedchamber!
We labouring common folk alone
are travelling toward the graveyard!

From being Stone Age humans,
through the changes of time,

we are gradually becoming refined.
As proof that we have also gained reason,

let all of us unite;
first,
let us take high and low from among ourselves
and throw them into the rubbish bin!

After that,
to turn everything upside down,
let us raise the flag of revolution!
Rise — conduct the uprising!”

Seeing the young woman speak
as though a sluice-gate had suddenly burst open,

wounded and unwounded alike
came and stood there,
mouths agape:

“Ah! What a wonder!
You speak so beautifully —
how can you do it?”

When everyone asked,
she answered:

“I did not learn to speak
only for myself —
I learned to speak
so I could teach everyone to speak.”

Until then,
a warrior who had lain unmoving
under cover of darkness,
with an honour-wound upon his chest,

woke and asked:
“Do you know me, woman?”

<!-- scan 34 -->

“Why would I not know you?
Are you not my beloved?
Why did you join the enemy's camp?

To look upon traitors is itself betrayal,”
she said, and moved away!

“Woman, forgive me.
I came to understand the enemies,
and then returned here itself;
do you not know?

I am dying now
because the enemy's arrow
has pierced my chest;
look, my dear!” he said.

Crying “Ayyo!”
she embraced his body.

At the edge of death he asked:
“My beautiful statue!
When you say ‘revolution,’ when is it?
When you say ‘uprising,’ how is it?”

“My dear, what I spoke of
was not a revolution that breeds riot;
it was a cultural revolution!

Not an uprising that conducts a fight;
a social-justice uprising!
Everything only through the path of righteousness!” she said.

The dying man smiled and said:
“A war without knife, without blood —
that is it, is it not?”

“Yes — the knife is what the enemy brings;
the blood is what we give —
that is the righteous struggle!” she said.

Still embracing her,
his life went out.
