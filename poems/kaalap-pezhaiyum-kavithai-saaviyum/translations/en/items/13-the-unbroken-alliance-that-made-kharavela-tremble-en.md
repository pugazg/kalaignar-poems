# The Unbroken Alliance That Made Kharavela Tremble!

<!-- scan 64 -->

A Stone Age, a Golden Age —
ages have been classified so;
and within those ages history has been drawn,
showing how civilisational growth,
culture and custom differed
from country to country,
from people to people.

In that line came the great guiding book
*From the Volga to the Ganga*!

In that book its author,
according to each period and each region,
introduces characters
and follows the method of telling history through story.
The aim of the *Casket of Time* I write is also that;

but I do not wish completely to cast aside
those already introduced to us
through literature, history and ancient legend.
So that, in the history of India
advancing stage by stage through many fields,
the part belonging to the Tamil people
of the Dravidian race is not left out,

I seek to mark their presence.
Even if I cannot bring in all of them,
I present at least some,
so that the distinction of a balanced history
may not be broken.

Here then —
to show that the ancient age
that came to the Tamils
was indeed a golden age,
I shall set before you a few poetic lines.

<!-- scan 65 -->

As support for them,
I also wish to give notes
written by the multilingual scholar Appathuraiyar:

“The Sangam works repeatedly say
that the three crowned Tamil kings,
competing with one another,
engraved upon the Himalaya
the bow, the fish and the tiger emblems.
Many scholars and most historians
regard these as mere inventions.
But the many references,
at the horizon of known history,
to south–north relations,
to Tamil naval warfare from Tamil Nadu,
to wars against peoples of the far north,
and to victories won in wars with Aryans,
do not permit us to dismiss them as inventions.”

“*Pattuppaattu* says
that Neduncheralathan himself ruled
from the Himalaya to Kumari:

‘The famed Himalaya where the Aryans faltered,
and southern Kumari — between the two,
he crossed beyond those who claimed kingship over the earth.’

This may have been an extended rule,
a supremacy acknowledged by all kings,
or a victorious progress made throughout the region.”

“A Chola king, says the poet Paavaik Kottilar,
defeated the Aryans
in a battle at Vallam near Tanjai:

‘Like the Aryan army shattered at Vallam,
where the rain-cloud-like Chola won,
may the bracelet swelling on my straight forearm break!’
(*Akam* 336)

The lover-girl turns the anger she bears toward her beloved
upon the bracelet she wears:
‘Let my bangle break,
like the Aryan army that broke at the battle of Vallam!’”

“Crossing the army of the northern Aryans,
and beholding all the southern Tamil land,

<!-- scan 66 -->

with the goddess of unswerving chastity,
the Pandya Nedunchezhiyan
slept upon the royal couch.”
(*Silappathikaram*, concluding note to the Madurai canto)

Do not these pieces of evidence
show the condition of India
as a land where two great peoples —
Aryan and Dravidian — stood?

And further —

to open the casket of time
with the key of poetry
and show the Kalinga emperor Kharavela
would itself be a significant historical note.

He was one who,
to push Ashoka and the victories attributed to him
into second place,
and to bring his own name into first,
to secure his throne
and extend the reach of his rule,
tried every political stratagem required for it.

The period in which he gained fame
is placed between 163 BCE and 153 BCE —
that is, 2,158 years before the time of this writing;
yet he was a king of kings
well practised in setting up inscriptions
even to proclaim as his own
the achievements he claimed to have won.

As one asks,
“Why seek a mirror to see a wound already on the hand?”
so, many have said — and written —
that inscriptions themselves stand as witnesses.
This is no tale;
no imagination;
but evidence shown before the eye:

“An inscription of Kharavela records
that he broke an alliance of Tamil kings
which had continued for 113 years,”
says the multilingual scholar.

<!-- scan 67 -->

As Ashoka, praised by the world,
set out to spread Buddhism
across the globe,
Kharavela, in rivalry with him,
set out to spread Jainism
and even received the title
“the Jain Ashoka.”

Around a king who had gathered such victories,
would there not be a thousand men?
The Mauryas had never ventured
to wage such a war upon Tamil Nadu;
he ventured to collide with it.

(His army advanced with drums sounding;
and so that the Tamil land
would not be ruined by its entry,
so that the Tamil people
would not stumble and fall
under the wars he drove forward,

the three crowned kings,
the Velir rulers,
and Ilanchetchenni —
who crossed Seruppaazhi like a spark of fire —
formed a war-alliance,
met Kharavela fiercely in the field,
and sharpened sword and spear!

Kharavela dreamed
that he would dig a pit
and bury that alliance;
that dream did not come true.
It is this, Appathuraiyar shows
in his book *Wars of the South*,
that Kharavela “boasts of through his inscription.”

If we read it sharply,
we understand how rulers leap and rage
when they confront an alliance
that stands as death itself
to arrogant rule.
This is not merely a truth of yesterday and today;
nor news carried casually upon the wind!

History warns us
what form the Kalinga king Kharavela took
when he saw opposition rise against him;
to speak it forth is my duty!

**Seruppaazhi** = a battlefield encampment.
