# Kovoorar Questions — Heads Bow Down

<!-- scan 152 -->

Kovoorar questions —
heads bow down.

The grammatical discipline
of the Tamil language,
and its matchless literary density,

are lamps that stand illuminating
the good conduct of the Tamils
and their civilisation and culture
of worthy distinction —

so the scholar
Olvai Duraisami has explained.

That is not a mere opinion;
it is a valuable opinion —
a fragrant thought born of knowledge.

I shall give only one or two examples
as proof of it;
seeing our stature and pride,
you will say,
“Good! Good!”

★ ★ ★

Poets and bards —
when we speak of them,
they are masters
at composing verse and shaping melody;

in general,
they are good to everyone.

Is it *puram*?
Is it *akam*?
Is it the mode of the battlefield?

Taking up cool Tamil
mixed into their very blood,
they will call like cuckoos;

at times,
they will also spring like tigers!

Resolved,
they will swim forward
through the flood of their own blood,
longing for final victory
to belong to the Tamils!

Such poets today
may be very few in number;
but in those days —

<!-- scan 153 -->

they stood high
both in number and in thought.

Poets who could set a poem to mode
in the blink of an eye —

the songs they fashioned
in many-hued Tamil
could turn all our thoughts
into blazing fire
and send enemies running.

Young Nedunchezhiyan,
who led the battle of Talaiyalanganam,
was himself naturally a poet;

and what a vow he uttered,
so that the fame of his youth
would never be forgotten!

“If I do not bring down the enemies
in this war
and crown the honour of our people
with victory,

let the people beneath my parasol
revile me, saying,
‘Our sovereign is a cruel man!’

Let the great learned ones —
the great poets —

refuse to sing in wonder
of the land that I rule!

Let this country disgrace me
as one without the virtue of giving,

as one who said ‘No’
to those who came saying,
‘We have nothing!’”

For this *puram* song,
which could turn even a worm
into a tiger,
to thunder forth,

Nedunchezhiyan,
who ruled the earth,
made himself a poet as well.

★ ★ ★

<!-- scan 154 -->

Not only when war had already broken out —
whenever royal elder and younger brothers
stood opposed to one another in the field,

poet Kovoor Kizhar
would be there!

Was it not that great poet,
that master of the threefold Tamil,
who first prevented the brothers
Nalankilli and Nedunkilli
from clashing with one another?

When Nedunkilli,
failing to recognise the poet Ilanthathan,

mistook him for a spy
and hurriedly ordered
that he be put to death,

it was the learned Kovoor Kizhar —
versed also in the art of politics —

who earned the distinction
of preserving, for the time being,
both the justice of Nedunkilli's rule
and Nedunkilli himself
without his head bowing!

★ ★ ★

After defeating
Malaiyaman Tirumudi Kari,

when King Killivalavan ordered
that Kari's children — his treasures —
be put beneath the feet
of a rutting elephant and killed,

it was Kovoorar
who rushed in like lightning
and intervened.

That is why,
for people such as us,
he is accepted even now
as a poet who lives forever
in the history of the sweet Tamil people;

we praise him:
may he live forever!

★ ★ ★

<!-- scan 155 -->

In another war that followed,
afraid and trembling to face the attack,
Nedunkilli fled
from his capital at Avur
to Uraiyur!

Experience warned Nalankilli
that it was dangerous
to leave deceit behind
like a snake that had been struck;

he accepted that counsel
and followed him.

One Chola laid siege to the fort;
another Chola set his seal upon the fort!

Between the two armies
stood the eminent Tamil poet
Kovoor Kizhar.

He spoke outside the fort;
inside it too,
he opened his pure heart!

What did he say?
What argument did he place before them?

That is what now sounds
in this poem!
It sounds gently,

striving to put an end
to the enmity of the two:

“You are not one
wearing the white-spathed palmyra flower;

nor is he one
wearing a garland
of dark-branched neem.

The garland upon your chest
is the same as his;
the garland of the man
who fights you
is the same as yours.

If even one of you is defeated,
it is your clan that is defeated.

For both of you to win
is not in the nature of things;
therefore this deed of yours
is no good to your clan.

This quarrel will bring
heart-swelling delight
to other kings like you,
kings of bannered chariots.”

<!-- scan 156 -->

Its meaning:

“You two stand
face to face
on the battlefront.

One of you
is not the Chera king
who wears a garland
of palmyra flowers!

Nor is the other
the Pandya king
who wears a neem-flower garland.

Both of you wear
the same *aathi* garland,
one like the other;

yet you have come arrayed
against one another.

Even if one of you loses,
it is the Chola clan
that loses!

Can both of you
win this war?
Tell me!”

At Kovoorar's question,
the heads of both kings bowed.

They bowed —
but if we ask whether
their frenzy for war subsided,

Tamil soil was drenched once again
by the flood of their blood!

Only for a short time
was there a season of peace.

So long as people refuse
to honour Kovoor Kizhar's voice —

“If even one of you is defeated,
it is the clan that is defeated” —

so long as they refuse to honour
that “humanitarian feeling,”

we shall say
that ceasefire talks
have no meaning
in human society!
