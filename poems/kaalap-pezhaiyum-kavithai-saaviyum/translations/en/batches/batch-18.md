# Batch 18 — items 49–51

## Item 49 — They Saw Many Battlefields; They Won in Naval War Too!

Source metadata:

- stable item: **49**
- canonical Tamil title: `களம் பல கண்டனர்; கடற்போரிலும் வென்றனர்!`
- contents witness: `களம் பல கண்டனர்; கடற்போரிலும் வென்றனர்!`
- canonical file: `../../../sections/49.md`
- physical scans: **241–245**
- printed pages: **240–244**
- title-witness status: **contents/title-page match**

### English translation

<!-- scan 241 -->

They saw many battlefields;
they won in naval war too!

“If the mother leaps eight feet,
the calf leaps sixteen” —

that proverb became
an unequalled example
for father Rajaraja
and son Rajendra!

Rajadhiraja Chola,
only in the final period
of his reign,

crowned his son Rajendra
and made him king.

Rajendra Chola, however,
in the seventh year itself
after taking the throne,

handed the responsibility
of leading the country

to his younger son Rajadhiraja,
creating a startling turn
in history.

Father and son,
it is said,
ruled the Chola mandalam together
for twenty-five years —

<!-- scan 242 -->

a novelty
not seen until then
in the history of India!

To say still more:
historians divide
into two parts

the wars Rajadhiraja fought
while holding the responsibility
of crown prince.

On the battlefields
of both those parts,

the younger son Rajadhiraja
won fame
as his father's support,
as his equal,

and in one or two,
as one who surpassed his father.

The war of Idaithurai Nadu —
this was the plain
between the two rivers,
Krishna and Tungabhadra,

today's “Raichur”;
there the first victory
belonged to Rajendra Chola!

North of Gangavadi,
west of Nolambadi,

the army of the Western Chalukyas
rose in fury;

he subdued that enemy
like sprinkling water
into a boiling furnace,

and made it praise him
and bow in worship!

Northeast of Hyderabad
in Andhra,

the old “Kollipakkai,”
the source calls today's “Gulbarga”;

there the Chola army
broke the opposition apart
and brought home
victory after victory!

He felled the Rashtrakuta capital
called “Mannaik Kadakkam”

and won the great fame
of being called
“the Chola who took the land” —
*Mannai Konda Cholan*.

In CE 1017,
Rajendra's army
advanced by laying siege
and making war

upon the king of Lanka,
Mahinda V —

<!-- scan 243 -->

that Eelam king
who once before
had turned his back
and fled from Rajaraja.

They defeated him,
recovered the Pandya jewelled crown
he had seized and kept,

and also won the distinction
of holding him
as a prisoner of the Chola country!

Thus the *Mahavamsa*,
the source says,
points to the victory
of the Chola army
in the Eelam war,

and establishes the fame
of the Tamil great warriors!

The Chola empire,
which had made the tiger flag fly
in Eelam,

also sprang with its army
upon the Pandya country!

Vallabha Pandya,
who had been subjugated
in the time of Rajaraja,
king of kings,

perhaps now refused to bow
and began to act in opposition;

hearing this,
the Chola army
broke his back,

the source says,
pointing to the inscriptional phrase
“Kantalur Salaiyil Kalam Aruthaan”!

★ ★ ★

Rajendra Chola,
who had seen many battlefields,

won the heroic title
“Gangai Konda Cholan” —
Chola who took the Ganga —

and established,
as its sign,
the victory monument
called Gangaikonda Cholapuram.

The chief cause of both
was his foremost expedition:
the Ganga campaign!

<!-- scan 244 -->

Beginning with Vengi country,
at the end of the war
at Chakrakottam,

the Chola tiger flag
rose high!

Namanai Konam,
Panchappalli,

a part of Masuni country
on the banks of the Indus,

Ottara country
of South Kosala,
called Maha Kosala,

and the regions
along the banks
of the Mahanadi —

after conquering them,
the Chola army,
says the Thiruvalangadu copper plate
with pride,

collected from its enemies
box upon box
of heaps of many jewels
as tribute.

That same copper plate
also says
that our classical-Tamil Chola,

to cross the Ganga,
lined up many hundreds of elephants
and made them into a bridge,

marched the army across,
reached the opposite bank,
and defeated the enemy!

Pandarathar says
that the Kosala region
called Purva Desa too
fell to the Cholas
as a prize of battle!

The sword that whirled everywhere
crying victory, victory,

whirled too toward Dharmapala
of Dandabhukti country
in the Midnapore district of Bengal;

and that boy's crown
fell because of it!

The Chola army then
stepped onward
into the land
of Ranasura,
king of Dakshina Lada!

Another ruler
of a Bengal state,
Govindachandan,
laid his victory
at the Chola's feet!

<!-- scan 245 -->

Another was Mahipalan,
called an incomparable warrior;

though his rule,
the source says,
spread as far as Kasi,

before the armies
of Rajendra and Rajadhiraja
it flew away
like dust in the wind!

As a sign
of the Vatapi victory,
Pallava commander Paranjothi

brought back a statue —
the Vatapi Ganapati!

Likewise,
the explanation is given
that the statue
brought by the Chola commander

in memory of defeating
many northern kings

is the Ganga Vinayakar statue
at the Nageswarar temple
in Kudanthai!

Among Rajendra Chola's
victories across the sea,

the victory over Kadaram
was the victory
that immersed them in delight.

His *meikeerthis*
and the Thiruvalangadu copper plates,
the source says,
still tell us of it today.

It is said
that pots of Ganga water
were placed upon the heads
of defeated northern soldiers;

as those men came
in ordered ranks,

the two kings,
Rajendra and Rajadhiraja,
stretched out both hands
to receive them!

Now let us show our head
to another enemy!

## Item 50 — The Field of Blood Itself Became the Coronation Hall!

- stable item: **50**
- canonical Tamil title: `குருதிக்களமே; கொலு மண்டபம் ஆனது!`
- contents witness: `குருதிக் களமே! கொலு மண்டபம் ஆனது!`
- canonical file: `../../../sections/50.md`
- physical scans: **246–251**
- printed pages: **245–250**
- title-witness status: **documented discrepancy — canonical has joined `குருதிக்களமே` followed by `;`; contents has `குருதிக் களமே!` and changes the first punctuation to `!`**

### English translation

<!-- scan 246 -->

The field of blood itself
became the coronation hall!

Rajaraja Chola
had the heroic son Rajendra,

and the grandson Rajadhiraja;

when all three,
together in one formation
or in separate formations,

fought in other lands,
they did not return
carrying upon their shoulders
only new victories.

Gold and wealth,
pearls and coral,
shining diamonds,

and still more costly things —
eye-catching jewels and ornaments —

came back in heaps upon heaps.

Not merely to raise
their own clan
or their sovereignty,

but so the Thanjavur tower
might rise high,

so the temple
at Gangaikonda Cholapuram
might climb toward the sky,

and for other constructive works,
Arulmozhi Varman
and his descendants
poured out that wealth.

Nor did the royal queens
fail to give gifts
to masters of dance
and musicians.

Meanwhile,
Rajadhiraja defeated
the Chalukyas
who had continued
to create obstacles —

<!-- scan 247 -->

seized their capital,
“Kampili,”

and in the battle of “Poondur”
on the Krishna River,
destroyed their army
root and branch.

In Rajendra's final period,
the Chalukya “Agavamallan,”
whose attempt at invasion
had not succeeded,

turned to deceit:
acting like a friend,
he began a treacherous plan
to roll up and seize the country.

He sent several envoys
to Rajadhiraja,
presenting them
as mind-bewitching magicians,

wrote on a palm leaf
that there was nothing
they could not accomplish,

and asked Rajadhiraja
to receive their “blessing”!

Because Rajadhiraja
was not one who believed
that sacrifices and *yagas* alone
would give victory,

or one who distrusted
his own courage and intelligence,

he understood the intrigue
and decided
that this was the fitting answer
to the enemy.

He seized two
of those who had come as envoys:

one he had made
to wear “five kudumis”;

another he had dressed
in an “Aravani” woman's costume.

He named the two
“Agavamallan” and “Agavamalli”

and sent them back
to Agavamallan himself!

Unable to bear
the rage and humiliation,
Agavamallan cried,
“War! War!”

and clashed with Rajadhiraja
at a place called “Poondi.”

<!-- scan 248 -->

The result:
Agavamallan fell,

Rajadhiraja seized Kalyanapuram,

and on that very battlefield
he put on the valour-filled title
“Vijaya Rajendra.”

A *veerabhishekam*
was performed there;

and a gatekeeper statue
was given as a symbolic prize
of victory.

Upon the pedestal
of that diamond-hard statue
were written the words:

“Gatekeeper statue
brought after conquering Kalyanapuram.”

The source says
the statue still stands
in the Thanjavur Great Temple!

Researchers say
that before this,
the statue had been
in the Airavatesvara temple
at Darasuram.

★ ★ ★

With the joy
of receiving the title
“Vijaya Rajendra,”

and with a little arrogance too,
Rajadhiraja failed
to approach even an enemy
with civility.

Making Agavamallan's envoys
wear five kudumis
and an Aravani disguise,

he behaved without refinement;

I would call that
“a stain”
on Chola history!

Agavamallan,
the Chalukya
who had been deeply humiliated
by Vijaya Rajendra,

swore an oath
and entered upon extreme schemes
somehow to fight him,
defeat him,
and kill him.

Thinking the task
must be carried out
without haste,

he waited until CE 1054
to avenge himself
upon Rajadhiraja.

<!-- scan 249 -->

Then Agavamallan the Chalukya,
watching for the right moment,
began the plot once more.

At the battlefield called “Koppam,”
on the right bank
of the Krishna River,

at what is today called “Chitrapur,”

he prepared a great army
and formed a ring
to bring down Rajadhiraja,
Vijaya Rajendra.

Thunderous war
on both sides!

All of Agavamallan's soldiers
surrounded Vijaya Rajendra,

determined to complete
the work of revenge;

they aimed only at him,
loosed arrows,
and hurled swords!

Rajadhiraja,
hero among heroes,
praised as a Vijaya of the bow,

was fighting seated
upon the royal elephant.

Under the rain
of repeated attacks,
he had to fall
from that elephant!

He collapsed,
he fell,

received the honour-wound
upon the battlefield,
met a warrior's death,

and became forever
one worthy of festival-fame.

He who had been
a field where courage grew,

a dwelling place
where victories lived,

courageous enough
to comb his hair
with the rib-bones of enemies......

he who was equal
to Indrajit and Abhimanyu,

the lion-dream of his enemies —

that rising storm
called Rajadhiraja —

<!-- scan 250 -->

when Vijaya Rajendra fell,
his younger brother
Rajendra Chola Devan

had no time even
to wipe the tears
from his eyes.

Like thunder,
lightning and storm
striking together,

he leapt upon the enemy —

hack-hack,
cutting off the arms of many;

thud-thud,
piercing through the army;

cutting the heads
of the enemy host;

crying out,
“Victory! Victory!”

and crash-crash,
bringing the Chalukyas down,

he fought like a battle-cyclone
and won.

Those who had brought down
Vijaya Rajendra
were brought down.

With the light of his eyes
nearly extinguished by grief,
he brought down those
who had brought down
his own brother,

and Rajendra Chola Devan
stood victorious
with a hundred arrows
across his body!

With those arrows
still lodged in him
on the field —

and with a strange delight
in looking upon them —

“Bring the crown!”
cried the Chola host,
again and again;

the battlefield surged!

Taking that very place,
where blood was swelling,

as his coronation hall,
Rajendra Chola Devan
completed the ceremony
of becoming king
of the Chola country!

This was a novelty,
the source says,
in the battlefield history
of India!

<!-- scan 251 -->

Rajendra Chola Devan,
who thus made history,

went from there
to Kolhapur,
planted a victory pillar there,

and only afterwards
returned to the capital!

A crown was placed
upon one man;

for that,
must thirty thousand human beings
meet their end?

A Jain ascetic,
walking beside the battlefield
and singing such a question,

presented a sight
that could turn
even the eyes of the merciless
into lakes and ponds!

## Item 51 — Marriages Too Can Bring a Turn!

Source metadata:

- stable item: **51**
- canonical Tamil title: `திருமணங்களாலும் வருவதுண்டு திருப்பம்!`
- contents witness: `திருமணங்களாலும் வருவதுண்டு திருப்பம்!`
- canonical file: `../../../sections/51.md`
- physical scans: **252–256**
- printed pages: **251–255**
- title-witness status: **contents/title-page match**

### English translation

<!-- scan 252 -->

Marriages too
can bring a turn!

Rajendra Chola Devan,
Rajendra Chola II,

made the battlefield of “Koppam” itself
his coronation hall
and put on the crown
as king of kings.

Immediately there,
he cut down
the commanders
of the opposing Chalukya army:

Jayasingan,
Pulikesi,
Dasapanmavan,
Araiyan,
and Asokamayan.

He also captured
the two royal women,
Satyavai and Sangavai.

And with the blood-fury
of vengeance still unappeased,

he took as his own
the enemy country's royal elephants —
Satrubhayankaran,
Karabhathiran,
Moolabhathiran —

as well as camels,
horses,
and other possessions
of the Chalukya ruler.

In CE 1054,
amid heaps of corpses,
he received the imperial crown
on this earth;

bearing the name
Rajendra II,
he lived in the world
for only nine more years,
the source says —

<!-- scan 253 -->

and died in 1063.

Those who came to rule after him
were his younger brothers,
Rajamahendran
and Veera Rajendran;

they too,
history says,
did not live long.

Thus after Veera Rajendran
died in 1070,

Athirajendra came to rule;
he too lost his life
after a short span,
in an internal rebellion.

Thus the Chola state,
which had risen in CE 850
stretching its arm toward the sea,

for two hundred years
performed service to the people,
service to God,

war-service in meeting enemies
upon the battlefield,

and the service of government;

with Athirajendra Chola
it set like the sun.

Yet after that,
history paints as a luminous picture

the fifty years
in which Kulothunga Chola
held the sceptre
under the name
“Chalukya Chola.”

A luminous painting, yes;

but even upon that Chola moon,
the small blemish
called “Chalukya lineage”

clung like the shadow
of the “old woman Avvai.”

Kulothunga pushed
that little defect away
and conducted
a rule of distinction.

In two hundred years
of Chola history,

who is this
“Chalukya Chola”?

<!-- scan 254 -->

Once that question
has found a place,
can we withdraw
without answering it?

Not merely victories in war —
agreements of reconciliation too;

through taking and giving women
in marriage,
through marital exchanges,

and by using the mind,
was not Rajaraja
an incomparable guide
in guarding firmly
the territorial expanse
of the Chola country?

Was it not he
who arranged,
in that age,

a dynastic mixed marriage
between his beloved daughter,
the younger Kundavai,

and the warrior Vimaladitya,
a son of the Chalukya lineage?

The son born to those two
was Rajaraja Narendra —

he was Rajaraja's grandson
through his son.

Ammangai Devi,
the second daughter
of Rajendra I,

was likewise Rajaraja's
granddaughter
through his son.

If the son born
to Rajaraja Narendra
and Ammangai Devi
was Kulothunga Chola,

would history hesitate even a little
to call it
the Chalukya-Chola lineage —

especially in the way
our social doctrine is developing?

In an age
when rubbish-heaps
of *shastras*
are multiplying?

★ ★ ★

Even in his crawling age,
Kulothunga was intent
on watching the path
along which he crawled;

<!-- scan 255 -->

so people said
he was a schemer
like his grandfather,

and would not,
even by mistake,
take a road that led to defeat.

In his youth,
everyone in the town
called him Rajendra.

When he became
prince of Vengi country,
he was given the name
Vishnuvardhana.

He had many other
special names:

Virudaraja Bayankaran,
Karikalan,
Manukula Deepan,
Ulaguyya Vandhan,
Parthagan,
Sungam Thavirtha Cholan —

many names,
and many titles won!

On the ninth day
of June, CE 1070,
he wore the crown
as Chola emperor!

Thus he also became
the first king
to bear the epithet
“Tribhuvana Chakravarti”!

He was crowned
at Gangaikonda Cholapuram,
made Kanchipuram
another capital,

and made Pazhayarai
and Tirumazhapadi
places suited
to his administration.

He declared
that Vikrama Chola,
the son who captivated his heart,

would stand beside him
and succeed him;

Kulothunga ruled
for fifty years.

Yet the victory he gained
in the Kalinga war
that gave rise
to *Kalingattu Parani*

<!-- scan 256 -->

seems to have been
the final victory
of his reign.

Vikrama Chola's rule too
was a rule that honoured
honesty and justice,

a rule that did not lose
what his father had left behind.

Even so,
the hold over Lanka was lost;

that may be called
the beginning of great loss,
the beginning of retreat.

The Perumal temple
at Mannargudi
was raised by King Kulothunga;

therefore,
the source says,
it is called
“Kulothunga Chola Vinnagaram”!

A copper plate
recording Kulothunga Chola's
grant of land
to the Buddhist vihara at Nagai

can still be seen,
the source says,
at the exhibition in Leiden
in “Holland.”

One final point:
as Dr. Rasamanickanar says,

after Gangaikonda Cholapuram
gained the status of capital,

Tillai — Chidambaram —
began to gain special prominence!

From the time
of Vijayalaya Chola
to the time of Rajaraja,

they regarded Tiruvarur itself
as especially distinguished!

Perhaps that is because
of the relationship
between the two towns!

## Translator notes

- Item 49 preserves the source's succession wording as printed, including the Rajadhiraja/Rajendra father-son phrasing and the later Rajendra/Rajadhiraja formulation; the English does not silently reconcile that sequence against outside chronology.
- Item 49 keeps the `Mannaik Kadakkam / Mannai Konda Cholan` sound-and-word relationship visible through transliteration. The Raichur, Kollipakkai/Gulbarga, Mahinda V, Kantalur Salai, northern-campaign, elephant-bridge, Ganga Vinayakar and Kadaram statements are translated as source claims, not externally corrected history.
- Item 50 preserves both Tamil title witnesses separately. The displayed English title follows canonical `குருதிக்களமே; கொலு மண்டபம் ஆனது!`; the contents witness's spacing and punctuation changes remain metadata only.
- Item 50 retains the source terms `அய்ந்து குடுமி` as “five kudumis” and `அரவாணி` as “Aravani” in the humiliation episode rather than silently replacing the historical/source wording with a modern identity label. Kalaignar's own immediate condemnation of the act as a `கறை` — “stain” — on Chola history is preserved distinctly.
- Item 50 keeps the battle onomatopoeia as active sound (`கர கர`, `பட பட`, `சடசட`) through “hack-hack / thud-thud / crash-crash,” rather than flattening the passage into neutral battle summary. The hundred-arrow image, battlefield coronation and Jain ascetic's thirty-thousand-lives question remain separate movements.
- Item 51 preserves the source's violent opening inventory, succession chronology, Chalukya-Chola framing, marriage-genealogy argument, anti-*shastra* polemic, Kulothunga title list, CE 1070 date, fifty-year reign claim, *Kalingattu Parani* reference, Lanka-retreat statement and Leiden copper-plate claim as the source presents them.
- The `ஒளவைக் கிழவி` image in Item 51 is retained as the “old woman Avvai” shadow clinging to the Chola moon rather than rationalised away; its source-cultural image will be considered again during full-work consistency review if a release glossary is needed.
- Administrative, dynastic and literary names are left source-traceable; no outside genealogy or chronology was used to repair apparent tensions in the Tamil.
- No final-cleared Tamil canonical file or verified page record was modified while preparing this batch.
