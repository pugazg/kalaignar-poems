### English translation

<!-- scan 179 -->

Can he
be bought with love?

There was a woman poet named Narkannai,
who fashioned poems in sugar-candy Tamil.

Instead of composing only of this world and the next,
she set six poems like golden gems
in the Sangam collections
*Agam*, *Puram* and *Natrinai* —

six poems that were the pressed juice
of the fruits of love!

Along with those six poems,
there is the war-song in *Puram*
of the warrior-clan mother called Kaavarp Penn,

who, when a maiden asked
about the son who had gone to battle,
said with swelling pride,
“Here is the cave where the tiger lived!”

That mother herself
was the foster-mother who raised
Perunarkilli, the great Chola,
as though she were his own mother.

Narkannai, enchanted
by Killi's beauty and prowess,
floated in a dream-world
as though drinking him in by the handful.

And Sattanthaiyar,
the lord among poets,
set Perunarkilli's great manliness
into a proud-stepping *Puram* song
and crowned him with a garland of praise.

After going a little deeper
and studying all this,
there arose in me an imagination
like a prop-root to the banyan!

<!-- scan 180 -->

★ ★ ★

Narkannai's love for Narkilli
rose like saffron dye,

like milk swelling in a golden bowl
and overflowing onto the ground.

I shaped that story
into a moving serial picture
called “One-Sided Love,”
into a little *Kuruntokai*-like epic.

Before you read and relish those days,
see now its first part —
like sugarcane to be drunk and savoured
in *Murasoli*'s “Time-Casket”!

Since this too points
to the history of the early Chola period,
regard it also as a link
in that chain of history!

★ ★ ★

In Uraiyur,
the ancient capital
of the prosperous Chola land,
where crops danced in the fields

like a young peacock in green silk,
waistcloth tied,
dancing Bharatanatyam,

there sat beneath the white royal umbrella
Veera-Venman Veliyan Tittan,

pouring out the great wealth of the state
upon sacrifices and *yagas*
conducted by ascetics.

“This does not suit Tamil tradition,”
said his son,
standing against it
and continuing to oppose it.

But Chola king Tittan
ignored every argument
made by his son Perunarkilli.

Like an ignorant child
who does not know the worth
of bitter medicine that cures disease
and knocks it away rather than drink it,

<!-- scan 181 -->

he rejected every good counsel
Narkilli offered
and kept pouring away
the country's wealth.

“Is it the people's task
to fill the royal treasury,
only for their lives to be turned barren
and grief driven into their hearts?

The beauty of a king
is to rule embracing his people;
what those without principle do
is set fire even to a noble purpose!

Milk, honey and cow's ghee
are poured pot after pot into fire;

sheep, cattle and horses
are cut down as sacrifice;

sacrificial pits are raised
and penance performed
to obtain the *avir-bhagam*.

Does this not shake the very foundation
of the philosophy that says,
‘Love itself is God’?”

So Killi questioned him
and tried to stop it.

“I am the king who rules this land!
And you, my own son,
stand against me,
wearing your tongue raw
with atheist talk?

Before I have your tongue cut out
and thrown to dogs and jackals,
leave this country!”
ordered Tittan.

“This very day
I leave Uraiyur that gave me birth.
But, King,

when I say there is one God
and that no living sacrifice is needed,
you call that atheism;

while you,
to reach the heaven you say
hangs above the sky,
turn this earth itself into hell
and torment living beings!

A madman may yet be made clear-minded;
but I leave having decided
that my father Tittan
can no longer be set right.”

<!-- scan 182 -->

So the golden-templed Perunarkilli
left Uraiyur
and went to Amur
in Mukkaaval Nadu.

At that very moment,
Sattanthai, king among words,
refused to remain before the Chola king
and followed Killi.

When the crown prince himself
had given up the throne
for the sake of his principle,

he cast aside as trivial
his own post as court poet,

vowing to remain by Killi
and protect the honour,
valour,
culture and tradition
of the Tamil people
from being lowered.

★ ★ ★

One day in Amur of Mukkaaval Nadu,
a rough wrestler came running,
slapping his shoulders,

and roared a challenge:
“Who is there in this town
who can defeat me?”

He declared further
that if the people of that town
could not stand before him
and were defeated,
all Mukkaaval Nadu
would become his possession!

The ruler of the land heard it,
marched forward like a warrior
and tried to bring down
that wild-buffalo of a man
in wrestling —

but he was defeated!

“From now on Amur belongs to me,
the victor! Aha! Oho!”
shouted the wrestler.

Then, with the speed
of a star falling from the sky,

<!-- scan 183 -->

one great warrior
of the martial clan
leapt up,
struck the earth hard,
thumped his chest and cried,

“Wrestler — stop there!”

and sprang upon
that human beast.

“Who is it? Who is it?”
Amur shook with the cry.

One crowd clapped:
“It is Perunarkilli,
son of Tittan of Uraiyur!”

Another crowd stood
with tear-filled eyes,
wondering what would happen.

“If victory itself escaped
the ruler of Amur,
will it come to a youth
younger than he?”

The people of Mukkaaval Nadu
stood astonished,
fingers to their noses.

“One breath from me
and you will fly like chaff!
Such arrogance in you?”

The brutal wrestler
charged at Killi.

Let us watch from a little distance,
through the window
of Sattanthaiyar's poem,
that wrestling bout
which whirled like a cyclone!

Narkilli sprang upon the wrestler,
folded one leg above his chest,

blocked his opponent's attack
with the other,

locked himself hard
around the brute's back,

and, like an elephant
trying to eat bamboo,
bending it,
breaking it
and trampling it,

he smashed together
the wrestler's head and legs,
broke him,
threw him to the earth
and killed him.

The poet hailed the Chola prince
with the honorific
“Poravai Kopperunarkilli,”

but grieved deeply
that Tittan,
who ruled Uraiyur,
had not come
to see his son's valour.

Sattanthai,
lofty Tamil poet,
lets word-honey drip and drip
as he explains in simile
the speed of Narkilli's battle.
Here it is:

<!-- scan 184 -->

★ ★ ★

“As though a festival had begun,
as though a woman had entered labour,

on a rain-laden day,
with the sun already sinking,

how fast the needle moves
in the hand of the lowly man
who weaves a cot —

faster than that was the battle
of the tall hero in garlands
against the warrior
who came to seize the town!”

(*Purananuru*, poem 82;
poet: Sattanthaiyar)

I shall give its fitting meaning
in the Tamil I have learnt.
You will feel
the sweetness of this simile!

“A poor worker weaving a cot
must finish and hurry away
before the festival begins,
so he can sell it and live.

Just then news pierces his ear:
his wedded wife is writhing
in the pains of childbirth.

He trembles to finish his work
and set out;

then he sees the sun
falling in the western sky
and signs of heavy rain.

How fiercely fast
would the needle in his hand
shoot through the binding strips
as he finishes that cot!

Faster even than that needle,
warrior Killi finished the battle
and flung his enemy away like dust!”

Thus,

before the garland of verse
from the master of Muttamil,
and the flower-garland
from the people of Mukkaaval Nadu,

<!-- scan 185 -->

Narkilli bowed his head
and offered thanks.

There,
a maiden of artistic grace,
her heart rising in waves of love,
looked at her friend
and asked with her eyes:

“Can he be bought with love?”

Unable to steady herself,
that maiden suffered,
climbed the mountain peak
and heaved a great sigh —

that maiden
was Narkannai!

## Item 38 — He Who Lives in the Hearts of the Grateful!

### English translation

<!-- scan 186 -->

The “Chola moon”
poured pearl-light
and spread its fame;

then rival groups multiplied,
unity broke,
and decline began,

before the land had to face
its “age of darkness.”

Before that,
two rulers had taken responsibility
and won renown.

After the rule of those two,
there was no one of comparable stature
who could properly be set beside them.

So, beside Kopperuncholan,
who bore the sceptre
and protected his people,

I take pleasure in lifting my pen
to speak of Killivalavan,
who bore the spear
and fought many heroic wars —

though I carry sorrow in my heart.

To call him
“Killivalavan who died at Kulamutram”
means this:

he went against Chera and Pandya together,
fought as a warrior,
received a glorious wound on the battlefront,

and the place where he fell
was called Kulamutram.

After Kulamutrattan
and Kopperuncholan,
Chola history rolled through
an age of darkness
for five hundred years;

only after that
do the Later Cholas
enter history.

<!-- scan 187 -->

Let us now meet Killi —
the one who died at Kulamutram —
who remains in literature
as “living history.”

★ ★ ★

The treasury of *Purananuru*
holds many poems of praise
singing his greatness,
written by many poets.

“Even for sinners
who cut the udder of a cow,

for those who trample
and destroy the womb of a woman,

for those who torture
the loving parents who gave them life,
there may yet be forgiveness;

but even if the whole earth
turns upside down,
directions reverse
and the world overturns,

the life of one
who murders gratitude
will fall to ruin and perish.”

So sang the poet Alathur Kizhar,
who had received gifts from him,
returning gratitude with affection
and offering good wishes.

When King Killi
laid siege to Karur,
Alathur Kizhar went before him and asked:

“You hear the sound
of the guard-trees falling;

inside the palace
he hides,
afraid and trembling —

is there greatness
in defeating such a man,
a coward without manliness?”

Killi heard the poet's
shower of counsel,

and turned back
with the army
that had come surging forward.

Like a worm
that had fallen upon burning embers
and somehow escaped,
the king of Karur survived!

“Kalan himself waits for his hour
to take a life;

but you,
guardian of the Chola clan,
on the battlefield,

<!-- scan 188 -->

at the very moment you decide,
you take the enemy's life!”

So Kovoor Kizhar marvels
at the valour
with which he completes his deed.

He says also:

“As a man who went to cut firewood
might find gold in the forest,
so those who come to King Killi
receive gifts.”

“Like a mother tiger
protecting her cub,
you are the king
who protects the Tamil clan!

Honey between the sugarcane,
vaalai fish at the sluice,
red water-lily flowers
plucked by maidens,

tortoises discovered
by those who plough the fields,

and, wherever the Kaveri flood turns,
grains of paddy everywhere —

O king who rules
this radiant, fertile Chola land!”

Thus the poet Idaikkadanar
raises Killivalavan high in praise.

“Your umbrella, O king,
is no umbrella merely to shade you
from the sun;

it is the white royal umbrella
that drives away suffering
and gives good rule.

Even when struck
by flood-ruin or drought,
the farming people labour
without losing heart.

Every victory
your warriors engrave
on the battlefront
cannot happen
without the support
of the plough-front;

so,
that the warrior clan may live,
may the farming clan be honoured
and live!”

<!-- scan 189 -->

Thus Vellaikkudi Naganar,
the poet,
offered the king
both counsel and blessing.

“Even if the whole world
must perish
under nature's fury,
why should we fear
while King Killi lives?”

So Erukkattur poet Thayankannanar
strung countless pearl-verses of praise.
Researchers say
he was a great Tamil poet
who lived near Kulikkarai in Thanjavur.

★ ★ ★

In Sirukudi,
a small village of Chola country,
Killi had a friend
like the shade of a generous tree.

He learnt that Pannan,
that noble elder,
gave away to the poor
all the paddy harvested
from his lands.

Killi went there
and gave him the title
“physician for the disease of hunger”!

“O Pannan,
whose charity pours like the sky,
may you live
adding even the days I have to live
to your own!”

He wrote this *Puram* poem,
placed it in Pannan's hand,
and set out toward Uraiyur,
the capital.

Whatever army an enemy brought,
Valavan wrote his heroic history
with the result already known:
they were defeated by him.

Yet one day
that gracious form,
with a face lovely as cow's milk,
fell within the grip of death
on the battlefield.

When the news was heard,
it was as though the eardrum itself tore;

the gathering of classical-Tamil poets
poured a waterfall of tears!

<!-- scan 190 -->

“To make a burial urn
for the dead,
I ask the finest among potters:

if the urn is to hold
our King Killi,

then bend the eastern and western skies
around it,

make the whole earth
into the potter's wheel,

and knead towering Mount Meru itself
to fashion the clay for that urn!

If it is only the physical body
you wish to bury,
your ordinary urn will do;

but to contain Killi's body of fame,
this whole earth itself
is the fitting urn.”

So Aiyur Mudavanar,
writing his lament in grief,
found that the Chola's fame
had no boundary!

## Item 39 — Not One Who Came on His Own; One Brought by the Commander!

### English translation

<!-- scan 191 -->

If we begin writing
the history of the South
from the most ancient times,

lists of the ancient Tamil kings —
Chera, Chola and Pandya —
long lists and short lists alike,
come and stand before us.

From the three crowned kings
who bore three banners
and nurtured Muttamil,

the people who saw
three Sangams — first, middle and last —

when we say Chera,
we think of the great emperor Senguttuvan;

when we say Chola,
of Tirumavalavan,
Karikal Peruvalattan;

when we say Pandya,
of Mudat Tirumaran,
who ruled from Kapatapuram,
the capital taken by the sea,
and fostered Tamil.

Up to the mature age
in which the three Sangams lived,
their crowned empires,
with grip unloosened,
lived in great prosperity!

In those times,
they fought fierce wars
to extend the lands they ruled
and settle long-standing enmities;

then they embraced,
cast aside bitterness,

and, though ruling
their separate territories,
maintained friendship.

<!-- scan 192 -->

No one has written
the history of any one of them
from its very alphabetic beginning
to a final point and said,
“This alone is the whole of it.”

Nor is that possible!

Later,
before the Later Chola rule
was born, grew
and became a great power,

in the intervening five hundred years,
there were many groups:

those called Kalavar,
those called Kalabhras,
and the Satavahanas,
said to have ruled up to the Ganga.

They formed governments
like small territorial divisions,
built forts for them
and ruled.

Yet among Pallava kings,
Mahendravarman of undying fame
and his son,
the great warrior Narasimhavarman,
were the strongest,
and, the source says,
among the best.

They were lion-like heirs
of the Simhavishnu line.

With Kanchi as their capital,
they ruled in monarchy
for three hundred years.

History says
that Pallavas such as Nandivarman
defeated even Pandya and Chola kings
in some battles.

Mahendra,
who once held that Jainism
was the faith he accepted,

later became a Saiva king;
then he mocked Jainism,

wrote the play *Matta Vilasam*,
and used it
as an instrument of religious propaganda.

Seeing this,
Jains looked back on the past
with deep sorrow,
asking:

“Can a mind change this much
the moment one crosses
to another camp?”

<!-- scan 193 -->

Many researchers have even raised doubt
over whether Kanchi itself
was the Pallava capital.

As evidence,
one loudly identifies
Petta Kanchi in the Andhra region
as the Pallava capital Kanchi!

The Chinese traveller
“Yuan Suwang,”
it is conjectured,
may also have spoken
of that Kanchi;

we shall know the truth!

In the Chinese traveller's
special account
there is also a drop of honey
for our thought:

“Ashoka planted many pillars
in the Dravidian country;
some of those pillars
stand damaged.”

When I think
of all the times,
and of all the kinds of scholars,
by whom the name “Dravida”
has been uttered,

my heart swells —
joy overflows!

The Chalukya Pulikesi
came to war
to make Mahendravarman
bend his knee in battle.

Both great armies
leapt into war
like erupting volcanoes.

Then King Pulikesi
had a falsehood carved
into an inscription:

that in the battle of Pullalur,
Mahendra had run away
and hidden!

The great king Narasimhan,
who rejected that claim,
was also Mamallan —

and because of that name
Mamallapuram itself arose,
the source declares.

As if to prove it,
he challenged:

“Will you come
for a face-to-face battle?”

<!-- scan 194 -->

The moment that challenge rang out,
Pulikesi ran asking,
“Where is the room
in which I can hide?”

and hid himself inside it!

Pallava Narasimhan
did not let that storm-maker go.

He sent his commander Paranjothi
in pursuit,

all the way to Vatapi,
Pulikesi's capital.

There,
he exposed to the world
the wound upon Pulikesi's back;

and, as a sign
that they had worn the garland of victory,

he brought to Tamil Nadu
as war-spoil
a beautiful elephant image
that had stood in the city of Vatapi.

Even today,
music programmes begin
with praise of Vatapi Ganapati;

that, says the source,
was only after
the elephant emblem
brought that day
by commander Paranjothi
became known to the Tamils.

Therefore Pillaiyar
is not one who came on his own
to grant boons;

history, as presented here, says
he was brought to Tamil Nadu
from Vatapi
by our commander Paranjothi,
after victory in war!

★ ★ ★

“For the king he marched with the army
to ancient Vatapi in the northern land,
reduced the old city to dust,
subdued the long-armed mountain-like foe,

and seized countless gems,
heaps of treasure,
ornamented elephants
and herds of horses,

bringing all these
before his lord.”

(*Periya Puranam* — Sekkizhar)
