## Item 5 — We Need a Heart of Gold; We Need the Love It Gives!

### English translation

<!-- scan 25 -->

Today we take up a sheet,
fill the pen with ink,

and paint in marvellous detail
the condition in which early man lived
six thousand years ago —

what he ate, how he slept,
all that he experienced.

Calling a thousand facilities and opportunities to our aid,
we conduct our research;

then announce its conclusions with ease.
But Tolkappiyar, older even than our master Valluvar —

more than two thousand years ago,
did he not compose the *Tolkappiyam*
with a stylus?

That incomparable grammar of life,
created upon palm-leaf,
begins by saying that a one-sensed life-form
knows through the body,

and ends by saying that six-sensed knowledge
is that which can be known through the mind —
explaining every class of life
and how many senses each possesses.

That great epic,
that painted grammar of life,
that poem which begins with tree, plant and creeper
and speaks onward into human history —

in this earth, a mountain;
on that mountain, a peak —
he who gave us that stainless grammar of tradition,

<!-- scan 26 -->

I would say that kneeling before him,
worshipping and paying homage,
is a mighty duty to be performed
in the very midst of drawing human history!

Six thousand years ago,
man had no clothes,
no power to prepare food of the six tastes,

no sense of love, romance, affection or duty,
no ordered bonds among bodies wrapped in bone and skin,

no habit of thinking about refinement or conduct;
what he saw became the sight,
what he seized became his style of life.

He wandered over forest and hill,
and made friendship too with goat and cattle.

Whenever hunger struck,
he learned to pluck whatever he saw along the path
and eat what he had plucked;

so even if he saw the “magnet stone,”
and even if that stone itself came toward him at first sight,

like owl and horned owl,
each would seek out
whatever prey each could find.

After that — who was he? who was she? ........
who would sing *aararo*
for one yet to be born? ........

So it is in one of Rahul Sankrityayan's stories:
when the beautiful enchantress Diva
and her desirous lover Sur

embrace in delight, drink from each other's lips
and lie there forgetting themselves,
she says:
“My beloved, you are my mother's son!
Even so, forgive me
for not having come close to you until now.”

“Enough — from now on,
do not feel shy with me,” he says;
and afterwards they —

<!-- scan 27 -->

became flood mingling with flood,
veena and its resonance —

then moved apart,
and later each ran off
to play a different veena.

A life slipping along with no bounds,
the custom of the age of gesture
before spoken language —

showing by facial sign
“No,” “You must not,”
and “I cannot desire this” —

that mime-language of performance
was the speech-language of that day.
There is a whole history behind
word-language later rising into sound.

When struck, crying “Ah!” “Oo!” —
calling to one far away, “Come!”;

in fear, trembling and screaming “Ayyo!” —
in overwhelming joy, laughing “Aav!” —

if these took second place and became musical sound,
then *iyal*, *isai* and *koothu*
are surely the basic grammar of every language.

In time, the dominion of the tongue
made even the language of gesture
a minister without portfolio,

and, in keeping with our master's command,
“Whatever else you fail to guard, guard the tongue,”
it seems a rapid-action guard was set
for the kingdom of the tongue!

For the tongue that opened the road
to the heights of civilization to begin speaking,
the advance that arose forty thousand years ago
was, they say, the cause.

Man, who ate raw flesh to appease hunger,
wanting to taste his food,
learned to kindle fire and cook it.

<!-- scan 28 -->

Only by hunting and killing animals
could he find a way to manage the hunger of the belly —
and even in that hunt,

competition, jealousy, war — new problems one after another;
these clashes that sprouted in the days
of our grandmother and great-grandmother

have not stopped even in the days
of granddaughter and great-grandchild.....
The counsel of the *Aathichudi*,
“Let anger cool,”

enters no one's ears;
why that is, I do not know.

Rather than the people of this age,
who have grown accustomed to a life of enmity,

the people of that age,
who moved uncivilized through cave-life,
seem in many ways better —

so that such a thought
need not arise,

the heart of gold that the earth possesses
must be possessed in greater measure
by woman and man;

that heart, though rare to obtain,

will create love —
will cultivate peace!

Another story will branch out;
its seed will sprout tomorrow!

## Item 6 — The Knife Belongs to the Enemy; The Blood Is What We Give!

### English translation

<!-- scan 29 -->

Whether Stone Age man wished
to learn many new things or not,

Nature herself stepped forward,
like a mother feeding milk to her child,
and taught him many things.

He killed animals for food,
ate their flesh,
and wore the flayed skin as clothing —

was that not truly the result
of a command unfolded by his brain?
And for him to make the tools
with which to live by force,

was it not Nature's own kindness
that told him, “Take iron and stone”?

Without lifting even a splinter,
letting the belly grow,
living as income pours in —
that paradise of landed domination;

if today there is a poor working class
rising with a roar to make it weep,
though there was none then,
the reason is surely that here

there sounds the cry of an intellectual revolution
that breaks the condition of slavery!

To the poor and ruined who asked,
“Why? For what? How?”
and were told by a crowd of scoundrels,
“It is all God's command” —

because Bharathidasan,
king above kings among poets,
beat the war-drum of verse,
calling for the worker to be set against
the binding knot called God —

<!-- scan 30 -->

we now see softness and humility
in the mansions of the very wealthy!

Because kindness and culture
could be found at the beginning of the Stone Age,
men made battlefields and fought
in forests, on hills and uplands!

Even if one says the Golden Age was better,
the support of wicked lords and great men
must be searched for only with a magnifying glass;

and to eyes wearing a Vedic lens,
not even drops of sweat
can ever be seen.

Festivals, spectacles,
all kinds of amusements —
a condition that had found unity in difference
changed;

because difference and division
grew swiftly,
hostility put down roots
and sent out shoots as a poisonous tree.

They hacked at one another,
struck one another with stones,
and by the hundreds, according to the count,
fell as victims on both sides;

all the possessions of both sides
lay wasted, unusable.

In the darkness of folly,
the warriors left standing on both sides
lit the lamp of death,
knelt and touched the earth,
and began to pay silent homage
to the martyrs of heroic death!

As I write this,
a passage from Pandit Nehru's
book of world history,
which I have read many times,

shadows itself across my heart
and stirs many memories.

Here — Nehru says:

“If we look closely at history,
we can understand the evolutionary process
by which this world has grown and changed —
‘as grass, as shrub, as worm, as tree,
as many forms of vegetation,
as bird, as snake,

<!-- scan 31 -->

as stone, as human’.”

He calls Manikkavasagar's *Tiruvachakam* itself
as evidence and witness;

and Pandit Nehru — the Light of Asia —
after praising the power
of human reason,
says in that same book:

“If, as we saw in the World War,
what we call progress means
united brothers destroying one another,
then there is some fundamental error
in that progress.”

What medicine can possibly cure
the anxiety of people like him
who say this?

Carrying a bowl of medicine,
a young woman came there —

I can say firmly:
she was not that beautiful enchantress,
the “magnet stone”!

With her eyes opened wide,
she walked toward the wounded,

placing each step so gently it might ache,
her lifted foot almost blistering —
slowly she walked.....very slowly.

She turned over one or two bodies and looked.
Except for the bodies of warriors who had fled,

all the others lay fallen
as honour-filled warriors
welcoming death.

The young woman walked with the medicine bowl —
an upright walk;
if you call it a majestic stride,
a proud stride —

<!-- scan 32 -->

all that tract of land,
registered in the names of many,
had been swallowed by that sinful landlord;

he had taken a heap of money,
distributed portions,
and belched in satisfaction.

To hide the sin he had committed,
he babbled like a madman:
“I shall perform sacrifices and yogas,”

“I shall win; I have no fear.”

What should have become the possession
of those who had the right to it,
men of fox-like nature
made their own property,

leaving many good people
in the middle of the street.
Cursing him — “May he perish!” —

the whole village boiled up;
the Rising Sun too rose.

At the waterside,
as though lying there waiting for her,
was a stone used for washing clothes.

The peahen climbed onto it and said:
“Comrades, lend me your ears;
listen to the ideas I speak!

We are only a few,
people living in the deep forest!

Those who burn with irritation and envy toward us
are, like us, only a few!

If irritation meets irritation,
only more irritation will flare,
and nothing but our ashes will remain.

To provoke both sides
and make them clash
requires not even a drop of intelligence.

<!-- scan 33 -->

The landlord lies asleep
in his bedchamber!
We labouring common folk alone
are travelling toward the graveyard!

From being Stone Age humans,
through the changes of time,

we are gradually becoming refined.
As proof that we have also gained reason,

let all of us unite;
first,
let us take high and low from among ourselves
and throw them into the rubbish bin!

After that,
to turn everything upside down,
let us raise the flag of revolution!
Rise — conduct the uprising!”

Seeing the young woman speak
as though a sluice-gate had suddenly burst open,

wounded and unwounded alike
came and stood there,
mouths agape:

“Ah! What a wonder!
You speak so beautifully —
how can you do it?”

When everyone asked,
she answered:

“I did not learn to speak
only for myself —
I learned to speak
so I could teach everyone to speak.”

Until then,
a warrior who had lain unmoving
under cover of darkness,
with an honour-wound upon his chest,

woke and asked:
“Do you know me, woman?”

<!-- scan 34 -->

“Why would I not know you?
Are you not my beloved?
Why did you join the enemy's camp?

To look upon traitors is itself betrayal,”
she said, and moved away!

“Woman, forgive me.
I came to understand the enemies,
and then returned here itself;
do you not know?

I am dying now
because the enemy's arrow
has pierced my chest;
look, my dear!” he said.

Crying “Ayyo!”
she embraced his body.

At the edge of death he asked:
“My beautiful statue!
When you say ‘revolution,’ when is it?
When you say ‘uprising,’ how is it?”

“My dear, what I spoke of
was not a revolution that breeds riot;
it was a cultural revolution!

Not an uprising that conducts a fight;
a social-justice uprising!
Everything only through the path of righteousness!” she said.

The dying man smiled and said:
“A war without knife, without blood —
that is it, is it not?”

“Yes — the knife is what the enemy brings;
the blood is what we give —
that is the righteous struggle!” she said.

Still embracing her,
his life went out.

## Translator notes

- Item 5 title `தங்க மனம்` is rendered **heart of gold**, preserving the familiar moral image that the poem returns to near its close.
- `தொல்காப்பியம்` remains *Tolkappiyam* and `ஓரறிவு` / `ஆறறிவு` are translated through the source's own sensory hierarchy rather than recast as a modern scientific taxonomy.
- The relationship episode involving Diva and Sur is translated as the source presents it; the English does not silently repair or explain the kinship claim.
- `ஆராரோ` is retained as **aararo**, because it is the culturally recognizable lullaby refrain and carries the source's sound-play with the unanswered identity questions.
- `இயல்`, `இசை`, `கூத்து` remain **iyal, isai, koothu** where the source is arguing for a developmental ordering of expressive forms.
- The Kural phrase `யாகா வாராயினும் நா காக்க` is rendered in sense as **“Whatever else you fail to guard, guard the tongue”**; the surrounding tongue/kingdom/guard wordplay is kept active rather than flattened.
- `ஆத்திசூடி` remains *Aathichudi* and `ஆறுவது சினம்` is rendered **“Let anger cool.”**
- Item 6 retains the source's class vocabulary — landed domination, poor working class, slavery, revolution — without replacing it with neutral sociological prose.
- The compact source phrase `கடவுள் என்ற கட்டுறுத்துத் தொழிலாளரை ஏவுக` is translated cautiously as **calling for the worker to be set against the binding knot called God**. The Tamil is final-cleared and is not silently normalized to a different Bharathidasan wording.
- The source's Manikkavasagar/*Tiruvachakam* passage and Nehru quotation are represented as quotations within Kalaignar's poem; the translation does not fact-check or harmonize them with external editions.
- `உதயசூரியன்` is rendered **Rising Sun**, retaining its literal image and political resonance without adding an explanatory gloss into the verse.
- `விழுப்புண்` is rendered **honour-wound**, preserving the heroic ethical charge of the Tamil rather than reducing it to merely “injury.”
- The closing contrast between `கலகம்`/`கலாச்சாரப் புரட்சி`, `சண்டை`/`சமூக நீதிக் கிளர்ச்சி`, and `அறவழி` is kept explicit. `அறப்போர்` is rendered **righteous struggle**, not as generic “war.”
