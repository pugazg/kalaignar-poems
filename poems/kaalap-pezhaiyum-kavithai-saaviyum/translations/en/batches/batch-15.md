## Item 40 — The Tenderness and Compassion Shown by the Soil of Kanchi

### English translation

<!-- scan 195 -->

The Islamic prophet Muhammad,
revered as one sent by God,

went from Mecca to Medina in CE 622;
that is said to be the “Hijra”!

It was in that same period here,
says Mayilai Seeni Venkatasami,
who wrote *The History of Mahendra*,

that the saint Tirunavukkarasar,
known as Appar,
and Mahendravarman lived.

By making that comparison
and calculating the age
of the Pallava king of Kanchi,
he gives all of us
something to rejoice in.

What were the boundaries
of the country of Narasimha Pallava,
son of that Mahendravarman Pallava?

To the north was Chalukya rule;
to the south,
Pandya governance
along the banks of the Vaigai;

to the west lay Chera land —

history draws the picture
of his realm lying
in the midst of these.

Determined that Pallava rule
must not grow strong,
must not strike deep roots,

the domination-mad Chalukya Pulikesi
invaded again and again.

Narasimhan rose roaring
to put an end
to that arrogance —

<!-- scan 196 -->

with strength of shoulder
and skill in war,
he made Pulikesi turn his back.

The Kanchi Pallava army
chased him as far as Vatapi;

and the Pallava made Pulikesi understand as well
the power of his commander Paranjothi.

The Pallava emblem
was beautifully installed
in a temple at Vatapi,

and became a historical sign
that, the source says,
can be seen even today!

Inscriptions praising
Narasimhavarman's famed victory say:

“the Pallava king
who fought many hundreds of battles
and wrote his victory
upon the back of Pulikesi,

who fled defeated
from the fields of Pariyal,
Manimangalam and Suramaram.”

The Velur, Kuram
and Chandramangalam charters
praise him in wonder;

history beats the drum of it!

Because Professor Dharmapala of Kanchi
worked at Nalanda University,

learned people of Kanchi,
the source says,
still take great joy and pride in it.

Nor did the Chinese traveller
Yuan Suwang forget to note

that many Digambara Jains
lived and flourished
in Kanchipuram of the Dravidian region,

or that Gautama Buddha himself
came to Kanchi
and made hundreds into Buddhists.

Narasimhavarman too
was not one to abandon
dear friends.

There was a minor ruler in Lanka
named Manavarman.

<!-- scan 197 -->

Enemies defeated him,
set him and his family adrift
in a small boat upon the Indian Ocean,
and drove him from his country!

Broken-hearted Manavarman,
trusting that his friend Narasimhan,
though not bound to him by kinship,
possessed the sweet nature
that gives honour to friendship,

steered his boat
toward the port of Mamallapuram.

“Will my friend Narasimhan
receive me with a twisted face?”
asked his heart.

When Manavarman arrived,
he was exhausted by astonishment —
overcome with wonder!

Narasimhan became an elder brother
to him and his wife,

welcomed them into his palace
as though they were members
of his own family,

and gave them every ornament.

“Brother dear as life!
I have stretched out a supporting hand
to give whatever must be given.

Ask what you need
so that in Lanka
you may defeat your enemies
and wear the crown again.

Do not hesitate;
I shall give without refusal,”
said the Pallava.

“O warrior who heaps victories
upon the sword-point!

Through the intrigue
of one with the heart of a scorpion
who pretended to be my friend,

I lost my land,
I lost my palace.

I have come asking for the gift
of your support,
so that Manavarman may live
without losing his honour,”
said the friend from Lanka.

<!-- scan 198 -->

“Take as many soldiers
as you need.

Here is commander Paranjothi,
whose heart is like steel;

he will provide
everything necessary,”
said the Pallava,
turning his gaze toward Paranjothi.

“I am ready
to send the force
that your friend requires,”
said Paranjothi with a nod,
and took leave.

★ ★ ★

With the Kanchi army,
Manavarman set out eagerly for battle;

Narasimhan embraced his friend
with affection
and sent him on his way.

In the Lankan war,
victory went to the enemies.

Heartbroken,
the dear friend returned
to Kanchi.

Narasimhan studied clearly
the cause of the defeat,

taught him the art
of how the strategy
must now be shaped,

and happily left
for the Mamallai port
to send Manavarman off again.

There a shock awaited them:

“Without King Narasimhan,
we will not board the ship!”

The soldiers,
all with one voice,
refused.

“Only if the Pallava sovereign himself
comes with us to Lanka
will we board,”
they pleaded.

Seeing Manavarman's distress,
Narasimhavarman calmed him:
“Do not fear.”

Then Narasimhavarman himself
boarded that ship.

With the cry
“Long live the king!”
the Kanchi army
began its sea journey.

After travelling
a little distance over the sea,

Narasimhan covered his body
with a blanket —

<!-- scan 199 -->

and by another way from the ship,
the guardian of Kanchi
climbed down into a boat!

His army went on
to reach Lanka.

With the confidence
that the king was still among them,

the great warriors encircled
the enemy army.

The result:
a garland of victory
for Manavarman!

The enemies died
on the battlefield.

Narasimhan,
king of kings of Mamallai,

protected his friend,
protected his friend's country,

and through this act
made the pure friendship
between the two
into a fine sight to behold.

For those who think
that rulers of the earth
have no heart at all —

and that even if they do,
no humaneness can sprout within it —

Narasimhan's restoration
of Manavarman to kingship
stood as an answer
that strikes them on the head.

“All this is the tenderness
and compassion shown by Kanchi,”

Manavarman wrote in a letter,
praising Narasimhan,
the good man of friendship:

“Your father hollowed rock
and discovered halls;

you hollow out enemy country
and plant pillars of victory,

and so the minds of our people
are cooled
as though touched by a chill wind!”

The king rejoiced
on reading the sweet letter;

yet he did not grow arrogant.

## Item 41 — Let Us Protect It: the Pallava Capital!

Source metadata:

- stable item: **41**
- canonical Tamil title: `பாதுகாப்போம்; பல்லவர் தலைநகரம்!`
- contents witness: `பாதுகாப்போம்; பல்லவர் தலைநகரம்!`
- canonical file: `../../../sections/41.md`
- physical scans: **200–203**
- printed pages: **199–202**
- title-witness status: **contents/title-page match**

### English translation

<!-- scan 200 -->

When I read
*History of the Pallavas*,

a textbook written
by Dr N. K. Mangalamurugesan,
who has written textbooks
for university students,

many of which are themselves
prescribed for students,

I was greatly astonished
to find him arguing strongly
that “Kanchi was not the capital”
of Pallava rule.

When I spoke about it
with Professor Anbazhagan,
he said:

“Rather than saying
Kanchipuram was not
the capital of the Pallavas,

why not simply say
there were no Pallavas,
and no Kanchipuram either!”

So that the idea
that Kanchi was never
the Pallava capital
should not spread among students,

I offer what I learned
while searching for evidence.

The scholar Mayilai Thiru Seeni Venkatasami,
skilled in researching
and writing history,

reminds us that
this is no story:

“Simhavishnu,
father of Mahendravarman,
ruled the Pallava country
with Kanchi as his capital.”

The Kasakudi and Velurpalayam
copper-plate charters,
he reminds us,
stand as evidence
explaining these events.

<!-- scan 201 -->

Pullalur,
where the Pallavas fought Pulikesi,
was in Chengai district,
only fifteen *kal* north of Kanchi.

The rock sculptures,
charming cave temples
and inscriptions
of the ages of Mahendra and Narasimha

are found at Mamallapuram,
Pallavapuram,
Mamandur,
Singavaram,
Mandagapattu,
Mahendravadi,
Tirukazhukundram
and Talavanur.

Mangala Murugesan himself
points to these
as identifying marks
of Pallava kings.

Then there is no need
for him himself
to deny Kanchi as the capital —

nor any need
to shift that capital
into Andhra country!

When the Chinese traveller
Yuan Suwang's account
speaks of the distance
between Kanchi and the seashore,

if we conceal and deny that,
saying there is no sea near Kanchi,

and speak after forgetting Mamallapuram,
how can our hearts be glad?

“Could Mahendravarman
have failed to notice
‘Mahabali’puram near Kanchi?

The Dharmaraja Mandapam
and Kodikkal Mandapam there
belong to Mahendra's age.”

Can we neglect
the research of Dr R. Rajamanickanar
that says so?

Can there be doubt
that a Pallava king
built them?

Mahendravarman,
after changing religion
and turning to Saivism,

wrote the comic play
*Matta Vilasam*,
had it performed,

<!-- scan 202 -->

and mocked Jainism and Buddhism.

In that religious-propaganda play,
there is reference
to clouds crawling
across the glorious towers of Kanchi;

and Mayilai Venkatasami explains
that it joins with Appar's sacred utterance:

“Karumugil thavazhumada-k Kacchi yegam panre.”

Can that explanation
simply be cast aside?

Even leaving all else apart,
one question arises:

how did one of Mahendra's honorifics,
“Chitrakara Puli,”
come to be inscribed
on a mandapam pillar
in the Ekambareswarar temple
at Kanchi?

The emblems of Pallava rule
and the many art treasures
they cherished
spread in many directions;

yet so many of the evidences
can be seen
around Kanchi and beside it.

To uproot that
and plant it in Andhra
is futile work,

and an act
that needlessly stains
the fame of Kanchi.

At a time when
true histories of Tamil Nadu
have been screened off
from the eyes of the world,

may this explanation
of this part of Pallava history
help prevent this
from becoming yet another screen!

★ ★ ★

<!-- scan 203 -->

The friend who argues strongly
that “Kanchi was not the capital”

has also written:

“The inscription
of Narasinga Pottaraiyan,
conqueror of Vatapi,

in the rock-cut temple
at Tirukazhukundram,
is considered to belong
to the age of that Pallava king.”

That is worth noticing;

it is evidence fit
to make one reconsider
what one had thought!

## Item 42 — The Charters Proclaim It!

- canonical Tamil title: `பட்டயங்கள், பறைசாற்றுகின்றன!`

### English translation

<!-- scan 204 -->

After the fall of Vatapi,
King Pulikesi too,
having lost his strength,
fell.

Then Vikramaditya,
of his line,
rose with force!

Burning to crush
and destroy the Pallavas of Kanchi,

he declared
that he needed no help
from any Chera, Chola
or Pandya minor ruler,

and with a vast army
of countless warriors
swept like a cyclone
upon Pallava country.

Seeing this,
Pandya Arikesari Nedumaran
was enraged.

Though the Chera and Chola
were minor rulers,
he embraced them into alliance,

and together,
with fire-spitting armies
arrayed at the battlefront,

met the Chalukya Vikramaditya
on the field of Nelveli.

Unable to withstand them,
without even turning back
toward the Pallava frontier,

he fled in fear and hid,
running all the way
to Vatapi as though crying,
“I am coming, I am coming!”

In wonder at it,
Sekkizhar sang congratulations
for the victory won
by the Tamil alliance!

<!-- scan 205 -->

Many researchers,
digging out what they present as truth
for all to understand,
say

that the battle of Nelveli
was in CE 675,

that Paramesvaravarman I
was then the Pallava king,

and that if only Vikramaditya
had not possessed such swagger,
he would not have fallen.

Whether defeat or victory,
there is a cause behind it;

Vikramaditya himself
stands as an example
that empty arrogance
can bring down even a warrior!

★ ★ ★

Vikramaditya,
who had fled defeated,
unable to soothe
the pain of his wound of defeat,

continued in that same year
with a great army
against the capital Kanchi.

The Pallava army
was shaken
by the sudden attack.

The multilingual scholar says
that the Kadwal, Sor and Kendur charters
report as historical evidence
the victory the Chalukya king
won at Kanchi.

They say that Vikramaditya,
riding his royal horse,
held a victory procession
inside the Pallava's Kanchi,

and made Paramesvaravarman,
grandson of Narasimha Mamallan,
warrior among warriors,
bow at his feet.

So says the charter
created by the enemy.

Unable to bear
the way the Chalukya,
intoxicated by his victory,
danced without knowing
head from foot,

all the Tamil kings
gathered together once again.

Their armies set out
toward the battlefield
of Peruvalanallur!

<!-- scan 206 -->

If even seven or eight mice
join together,
a cat will struggle
to face them —

and here,
if tigers themselves unite,
this earth too
will know their strength!

Through the unity
of the rulers of Tamil Nadu,

the Chalukya army
was brought down
to the point of surrender!

With a pure heart,
the Pallava king
thanked every Tamil ruler
who had come to his aid:

“Paramesvaravarman,
riding in procession
upon his royal elephant
called ‘Arivaranam’,

threw the enemy soldiers
into confusion
in the streets of Kanchi.

Vikramaditya,
covering himself
with a ragged cloth,

crossed many streets
and ran — ran —

his feet striking
against the back of his head,

until he fled
beyond the boundary
of Kanchi city.”

(The victory charter
expressing this idea,
known as the “Kuram Charter,”
beautifully describes
in stacked phrases
the outcome
of the Peruvalanallur battle.)

Is that all?

(The Chalukyas' “Kendur” charter,
the Pandyas' “Chinnamanur” copper plate,
and Sundaramurthi Nayanar's *Tevaram*

also proclaim these
as historical evidences;

they raise the victory flag
for the Pallava triumph.)

## Translator notes

- Item 40 preserves the title witnesses separately: the canonical/title-page witness has no final exclamation mark, while the contents witness does. The English displayed title therefore carries no added final exclamation mark.
- Item 40 retains the source's Hijra/Appar/Mahendravarman chronological comparison, inscriptional claims, Dharmapala/Nalanda and Yuan Suwang passages, and the Manavarman–Narasimhavarman friendship narrative as source-presented history. No outside historical reconciliation has been inserted.
- The Manavarman episode preserves its full dramatic progression: exile by boat, palace welcome, first military expedition and defeat, strategic retraining, soldiers' refusal to sail without Narasimhan, the king's boarding and concealed departure, victory in Lanka, and Manavarman's concluding letter.
- Item 41 preserves the argument with the textbook author, Professor Anbazhagan's retort, the listed Pallava sites, Mahabalipuram/Mamallapuram argument, *Matta Vilasam* reference, Appar quotation, `சித்திர காரப் புலி` title and the concluding Tirukazhukundram inscription as distinct evidentiary moves. Claims remain attributed to the source rather than independently adjudicated.
- `கல்` in the source's distance statement is retained as *kal* rather than silently converting it into a modern unit, because the canonical Tamil does not itself provide a conversion.
- Item 42 preserves both battle movements presented by the source: the Nelveli defeat of Vikramaditya and the later Kanchi/Peruvalanallur sequence. The hostile-charter perspective and the later Pallava victory-charter perspective remain separately visible.
- The mouse/cat and united-tigers analogy, repeated `ஓடினான் ; ஓடினான்`, the royal elephant `அரிவாரணம்`, the Kuram Charter parenthesis and the final Kendur/Chinnamanur/*Tevaram* parenthesis are all retained as source architecture rather than compressed into summary prose.
