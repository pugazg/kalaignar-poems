## Item 19 — A Historical Event to Grieve Over!

### English translation

<!-- scan 99 -->

That Senguttuvan was a son born of the Chola line,
and that his mother
was Narchonai, daughter of Karikala Chola —
this is what *Silappathikaram* tells us.

What does the *Pathitrupathu* decade say?
That the maiden's name
was Manakkilli, daughter of the Chola.

Since it is natural enough
for one person to bear two names,
there is no need to magnify this
and become confused.

Greater confusions than this
raise their heads in Tamil history.
There are three —
first, second and third Karikalas;

and the victories won by those three
collide with one another
until we struggle to know
which victory belonged to whom!

As though there were another Kuttuvan,
there is one called Velkezhu Kuttuvan;
yet scholars say he is no other man —
only another name
for Cheran Senguttuvan himself!

In England, from Henry the First
to Henry the Eighth,
however many Henrys may come,
time and history remain exact.

Here, because literature and inscriptions
are what have come down to us as history,
I would say that only as far as possible
have we been able to research
and fix the dates with certainty!

<!-- scan 100 -->

The peerless warrior who went to the Himalaya and won;
the Chera who, whenever the occasion came,
added glory to the Tamils;

the great king who held honour alone
to be the good life,
the valiant Tamil who stood like a great mountain
to guard his people —

“there is none who can defeat him,” people said;
“whoever comes against him will fall,”
the words rang out;

and as Senguttuvan's fame spread everywhere
like a full, resounding note,
some kings who ruled the earth
boiled over with jealousy!

Like embers that refuse to die,
jealousy and unbearable irritation —
seeing a Tamil's greatness,
these arise in another Tamil himself;

and among those kings,
one who sprang up so
was Palaiyan of Mogur.

To finish his enmity
and send the Chera to the burial pit,
Palaiyan made new plans,
wove wicked plots,
prepared for war,
and had the war-drum beaten.

On the frontier of his country
was a chieftain called Arugan;
thinking the time had come,
he leapt upon Palaiyan
in support of the Chera.

Palaiyan's anger against Arugan
was a thousand times fiercer
than even his anger against the Chera;
he rose first to destroy him.

Escaping that assault,
the chieftain Arugan resolved
to bring Palaiyan down
and throw an unforgettable challenge
at his honour;
he joined the Chera army
and broke Palaiyan's fort!

Senguttuvan, who appeared
like a lion-bull at the battle-front,
shattered Mogur and roared —

<!-- scan 101 -->

“Wherever you may be,
I shall not let you go!”
He surged forward
and captured Palaiyan too.

He cut down completely
the neem that stood
as Mogur's guardian tree;

and, ordering the elephants of Mogur,
now bound under his rule,
to drag away the felled tree,
that incomparable Tamil king
returned to his capital.

As soon as he returned,
Arugan moved to fulfil his own desire —
to settle his score with Palaiyan.

To make a hauling-rope
for tying and dragging the guardian tree,
he summoned the warriors
who obeyed his commands;

he ordered the hair of the women
of Palaiyan's palace to be cut,
twisted into a rope,

and told them to bind the guardian tree
with those ropes and drag it.

Hearing this,
the Chera spat fire at Arugan:
a stain had fallen
upon the victory he had won.

“Using my support, Arugan,
you have settled your own enmity.

To show the anger you bore toward Palaiyan,
you sent forces
and took your revenge;
yet —

to bind and drag
the guardian tree of an enemy land,
against all culture,

you ordered the hair
of the women of Palaiyan's family to be cut.
You may call it an act of revenge —

<!-- scan 102 -->

No! No!
A base act!
A base act, I call it!”
said the Chera.

He was deeply troubled;
not only the guardian tree —

he released Palaiyan too
from captivity,
and only then eased his grief.

Among the Tamils of the Dravidian people
there was valour, there was wisdom,
there was ability whose fame scented all eight directions;

there was the force of a storming tiger in war,
and an honour that would never show its back
to an enemy!

There was fearlessness
that never left them for even an instant,
meeting death in battle
with a spear borne in the chest;

there was a summit of devotion to art
that crossed the surging seas;
there was the strength of principle
that would not let its stand collapse —

but,
but,
alas —
among the Tamils,
that one thing alone,
unity of the people,
was absent.

Is that not why
this event of history
must make us grieve so deeply!

## Item 20 — Even in Falling, He Is Victory's Favoured Son!

- canonical file: `../../../sections/20.md`

### English translation

<!-- scan 103 -->

Think upon the battle of Venni,
which stands as an example
of both valour and honour —

and the nerves of warriors will tremble
like the strings of a plucked veena;

the heart will heave;
both eyes will become waterfalls.

Between the great warrior
Karikala Chola
and the king called Cheralathan,

at Koyil Venni,
a Tamil town near Thanjavur,
was fought a battle

where countless corpses fell;
the sturdy Cheras and Cholas
stood face to face,

both sides bearing spear and sword,
and fought in that field of blood.

The spear flung by Karikalan
flew and, in the blink of an eye,

pierced the Chera's chest,
bathed in the mire of red blood,

and showed its point through his back,
ending the warrior-king's life.

<!-- scan 104 -->

I will not wholeheartedly say,
“The Chera king died.”
His honour was broken, he felt,
and without asking an exception
to Tamil custom,

he decided to give up his life
by *vadakkiruthal* — sitting facing north;
that is why I spoke as I did.

“I shall take spear-shaft and sword
and hasten to sit facing north,” he said.

It was not a wound gained
by turning his back in battle;
even the wound where the spear-tip
had merely emerged through his back,

he imagined as a disgrace
that had befallen him in war;
without consoling himself
that it was a warrior's honour-wound,
without taking pride in it,

the Chera fell into death
through *vadakkiruthal* —
and, poet Kazhathalaiyar sang,
he clothed himself in fame.

What was that poem?

“The war-drum forgot to sound —
the yaazh forgot to sing —
the milk-pots stand dry,
without milk —
no one touches the honey —

no farmer goes to the field —
the open spaces around the town
have all become desolate —

because our Chera king,
to guard his honour,
has gone to sit facing north
and give up his life!”

★

“The earth-drum forgot,
the tuned yaazh forgot;
the broad-mouthed pot lay open
and forgot its flowing ghee;
the circle forgot the bee-thronged liquor —

<!-- scan 105 -->

the farmers' clamour forgot,
and the festival too;
in the wide town the gathering crowds forgot;
ashamed of the wound upon his back,
the king of martial worth
sat facing north with his sword.”

(*Purananuru* 65 — Kazhathalaiyar)

As Kazhathalaiyar sang this poem of mourning,
a woman poet born at Venni,
where the battle took place —
Vennikkuyathiyar —

even while praising Karikalan's victory,
sang:

“Descendant of the mighty one
who drove ships upon the dense threefold sea
and mastered the labour of the wind!
Karikala Valava,
whose elephants move in exultation!

Victor — your might shone
when you crossed the battle you entered;
yet is not he better even than you —
he who, at clamorous, fertile Venni's battle-field,
reached the fame of the whole world,
and, ashamed of his back-wound,
sat facing north?”

(*Purananuru* 66 — Vennikkuyathiyar)

Thus Kuyathiyar says:
Karikalan won and gained fame;
Cheralathan sat facing north
and guarded his honour —

and she places a garland of poetic pearls
upon them both.

Like fire, this news
spread throughout the sacred land;

from all eight directions
the learned gathered.

To die together
with the Chera king
who had fought like a lion and fallen,

<!-- scan 106 -->

they hastened,
wishing to renounce life
through *vadakkiruthal*:

“Cheralathan, who fought
Karikala Valavan at Venni Parandhalai,
ashamed of the wound,
sat facing north with his sword
beside the surging waters;

hearing that grievous, grievous word,
the learned, desiring to go with him
to the world so hard to attain,
parted in a great separation.”

(*Akananuru* 55 — Mamulanar)

On one side,
joy at Karikalan's victory;
on the other,
heart-sorrow at the sense of honour
that made the Chera king decide
to sit facing north.

Those who fell in battle
and those who won —
both were worthy of praise;

what can possibly be shown
as equal or parallel
to the broad hearts
of the poets
who garlanded both with fame!

## Item 21 — Where Culture Is Maimed, They Would Not Even Wish to Look!

Source metadata:

- stable item: **21**
- canonical Tamil title: `பண்பாட்டுக்கு ஊனம் எனில் பார்க்கவும் விரும்பார்!`
- contents witness: `பண்பாட்டுக்கு ஊனம் எனில் பார்க்கவும் விரும்பார்!`
- canonical file: `../../../sections/21.md`
- physical scans: **107–111**
- printed pages: **106–110**
- title-witness status: **contents/title-page match**

### English translation

<!-- scan 107 -->

While still a sturdy young bull,
he escaped in anguish
with his ankle scorched
in the fire lit by certain wicked enemies;

he became famed for the Kallanai,
and built embankments for the Kaveri;

with Uraiyur and Poompuhar
as two incomparable capitals —

that Chola king was Karikalan.
His daughter,
the beautiful princess Aathimandhiyar,

was herself a queen among poets,
and a great queen of dance
upon the stage.

Nalankilli, Nedunkilli and Mavalathan —
three brothers she had;

though Aathimandhi laboured hard
so that no cloud of civil strife
should form among them after Karikalan,
it was of no use.

Though they took the Tamil way
as their path,
they acted without unity,
walking the way Tamils so often walked.

To make her own path
a path of love,
Aathimandhi, queen of Tamil art,
fell in love with the broad-shouldered Chera,
Aattanathi,
master of the flourishing art of dance.

Because that truth remained locked
inside the palace,
many princes from near and far
came to see her as a prospective bride;

<!-- scan 108 -->

she did not wish even to look
at any one of them.
Beginning with her confidante,
and when Karikalan —
whose fame surpassed the sweep of the sky —
asked whom else she desired,

she recited, as though from memory,
a poem beginning
“Mallaar kuzhiiya,”
whose sense was:

“In festivals where warriors gather and dance,
and in *thunankai* dances
where men embrace courtesans,

I have not seen him.
I too am a woman skilled in the art of dance;
likewise,

the man who captured my heart
so that these bangles loosen upon my arm
is himself a master of that art.”

Hearing this,
Karikalan sent word of the marriage
of Aathimandhi and Aattanathi
to all the kings
and conducted the wedding.

But one or two villains,
who had waited many days,
were stunned as though a cotton pod had burst,
and joined together to devise a plan
to cut their life of love short halfway.

When someone in the house next door,
or the house opposite,
is eating a sweet jackfruit segment,
how can hearts bitter as *etti* fruit
simply stand and watch?

And so,
those whose nature was like a honey-sting
fixed a day
to cut their love apart with a sword;

<!-- scan 109 -->

they chose the day
and did the work of jackals.

After finishing dance practice,
the two lovers danced and rejoiced
until the festival of the Kaveri's fresh flood ended;

later they read together
the midnight bedchamber epic,
and, kissing that thornless rose,

embraced and fell asleep in exhaustion.
Then the plotters bound Aattanathi with rope,
lifted him away;

though Aathimandhi woke and chased them,
they kicked her aside,
and into the Kaveri,
roaring as it ran with an “O!”

they flung him —
like a ball swinging at the end of a rope.

Those nameless, worthless husks
escaped,
leaving the daughter
of the earth-ruling king in torment.

She of the cool, flawless moon-face
ran along the wave-beaten riverbank,

burning for him,
crying “Aththaan! Aththaan!”
until her heart seemed to burst.

He, caught within the rope
in the river's whirl,
thrashed like a splinter
caught in a cyclone.

When the news reached King Karikalan,
he stood bewildered,
not knowing how to console
his precious Aathimandhi.

But she,
calling and calling for her man,
ran along the riverbank —
ran and ran.

She danced a dance of frenzy,
danced and danced;
like a lily-stalk falling into flame,
she withered and withered —

<!-- scan 110 -->

and in that midnight hour,
an old woman who rowed a boat upon the river

hurried in distress
to the maiden Aathimandhi,
who had collapsed upon the bank,
and turned her face to see.

Lightning struck — “paleer! paleer!”
Thunder whirled and cracked — “suleer!” —
like a lash of the whip.

To Aathimandhi,
who opened her eyes only halfway,
the old woman said:

“Are you not, child,
the one who sang to my granddaughter,
your friend Nilavazhagi,
when so many came to see you as a bride:

‘Even in the festival where wrestlers gather,
even in the *thunankai* where women embrace,
nowhere have I seen the man worthy of me;
I too am a woman of the dancing-floor,
and the lord of high worth
who loosened the bright paired bangles on my arm
is himself a man of the dancing-floor.’?”

Hearing the old woman recite
her own poem,
her own pure Tamil song,
without rejecting a word,
Aathimandhi marvelled;

she sobbed,
and the tears poured from her eyes —
one might say they conquered the Kaveri itself.

Filled with compassion,
the old boatwoman said affectionately
that her own name was “Kaveri”;

a smile blossomed
in her toothless, hollow mouth,
and she told them
that she had safely sheltered Aattanathi
in her hut —

<!-- scan 111 -->

news that filled everyone nearby with joy.

She handed Aattanathi,
son-in-law of world-ruling Karikalan,
back to Aathimandhi,
who ruled through Tamil song.

The two danced in joy,
and sang in reverence
to the old boatwoman.

Aattanathi–Aathimandhi
is the very epic that declares
that art and love
were already a settled culture
among the Tamils of that age!

★     ★     ★

**Note:** Aathimandhiyar and Aattanathi
were not merely people of good conduct;
our culture is such that
where even that conduct would be maimed,
they would not wish even to look.

## Translator notes

- Item 19 retains the source's distinction between Senguttuvan's victory and Arugan's retaliatory act. The cutting of the palace women's hair is translated as the moral violation condemned by Senguttuvan; no attempt is made to soften the source's judgement `இழி செயல்`.
- `காவல் மரம்` is rendered **guardian tree**, preserving its political/ritual function in the narrative rather than reducing it to an ordinary tree.
- Item 20 retains `வடக்கிருந்து` / `வாள்வடக் கிருந்தனன்` through the term ***vadakkiruthal*** with an immediate sense-gloss (“sitting facing north”), because the practice is central to the poem's honour argument and should remain culturally traceable.
- The *Purananuru* 65, *Purananuru* 66 and *Akananuru* 55 passages are rendered for sense and rhetorical continuity; they remain visibly attributed to the source poems and poets and are not presented as independent critical editions.
- Item 20 preserves the distinction between the spear entering Cheralathan's chest and emerging through his back, and the poem's argument that he nevertheless treated the back-wound as a matter of honour.
- Item 21 preserves the Aathimandhi–Aattanathi dance/love narrative, the quoted `மள்ளர் குழீஇய...` poem, the river-rescue turn, the Kaveri/Kaveri name resonance, and the source's closing cultural note.
- `துணங்கைக் கூத்து` is retained as ***thunankai*** where the cultural form matters, with the source's own contextual description kept in the surrounding line.
- `அத்தான்` is retained as **Aththaan** in Aathimandhi's cry because its intimate kinship/love register has no exact single-word English equivalent without flattening the emotional tone.
- The similes built from jackfruit segment / bitter *etti* fruit, honey-sting, thornless rose, cyclone-splinter, lightning and whip-crack are preserved rather than rationalized into plain narrative prose.
- No historical, literary or genealogical claim in this batch has been corrected through outside knowledge; the English translates the final-cleared Tamil source as it stands.
