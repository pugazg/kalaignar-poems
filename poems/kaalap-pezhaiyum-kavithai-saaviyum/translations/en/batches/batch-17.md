### English translation

<!-- scan 220 -->

The Three-Crowned Chola
who stood as a model!

The age of Rajaraja's rule —
an age that showed the way
and opened eyes

for the growth of joyous Tamil,
for the training of ethnic welfare,
for the rise of ethnic consciousness,

for cultural maturity,
for the celebration of ancient glory,
for the flowering of an egalitarian society,
for the happiness of the farmers.

Yet I hesitate
to inscribe it in history
as a golden age.

And when I make that hesitation clear,
when I say that the reasons for it
are as transparent as crystal,

courage and integrity
stand beside us as allies.

At the same time,
I do not wish to diminish
the distinction of those pioneering kings

who brought northern territories
under their sway,
showed their strength upon the seas,

formed a vast realm
from Kumari toward the Himalayan peaks,
established the pride of empire,

and laboured to make it flourish
for three hundred years.

Yet in their victories,
in their proud titles,
without seeing even a fault
smaller than a millet-seed,

like the farmer who forgot
to winnow while the wind was blowing,

when the chance came
they failed to secure
the life of the Tamil people —

<!-- scan 221 -->

hearts that did not think of it,
crippled into inability,
without striving,

the Tamil race itself
brought its progress to an end.

That murmuring sorrow
makes it impossible for me
to praise without reservation

the glory of the empire
and the greatness of the Three-Crowned Chola
who built it.

That is the truth —
and even while writing it,
my hand trembles!

The age of the Chola emperors
was a Sangam age
only in the field of politics;

it was an age
that saw many rare victories.

But in the field of life
it did not reach
the dignity of Sangam times,
the cultural dignity of the people.

Not only historians;
people of self-respect
who cherish the truth of Tamil
have grieved over it.

Into the faultless art,
literature and culture of the Tamils,

as a beetle enters fruit
and makes it impossible for us to eat,

there entered customs
brought back when the Tamil empire,
spreading far and wide,
went northward.

Tamil culture became
fruit bored through by the beetle!

One cruelty alone is enough as example:

was there ever in the Tamil land
the custom of a woman
climbing the funeral pyre
with her dead husband
and burning alive?.......

From the queen of Bootha Pandyan
to Chola queens who ruled the earth,

<!-- scan 222 -->

that contagious disease seized them;
was it not Raja Ram Mohan Roy
who was finally able to give the medicine?

Not the funeral pyre alone:

distinctions of high and low
were taught among human beings
born together,

with the threat
that this was the command of the Vedas;

as the harvest of victory
for those “born from the forehead,”

caste restrictions
and Chaturvedi Mangalams arose,

and dams were built
so that the clear rivers
called Jainism and Buddhism
could not mingle equally.

When history shows us
how these came and made us suffer,
does not anguish itself torment us?

The period of the Chola emperors
is the one age in southern history
able to compete firmly
with the Sangam age.

But politically alone
was it a Sangam age —
an age of many rare victories.

In the field of life,
the Aryan scent had already begun to blow;
there began the age of slipping.

Not historians alone;
all those of self-respect
who honour the truth of the Tamil people

stand grieving and speechless
at the change
that had grown into history.

Ancient or modern?
Rational or not?
What accords with our culture?

Before such things
could be examined and judged —

<!-- scan 223 -->

Rajaraja surpassed
the line of those who had come to the throne.

Many flashes of light
called “reward”
seemed to change form,

becoming a moon of cavalry,
then the high noon sun,

until he stood
as the circle of radiance
in Chola history.

He was not old in years;
nor for that reason merely a youth —
he was adamantine.

If he stood holding a sword,
his heroic form looked
as though a crescent of the sky
had been dipped in black
and fixed upon his face as a moustache.

There was such majesty in that face
that even a kiss could not approach it;

as though the forehead of an elephant
had been taken
and fashioned into a chest full of strength,

as though a mountain itself
had appeared as a man —

such was that handsome male lion.

If amid the clamour of work
he gained even half a *naazhigai*
or a full *naazhigai* of rest,

he would hurry
to see the royal women,
five and six together.

On the way,
if through a latticed window
he heard a woman's voice call
“Arulmozhi” or “Arun,”

he would forget himself
and turn toward the voice.

Grandmother Sembiyan Mahadevi
would embrace her grandson;

beside her,
Kundavai Pirattiyar,
pouring affection upon her younger brother,

would ask after his welfare
and discuss the country's friendships —

all with humility,
love and overflowing concern
between grandmother, sister and king.

“Has Devar still not come?”
he would ask Kundavai.

“I have come!”
Vandiyathevan would laugh
as he entered —

<!-- scan 224 -->

his elder sister's husband,
one of the greatest officers of the state,

winking at Kundavai
as though asking,
“What is it?”

Kundavai and Devan
would move a little aside
and speak in private.

Even while that conversation continued,
a group of women,
full of delight,
would arrive.

And when I say “a group of women,”
it was the group
of King Rajaraja's wives:

Ulaga Mahadevi,
Chola Mahadevi,
Nakkan Tillai Azhagi,
Panchavan Mahadevi,
Trailokya Mahadevi,

Abhimana Valli,
Ilada Mahadevi,
Prithvi Mahadevi,
Meenavan Mahadevi,
Veera Narayani,
Vanamahadevi.

Among them,
the blessing that came to Vanamahadevi
was to be the mother
of Rajendra Chola.

He too would inquire after the welfare
of all eleven,
beginning with Chola Mahadevi;

then bow at the feet
of grandmother Sembiyan Mahadevi,
receive a spear from her hand......

and go to the royal council
for deliberation.

Every day,
this was Arulmozhi Varman's royal duty.

And within it,
in the time he set aside for family,

all those meetings!
All those loving conversations!

Everything moved
with the speed of swords
flung upon the battlefield,
with the swiftness of spears!

Rush! Alertness!
And only if nature granted it,
a pause some day!

<!-- scan 225 -->

Thus Rajaraja spun like a top —
performed duty like the sun.

His younger daughter Kundavai,
wife of Vimaladitya,
would come.

There would be no talk
of her domestic life;

the two would discuss
only the “burden of the realm.”

Once a spear is taken up,
on the field
it must do the work of a spear.

Once the sceptre is taken up,
must one not attend to the subjects?

Can one then attend
only to one's own family?

No.

This Three-Crowned Chola himself
stood as the model
that shows it cannot be done.

### English translation

<!-- scan 226 -->

That future age itself
will be a precious age!

Have you seen it anywhere?
Has anyone ever told you?

Sixteen years as crown prince,
thirty years as the hero who ruled the land —

a total of forty-six years.

In those years he bore,
among others,
the titles
“Mummudi Chola”
and “Jayankonda Chola,”

which in turn became
names of mandalams and valanadus;

and also:
Cholendra Simhan,
Sivapadasekaran,
Sikhamani,

Jananathan,
Nigarili Cholan,
Rajendra Simhan,
Chola Martandan,
Rajasiriyan,
Raja Martandan,

Nithya Vinodan,
Pandya Kulasani,
Keralantakan,
Singalantakan,
Ravikula Manikkam,
Telungu Kula Balan......

and still more,
countless titles and distinctions,

winning victory wherever he went,
laying the foundation
for a Tamil empire
to stand for three hundred years.

Yet for that warrior among warriors,
that victorious son —

what year was he born?

He did not know;
his household did not know.

<!-- scan 227 -->

The country did not know.
It is hard to believe!

But this is an example
that even the unbelievable
can happen.

For the many battle-events he saw,
there are countless inscriptional witnesses.

For the temples he built,
there are many documents.

There is information
to tell us of his administration,
his system of rule,
the distinction of his sceptre.

But for that ruler
who carved his fame
with strength and resolve
greater, the source declares,
than Alexander who shook the world,

his birthday alone
went unrecorded!

We do not know the year;
we have only the note
that he was born
on the Sadhayam star-day
in the month of Aippasi.

On that basis,
Rajaraja's Sadhaya festival
is held every year.

For the king
who drew notes upon inscriptions and charters
and carved the events
of the Chola imperial age,

those who lived with him —
or among those born as his children —

not one thought properly enough
to write down
even the day he died!

That they failed
to leave such things
as property for the future
is a disappointment;

for that,
we must bow our heads.

<!-- scan 228 -->

Because there is no note
of a person's birthday,
does it follow
that the person was never born?

When the monsoon
pours heavy rain,
can we calculate
which particular cloud gave it?

Do we not look
at the full lakes and ponds
and know that great rain fell?

So too,
from the empire that was formed,
we measure Rajaraja's greatness
and praise him.

We count the wars he fought
and stand amazed;

we think of the victories he won
and our hearts swell....

As we may forget the cloud
but never the rain,

though his birthday was forgotten,
we have not forgotten his greatness!

Was not the rule
of world-renowned Rajaraja
one that gave Tamils
a special joy?

In the fourth year
after he wore the crown,
the Chola lion
marched upon the Chera country.

The commander who led it
was his fearless, beloved son,
Rajendra Chola,
who bore the name Panchavan Marayan.

Chera and Pandya
came against him in that war;

he defeated them both,
and the Chola clan,
the source says,
stood upright like a mountain.

★ ★ ★

In Kudamalai country,
Rajaraja's envoy
had been treated in a way
that destroyed his honour.

The great Chola army roared.

Hearing the king's command,
“Son, depart!”

Rajendra Chola swore,
“I shall return
only after making the enemy kneel.”

<!-- scan 229 -->

No one could stop
the sword he raised!

Kudamalai country bowed;
Thanjavur laughed aloud,
and its anger cooled!

★ ★ ★

The chieftains of Kongu country
and Kodungallur,
and those who ruled
the western coast,

were all ready
to bow at his feet
and lower their crowns.

Without obstruction,
wherever one looked there —
Chola army! Chola army!

Having won victory
and the title
“Keerthi Parakrama Cholan,”

that victorious son
returned to Thanjavur!

★ ★ ★

Gangapadi,
then a part of Mysore,
had Talakad as its capital.

King of kings Rajaraja
first crossed the Kaveri;

then entered Gangapadi
with his army.

He captured it,
brought Tadigaipadi too
under his rule,

planted the flag —
and Chola triumph
flourished there!

★ ★ ★

The Eastern Chalukya country
was suffering great confusion.

Unable to withstand his enemy,
a man called Saktivarman —

<!-- scan 230 -->

afraid —
surrendered before Rajaraja!

The Chola gave military aid,
conquered Vengi,
raised the victory flag,

then stretched out
a hand of mercy:

“Take hold of this.”

Saktivarman took hold.

Even after that,
Rajaraja married his daughter Kundavai
to Saktivarman's younger brother
Vimaladitya,

and strengthened his position
in Vengi country.

That marriage declared
that he was not merely Rajaraja;

in politics too,
he was a king of statecraft!

★ ★ ★

The inscriptions sing
victory-poems to the sky:

that he conquered Kalinga,
and that he seized
a part of Lanka.

★ ★ ★

In Rajaraja's history of victories,
the capture of the “Lakshadweep” islands
stands, the source says,
like a Himalayan summit.

In CE 1013
he created a naval force.

Without even using that force,
he conquered “those islands”; 

the effort begun by the father
would later serve as a forerunner
to the victories
won in the naval wars
of his son Rajendra.

<!-- scan 231 -->

Ranks of troops
that piled up victories;

camps that struck the enemy
at the very centre of the forehead
and drove him away;

commanders who, hour after hour,
“training, training,”
without slackening,
trained warriors into discipline —

within that Tamil army,
the source says,
there was also a force
called the Velakkara army.

If through their own failure
even a small danger
threatened the king,

those soldiers were ready
to destroy their own lives;

a suicide force,
the source calls them —

men who had surrendered
the whole of their hearts
to the king of Thanjavur.

★ ★ ★

We praise and honour
the tower of the Great Temple of Thanjavur
as Rajaraja's memorial gift
to Tamil Nadu.

It is indeed
a gift that endures!

Yet even there,
one deficiency troubles me.

Not that he built a temple —
but that he built that temple
from within a fort.

The temple remains;
where is the fort?

That question is not asked
in Thanjavur alone —

throughout Tamil Nadu
it rises as a question of longing!

Fortunately,
our kings and their descendants
at least preserved
those inscriptions,
documents,
and the literature of that age,

so that our history
was not erased altogether.

Let us take heart in that —

<!-- scan 232 -->

The schemes King Rajaraja
progressively designed
for the welfare of citizens,

his system of government,
and the structure built for it —
all were filled with distinction.

Yet Sanskrit dominance grew,
and the mother tongue Tamil
came to suffer.

Even if we say
that this was a deficiency
the Tamil people had to bear,

it was like buttermilk
mixed into milk!

For labourers and cultivators,
the kings might have tried
to do still more good.

Perhaps because service to Mahesan
was abundant,
service to the people
lay down a little!

Some say
that was the fault of the age;

it is more fitting to say
it was the fault of the guardian-ruler!

“Even the faults of an age
must be set in order
so that the guardian may attain fullness” —

is that not
the view of the good?

Though that is the central idea,
from that age to this one,

the strength of a government
sits in the first row
of the list of splendours;

and so history
gives that strength
first place.

Here is the inscription
shown even by the Thanjavur temple
Rajaraja created —

<!-- scan 233 -->

“Bearing in mind
that the wealth of the great earth,
like the Goddess of Fortune,
had become his own;

having graciously broken the fleet
at Kandalur Salai;

Vengi country and Gangapadi,
Tadigaipadi and Nolambapadi,
Kudamalai country, Kollam and Kalinga,

the fierce-working Sinhala Eelam mandalam,
Rattapadi of the seven-and-a-half lakhs,

and the twelve thousand ancient islands
of the three seas —

having taken them
by the rod of firm victorious strength,

in every year
of his growing, resplendent reign,
worthy of worship —

the regnal year
of Udaiyar Sri Rajaraja Devar,
Ko-Rajakesari Varman,
who took the lustre of kings.”

He conquered
these twelve countries;
he brought them
beneath his umbrella.

And their rulers and guardians....

Sezhiyan,
Chera,
Mahindan,
the king of Udagai,

the king of Kudamalai country,
the ruler of Nolambapadi,
the king of Gangapadi,
the ruler of Tadigaipadi,
Satyasrayan,

Telungu Bhiman,
Saktivarman,
the king of Kalinga,
the ruler of the ancient islands
of the three seas,
Pazhuvettaraiyar,

Kolli Mazhavan,
Otriyuran Prithikanda Varman,
Tiruvaiyan Sankara Devan,
Kodumbalur Velan Sundara Cholan,

<!-- scan 234 -->

Nannamaraiyan,
Veera Cholan Ilada Peraraiyan,
Rajaraja Vanakovaraiyan,
Malaiyaman,

Vallavaraiyan Vandiyathevan,
Vimaladitya —

thus the explanatory list,
the source says,
lay at his fingertips!

Whatever else may be said,
there lived for many years
one of our lineage
whom all in Tamil Nadu
could honour;

one who excelled in every art,
one who spread those arts,

one who cultivated courage
and heaped up victories;

one who loved agriculture,
who cared that the wealth of the soil should flourish,
that water-resources should abound,

who took interest
in forming rivers and canals,
deep and broad lakes,

who had them dredged often,
restored and maintained,
so that crops might grow,

who continued the work,
who protected the people
from ruinous floods
and storm-winds;

one who lived many years,
led armies,
and drove away enemies —

<!-- scan 235 -->

he was good,
        he was capable —
    and what gives us
    still greater joy
    is this:
    he was
            “one of ours.”

    Let all our people
    live as good people,
    as capable people;

    when that history
    gives us such a future,
    that future age
    will be a precious age for the Tamils —

    let it remain
    a future age!

## Item 48 — The Undying Art of Sculpture and the Beautiful Art of Painting!

### English translation

<!-- scan 236 -->

The undying art of sculpture,
and the beautiful art of painting!

After Rajaraja's father,
Parantaka II,
died naturally;

after his elder brother Aditya too
fell through conspiracy;

Madhurantakan — rightly or wrongly,
a man spoken of as connected with it —
wore the crown
and ruled until his death.

Only after those three
did Rajaraja become king
and receive the crown.

So whatever else we may say,
in CE 985
his age must have been
somewhere between forty and fifty!

Only after he too
had ruled for thirty years
did Rajendra Chola
assume royal responsibility;

scholar R. Rajamanickanar,
after research,
writes that Rajendra's age then
was fifty.

Even for him,
all that is known
is that the year he came to rule
was CE 1012.

He was born
on the Tiruvathirai star-day
in the month of Margazhi —

but in that family
of kings among kings,
there is no other detail!

For the father,
Sadhayam;
for the son,
Tiruvathirai —

only those are discovered
as the days on which
they “descended” into this world!

<!-- scan 237 -->

Only after son Rajendra
ascended the throne
did father Rajaraja
die naturally;

but even that day,
when we speak of history,
was forgotten.

The heart cannot help
but become wounded!

        Fortunately,
our ancestors forgot the dates,
but did not forget the news!

Is merely naming
a month and a star enough?

Every month,
days and stars
come and go.

Never mind —
        our old palaces
are historical splendours
that forgot birthdays and death-days,

places that became hidden confinements,
burying them out of sight!

Very well, let it go.
History at least
has reached us as a torn fragment;

let us take comfort
that even that fragment
did not fall into destitution,
and continue!

★ ★ ★

In Rajaraja's time,
Chola country,
with Thanjavur as capital,

extended north to the Tungabhadra,
northeast to Vengi country,

south to the central part of Eelam,
and through what are today
South Arcot, Tiruchi, Thanjavur and Pudukkottai.

It formed the ancient Chola mandalam;
and to avoid administrative difficulties,
many “valanadus” were created.

The subdivisions of a valanadu
too were called “nadus” —

the list runs on:
“Pappa Nadu,”
“Uratha Nadu” —

<!-- scan 238 -->

A group of ministers
assisted the king.

Government officers belonged
to divisions called
“Peruntaram” and “Sirutaram,”
with officers of the next rank beneath them.

They did not receive
a separate cash salary;

lands were granted to them,
and the yield from those lands
served as their pay.

To honour the honesty,
integrity and rare service
of certain officers,
lands were sometimes given
tax-free.

Each region called a “nadu”
was under the supervision
of a guard-officer.

An order was issued
to measure and account
for all the lands in the country,

classify them as forest,
upland and cultivated field,
and regulate land tax;

it was put into practice immediately.

There is a note,
the source says,
that an inscription found at Karur
names an officer as
“Ulagalavitta Tiruvadigal Sattan”!

Lakes that appeared like seas
too were gifts of grace
from the Chola kings!

Assemblies were formed
in every village;

four kinds of local councils
were constituted here and there
through elections.

Because high-caste domination
entered among them,
those local bodies were called:

“Chaturvedi Mangala Sabha,”
“Devadana Councils,”
“Brahmadeya Sabha,”

and “village councils
to which non-Brahmins were elected.”

I am not building a story;
I say that the researcher
shows inscriptional evidence!

<!-- scan 239 -->

The Uthiramerur inscription too
claims a share in this
root-level local-government system

for Parantaka Chola,
who had ruled sixty years
before Rajaraja.

Committees were formed as well
to conduct public affairs.

If there was even a drop of stain
in conduct, character,
integrity or honesty,

a person's name could not even
be written upon the *kudavolai*;

he would immediately
lose eligibility.

Only the fit
would be chosen;

the unfit
would be cast aside.

There were committees
for the lake,
for cultivated fields,
for accounts,
for the sluice —

and only those without blemish
would bear responsibility
in the committees
through which people performed civic duties.

The source also presents Rajaraja
as the first to group local bodies
into unions
and conduct orderly administration.

Rajaraja lived
with the principle
that every religion was acceptable.

Though he had a special devotion
to Saivism,
he embraced people of every faith
and took delight
in supporting good people
whatever their religion.

His elder sister Kundavai Nachiyar herself,
at a place called Dadapuram,

for Saivites,
Vaishnavites,
and Jains —
three communities —

<!-- scan 240 -->

personally led the building
of three temples
so that each might find fulfilment.

★ ★ ★

“I shall follow my father's path
without straying
and win a place
in the minds of the Tamil people” —

with that vow,
Rajendra Chola
bowed at the feet of his forebears
and took up the sword.

He became the living form
of Ayyan Tiruvalluvar's two Kurals:

“The good a father does his son
is to make him stand foremost
in the learned assembly,”

and:

“The good a son does his father
is to make people say,
‘What penance did this man's father perform
to gain such a son?’”

Seeing his son Rajendra rule in that way,
the great warrior Rajaraja
lost his heart in delight.

The mountain-sized support
the two gave
to art and literature
stood on one side.

Though it also formed
a fortress for theism,

the work they did
to make the arts of dance and song
into undying sculpture
and beautiful painting

was work praised by the world.

Those works are
Thanjavur Rajarajeswaram
and, its equal,
Gangaikonda Cholapuram!

## Translator notes

- Item 46 preserves the title witnesses separately. The displayed English title follows canonical `முன்மாதிரியாகத் திகழ்ந்த மும்முடிச் சோழன்!`; the contents witness's missing final exclamation mark is metadata only.
- Item 46 retains Kalaignar's central contrast between political/imperial achievement and his critique of social-cultural change.
