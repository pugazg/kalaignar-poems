## Item 34 — It Did Not Vanish; It Was Reborn!

- contents witness: `மறையவில்லை; மறுமலர்ச்சி பெற்றது!`
- canonical file: `../../../sections/34.md`

- printed pages: **160–165**
- title-witness status: **contents/title-page match**

### English translation

<!-- scan 161 -->

It did not vanish;
it was reborn!

The golden age
of the Cholas before
the third century BCE,

beginning with the early Chola king
“Sibi,”

from about CE 350
lost its crown, grew still,
and came to a state
that called for sorrow.

In that radiant age
when they wore the crown
and built their rule,

the first two Karikalans,
Killivalavan,
Kopperuncholan,

and many other great emperors
completed their reigns
and spread the thunderous roar
of their fame across the earth.

Though the Cholas then
had to meet a dark age,

their time began once more
to shine with light from Uraiyur,
from around CE 850 onward.

Though flashes appeared
here and there like streaks of light,
history says five hundred years
passed as an age of darkness!

But before that,
in Karikalan's time,
the great Chola country
had the North Pennaiyar
as one boundary —

<!-- scan 162 -->

and Kanyakumari,
they say,
as the next.

Kanchi Mandalam too
was joined to it.

The extent of the realm,
and its division into many *kottams*
according to that extent,

with each bearing
its proper responsibility,
gave strength and prosperity
to administration.

The Chola country was so fertile,
sings the poet Alur Mulankizhar with delight,
that the crop grown on a patch of land
no larger than a place where an *aanai* could lie down
could feed seven people with nectar-like food.

That Tamilakam stood
as a realm of Tamil verse
is shown by this too:

there were seven hundred
cultured poets in the Sangam age,
and among them
about fifty women poets.

Because they guarded Tamil
as the eye guards sight,

they preserved for us,
as eyelids guard the eye,
the history of our victories
that reached as far as the Himalayas.

In the rich, flourishing
golden Chola land,
though the rulers wished
to govern in accordance
with Tiruvalluvar's Kural,

that was also the moment
when speakers of the northern language
entered like a black cat
glimmering in darkness —
so, for the people of Tamilakam,

in the state-established
`அய்யம் பெருங்குழு`,
there came to be
minister, commander,
priest, envoy and spy;

and an “Eight Great Assembly”
was formed with
*karanathiyavar*, *karumavithi*,
*kanaka-chutram*, gatekeepers,
townspeople, army chiefs,
elephant warriors and horse warriors;

and, bringing all these together,
the village assembly met
to carry out the constructive work
of the state —

<!-- scan 163 -->

with “village courts” established
to function as ordered councils
and dispense justice;

rules were framed
and government carried on its work.

That the state functioned
by doing justice in due form
can be understood even from this:

when crowned king Kopperuncholan died,
the poet Poththiyar,
overcome with grief,
made into verse his cry:

“O king!
How beautiful was the way
you sat in the council
and conducted affairs of state!”

There is evidence from that age too
that members and leaders
who conducted village administration
were chosen through the *kudavolai* method
in which the village assembly took part.

Those accomplished
in different fields
could receive titles such as
*Enathi*, *Kavithi* and *Etti*.

The king who ruled the town
also had the custom
of spending a large part
of his leisure time
with renowned poets
who spoke what was,
as it was,
without empty exaggeration.

Knowing of Kovoor Kizhar alone,
I would say,
is evidence enough!

So many learned men
stood as poets beloved by kings.

In great cities
such as Puhar-pattinam
were shining mansions,
flower gardens,

shallow wells,
beautiful swimming pools,
and splendid crystal halls
for pleasurable play.

P. T. Srinivasa Iyengar
also speaks openly,
in Chola history,
of marriage customs.

<!-- scan 164 -->

There were two forms
of marriage among the ancient Tamils:

musical instruments would sound,
God would be worshipped,
flowers and rice would be mixed
into auspicious bathing water,

and that very night
the bride and groom
would be joined in household life.

That is one form of marriage.
What is the other?

“There is no raising of ritual fire
on the wedding platform,
no circling of the fire,
no priest to receive *dakshina*;
this is a marriage wholly belonging
to the Tamils,”

says Srinivasa Iyengar★
about the wedding ceremony
in *Silappathikaram*!

Without strongly drawing attention
to the fact that an aged Brahmin
also comes there
to show the Vedic way,

he simply points out
the ancient custom of the Tamil
and goes on his way —

may we not praise
that truthfulness?

The literary picture of Poompuhar
painted by Ilango Adigal alone,
I would declare,

is enough as evidence
for the ancient civilisational greatness
of prosperous Tamilakam.

Through it we see
the wealth of the Tamil land,
the hearts of the Tamils —

the love they possessed,
their valour,
their sense of duty,

the knowledge of the earth,
and knowledge of the heavens
they had learned — ah!

As we go on thinking of all this,
we who laboured to create
a new Poompuhar
and won success —

have we not used
those old glories as manure
and planted this flowering sapling?

When will it become a garden,
when will we pluck
its riot of flowers
and heap them with joy
at the golden feet
of Mother Tamil?

<!-- scan 165 -->

Is that not what
we must long for!

For our dream to triumph,
how many more burdens
must our shoulders still bear?

And further —

when the Last Sangam age
was coming to its end,

the Andhra kings called Satavahanas,
who had ruled as far as the Ganga,

and the Pallava kings
who had submitted to them,
passed through a change in their age of power;

Pallava rule began to spread
south of the Palar,

and the ancient city Kanchi
became its capital.

The first Pallava king
to rule from there,
at the beginning
of the fourth century CE,
was Sivaskanda Varman!

As gathering clouds
darken the sky
and reduce its light,

so for nearly five hundred years
the Chola line remained
without light —

then began once more
to rise after CE 850!

The man who then
took responsibility for rule
and mounted the royal throne
at Uraiyur

was the Chola king Vijayalaya,
father of the warrior Aditya.

That great dawn wrote into history
for humankind this truth:

the greatness of the Cholas
had not vanished;
it had gained a renaissance!

<!-- scan 166 -->

Between the Chola country's decline
and its later rise
there lay only
a five-hundred-year interval.

But within that span,
Tamil names such as
Senguttuvan, Karikalan
and Ilancetcenni disappeared,

and the northern-language name
Vijayalaya Chola
came swiftly in their place.

You will know the answer
to the question why!
You will gain the explanation!

★ (Vide his “History of the Tamils” - Page 80)

## Item 35 — A Noble Friendship Higher Than Life Itself!

### English translation

<!-- scan 167 -->

A noble friendship
higher than life itself!

Pisiranthaiyar —
that concentrated blaze
of classical Tamil,
who stood like a lamp of wisdom
to the Pandyan Arivudai Nambi —

held for Kopperuncholan,
king of the Chola country,
an affection no words
could ever weave.

One day,
in his birthplace,
the village called Pisir,
in a grove dense with shade,

the poet of principle
longed to fulfil a desire:

to compose a poem
about his life-friend,
the Chola king.

Making a swan-cock
the centre of his imagination,
the poet shaped a lovely poem
in sugar-sweet Tamil!

“O swan-cock!
O swan-cock!”
he called,
opening the living heart
within him,

while on one side
he held down the storm in his mind
that he still had not gained
a chance to see
Kopperuncholan,
the king Perungokilli.

He spoke from the fullness
of his breast:

“O swan-cock!
O swan-cock!

from the tip of maiden Kanyakumari,
with your beak
that has pecked the *ayirai* fish,

you fly very high
through the sky,
travelling toward
the snow-mountain-filled north;

may your journey triumph!

<!-- scan 168 -->

On the way you pass,
my darling bird,
there lies the capital
of the Chola who subdues his foes!

If you and your mate
go that way,
you will see in the Chola's eyes
the light of affection!

If you tell him,
‘We belong to Pisir,
the town where the poet Anthaiyar lives,’

the king himself
will run to welcome and honour you,
and delight your mate
with many ornaments!”

★ ★ ★

Whether the swan-cock,
after hearing Anthaiyar's words,
ever went to the place
where the Chola king lived or not,

the poet, content
that he had carved
the greatness of his life-friend,
recited the poem
to his beloved wife
and rejoiced.

“What a beautiful imagination,
that swan-cock!” she said,
serving rice to him
and to their grandchildren.

“Look here —
two rows of *mullai* flowers
are smiling!” he said.

Opening her bow-like brows,
his wife asked,
“Where?”

“The maiden's milk-white smile —
and here,
the smile of this white rice —
those are the *mullai* flowers
I meant!” he said.

“Mind what you say;
the children are beside us!”
she warned.

“Our son's diamond statues
are not yet old enough
to understand such words!

<!-- scan 169 -->

Our grown son
has gone to the fields
with his wife;
it will take time
for them to return.
Do not fear!” he said.

“They will come now;
do not start cuddling me,”
she warned.

At once the poet
closed his mouth —
with the rice she had served!

It caught in his throat;
he choked and struggled.

The woman sprang up,
tapped her husband
on the head,

and rubbed his chest
with her hand;
he embraced her —
she slipped away!

Like anklet-bells
ringing with concern,
the old lovers
burst into delighted laughter.

Sweet as the base
of a sugarcane,
the grandchildren babbled,
clapped their tiny hands
and laughed too.

Then, as though a sudden barrier
had been thrown across
joy pouring like a breached tank,

a piece of grave Tamil news
fell upon their ears.

The sons whom Uraiyur king
Kopperuncholan cherished as life itself,
it said,
had raised an army against him;

and the Chola king,
who composed poetry
in parrot-sweet Tamil,
was plunged into great sorrow
by the deed of his children;

and the man
whose heart was a white flower,
seeing no other way,
had taken the warrior's sword
in his hand
and risen to enter the field.

Hearing this strange news
of battle breaking out
between father and sons,
Pisiranthaiyar said

<!-- scan 170 -->

that the happiness in his mind
had become like a possession lost,
and with deep anguish
set out for the Chola country.

★ ★ ★

He heard that the learned men
of the town had gathered
in a public village council
established by Kopperuncholan.

His heart beating
with an agitation beyond words,
he went there
to learn whether
the smoke of war
had been put out.

The great poets
Poththiyar, Boothanaganar,
Naththathanar
and Pullattrur Eyittriyanar

leapt from the council,
ran to him,
embraced Pisiranthaiyar
against their chests
and rejoiced:

“Come! Come!”

“My limbs tremble,
my heart trembles —
that is how I have come, sir!

What happened
to the war that arose
between the lion
and its cubs?”

The poet stood there
like a question mark.

“Nothing ended in disaster;
listen!

Something else arose,
and this great man
leapt like lightning
and stopped it.

Asking,
‘Must the hen itself suffer
because the chicks trample?’

he cooled even Kopperuncholan
with tender Tamil verse
and became the reason
for the war being stopped.

This is Pullattrur Eyittriyanar!”
said Poththiyar,
introducing him.

Pisiranthaiyar leapt
with overflowing joy.

Then he returned home,
his happiness blossoming —

but a message waited there
that would turn his heart
into a storm.

★ ★ ★

<!-- scan 171 -->

The Chola king,
who had ruled the land
like the sun rising in the east,

unable to mend the heart
broken by the opposition
of the children born to him,

had gone to practise *vadakkiruthal*
and give up his life —

so the poet read
in a palm-leaf message
sharp as a blade.

“This very moment
I will leave,
go home,
take my farewell
and return.

We who have lived
all this time for friendship
will not be separated
in the world of death!

Tell the Chola
who has gone to sit facing north:
Pisiranthai will soon come
to sit beside him!”

Saying this,
the lion among poets
set out,

tears dropping
from his two eyes
like dew falling
from a leaf.

★ ★ ★

The pure-Tamil Chola
who had ruled through his appointed days,

his heart wrapped in fog
as he thought of the war
raised by his sons,

wished to die
through *vadakkiruthal*,
choosing that as the path
by which all anguish of mind
might disappear.

Poets living across Tamilakam
heard of it;

some, saying their own lives
were no nectar to be hoarded,
went and sat with him.

As the king's life,
resolved to depart,
slowly melted away
and his whole body weakened,

some of them looked
at the great Kopperuncholan
and said:

“O king of kings!

You say the master
of green Tamil verse,
Pisiranthaiyar,

holds a friendship with you
that never bends within,
even though you have never seen each other.

Your span of life
is about to end;
why has he not come?”

<!-- scan 172 -->

“He vowed that he would return
and give up his life beside you —
what became of those words?”

When mockery sounded in this way,
the king grew angry
at those who raised doubts
about the integrity
of his friend's friendship
even at the place of death.

“The friendship of Anthaiyar,
that gold who arose
in the little Tamil village Pisir
of the Pandya country,

is a pure friendship
that may not come forward
to show itself while life goes well,

but when one falls low,
it will run without hesitation
and stand beside him!

Learned men,
know this!”
answered the king.

The very next day
he gave up his life
and became a *nadukal*.

Pisiranthaiyar did come late;
there, beside his friend,
he too sat facing north
and gave up his life.

The poet Kannaganar,
turning tears into flowers,
strung a garland of verse
and placed it
upon those hero-stones.

Here it is:

“The worthy will always
stand beside the worthy.....
Those without true worth
will fall away in history
like dry leaves!”

Is not the friendship
of these noble men
a great friendship
higher than life itself!

## Item 36 — He Is Young; He Is a Son of Tamil!

- canonical file: `../../../sections/36.md`

### English translation

<!-- scan 173 -->

He is young;
he is a son of Tamil!

That was an age
when even a tender shoot,
still too young for school,

would rise with a spear
that leapt forward,
with a sword
that gathered up fury,

crying,
“I will pluck out
and cast away the foe!”

It was an age
granted to the Tamils
like gold —

an age when many poets
nurtured the Sangam!

Warriors went
to the battlefield
like surging tigers;

auspicious women
stood blessing them.

Even when the wind blows,
Tamil valour comes
and strikes within it!

The sea-wave rises high —
in it the Tamil's stature
rises and shines!

The smoke of *akil* wood
spreads fragrance —
in that too
Tamil's fame is fragrant!

A lovely moon appears
among the clouds —
in it appears
the treasure of a Muttamil epic!

Even King Ashoka,
who ruled the great land
praised by the world,

spoke of those
outside his rule

<!-- scan 174 -->

as the mighty people
of the southern country.

Among those broad-shouldered
peoples of the South
was one division,
the valiant Kosars!

They had the power
to bring forth courage,
and were famed
for truthfulness joined with purity.

Leaving Tulu country,
they stopped their wandering
as far as the eastern sea
and, history says,
settled in the regions
called “Niyamam” and “Sellur.”

“● South of Sellur —
where the great sea roars —
at Niyamam,
the fierce-eyed Kosars,
their faces scarred
by iron weapons...”

★ ★ ★

This is the message
given by *Akananuru*.

It introduces
two lion-like Tamil warriors,
one called “Anni”
and one called “Minjili.”

Joining those two names
comes a young Tamil woman —

though she was a maiden
at the age when her eyes themselves
became weapons of war
that felled young bulls,

she was a virtuous,
sacred young woman!

Many longed
to embrace her.

Some Kosars
asked her father
for her hand;
he refused.

The beautiful maiden
called Anni Minjili
was a woman of chastity
that breathed fire,
like the warrior Anni
who died in battle!

She was firm
like the warrior Minjili
who defeated and killed
Aay Eyinan
and made Nannan's town
his own!

<!-- scan 175 -->

“Like a picture of the moon —
like long-eyed *kendai* fish
moving through water —

a parrot-girl who moves with grace,
with eyes fit for good union —”

thinking so,
some Kosars came sweetly seeking her.

Her father asked,
“What do you think my daughter is?

She is sugarcane, man!
I will not agree
to place her
in the hand of a savage monkey!

Do you know
where all her thoughts rest?

Upon the man of Azhundur!
Tithiyan,
the guardian of that town,
is her guardian too!

Do not come again.
Understand what I have told you
and act accordingly — now!”

History says
that even among Kosars
renowned for truthfulness,
there lived some
with hard hearts.

They devised a plan
to seize that maiden.

Many fertile fields
where bean crops grew
were the property
of the Kosars settled at Niyamam.

One cow alone
was the cherished family property
of Anni Minjili.

With a dreadful plan,
some Kosars drove that cow
into their bean field
and let it graze.

If a hungry cow
is given a crop-field for pasture,
will it run away in fear
without rushing in to eat?

Like the belly
of a cruel ruling class
that swells by gathering
and devouring together
all the labour
of the working class,

that cow's stomach swelled
until there seemed
no room left within it!

<!-- scan 176 -->

“If the old man
who said, ‘No maiden for you,’
is made into a man
with no eyes,

then the peahen
will have no protector!
All of us can share
that peerless beauty.”

So plotted those men
of degraded nature,
working their trickery.

They put Anni Minjili's father
in the accused's cage
and told the story
of the cow grazing
in the bean field that day.

The Kosars of the village council asked:
“Tell us — what is the truth?”

The old man's face
darkened like a rain-filled sky.

“I will not ask,
‘Who saw my cow graze?’

Mine is a lineage
that will not tell a lie
no matter who opposes us.

It is true:
my cow grazed
the bean crop!” he said.

He admitted the offence.

Therefore the council
of the Kosars pronounced judgment:
“Gouge out his eyes!”

The next moment
they dragged away
Anni Minjili's father —

that ripe old man, poor man —
and tore out both his eyes.

“Have you lost your sight,
Father?

I will not leave
these sinners unavenged!”
cried the daughter,
raising the sound
of a war-drum.

“They destroyed my eyes
so that they could steal you!

Who has the courage
to approach my daughter,
who carries chastity
as a sword?”

Before her father
could finish speaking,
the woman standing like a leader
answered.

<!-- scan 177 -->

“Until the lives
of those who blinded
my father, dear as life,
are gone —

I will not eat
from an eating vessel;
I will not change my clothes.

Until vengeance is complete,
there will be no hunger,
no sleep for me!

I will finish the feud!
I will give the enemies
as sacrifice
for my father
who has lost his sight!”

Anni Minjili's vow
reached Tithiyan of Azhundur
the very next day.

Hearing of the sorrow
that had struck the maiden,
he grieved
and longed to see her.

As soon as the news
entered his ears,
he saw “Anni”
standing before him
like a tongue of red flame.

“Delight of my eyes!
One who fits my very thought!

I know neither the method
nor the path
by which to place medicine
upon the wound in your heart,”
he grieved.

“You have cavalry
in your fort, king!

You have shoulders
like a mountain,
my dear!

If it is true
that you love me,
Anni Minjili,

set out without delay
for war against the Kosars!

They have made my father
one who lives in darkness —
those ignorant villains
must be brought down.

I have come seeking
broad-shouldered Tithiyan,
the hero who will hack apart
those eye-gouging snakes!”
she said.

“See —
my sword has risen
from its sheath!

Know, beloved,
that my vow will not die!

<!-- scan 178 -->

Only after I return
having taken the lives
of those who injured
your father — my father-in-law —

will these hands
touch you!”
roared the man of Azhundur.

The war-host set out!

The bodies
of the wicked Kosars
were shattered —

they fell dead
like a long wall
that cannot stand.

Her vow was fulfilled
as she desired.

She leaned upon the shoulder
of the returning Tithiyan!

An *Akananuru* poem
is the basis for this.

★ ★ ★

Note: (*Akananuru* poem 262, sung by Paranar. The characters who appear in this historical incident are not imaginary. There may be chronological contradictions and differences of place. When the literary evidence is compared, this is, in fact, a moving portion of Chola-period history that really occurred.)

## Translator notes

- Item 34 preserves the source's own chronology, administrative claims and Chola/Pallava transition as presented. Translation does not resolve them through outside historical knowledge.
- The source form `அய்யம் பெருங்குழு` is retained visibly rather than silently regularised to another council-name. The eight-member list is likewise rendered with culturally/source-sensitive administrative terms where a confident English institutional equivalent is not supplied by the source.
- Item 34 preserves the marriage passage, the inline star attached to P. T. Srinivasa Iyengar, and the scan-166 English footnote exactly as a source-visible note. The verified scan confirms the printed footnote `(Vide his “History of the Tamils” - Page 80)`.
- Item 35 retains the swan-cock address, the domestic comic interlude, all star separators, the father–sons crisis, Pullattrur Eyittriyanar's intervention, and the closing *vadakkiruthal* / *nadukal* friendship sequence. `வடக்கிருந்து` is translated consistently with earlier batches as *vadakkiruthal* rather than flattened into a generic death scene.
- Item 36's displayed English title follows the canonical male lexical form `இளையவன்; அவன் ஒரு தமிழ் மகன்!`, even though the central narrative later turns to the young woman Anni Minjili. The title is not silently rewritten to harmonise it with the body.
- The compact *Akananuru* quotation on scan 174 is translated cautiously as a distinct classical quotation; the verified source form `செல்லூர்க் குணா அது` and the scarred/fierce-eyed Kosar imagery remain traceable rather than being normalised into a modern prose paraphrase.
- Item 36 retains the working-class/ruling-class belly simile, the eye-gouging judgment, Anni Minjili's vow, Tithiyan's answering vow, the concluding star separator, and the complete source `குறிப்பு:` based on *Akananuru* 262. The note's claims are translated as source claims, not externally adjudicated.
