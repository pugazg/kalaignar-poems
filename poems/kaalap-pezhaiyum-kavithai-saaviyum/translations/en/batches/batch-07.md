## Item 16 — As a Flavour United Within the Segment!

### English translation

<!-- scan 82 -->

Whoever comes forward to write the history of the earth
may write without even the paper needed today;

may write without a good palm leaf;
may write without the favour of the computer
that displays the prowess of science —

but
can history be written
without a note on Tamil Nadu?
On that question we may raise our heads
and issue a challenge:

write it; write it if you will —
it will not be complete,
even if it is methodically arranged!

I had the opportunity to write,
as a continuing historical novel,
*The Pandyan of Rome*;

it was released with a useful address
by that great teacher of heroic Tamil,
like light to the eye, like the broad sky —
Mozhi Nayiru Devaneya Pavanar.

The fully qualified Justice,
the upright man praised by the worthy,
Anantha Narayanan, fragrant with affection,
spoke wonderfully and rejoiced in heart.

Receiving the first copy of the book,
and offering praise with honey mixed into verse,
was the friend who would walk with an arm over my shoulder —
Kannadasan,
who gave Tamils poems in heap after heap.

<!-- scan 83 -->

The evidence I supplied
in that book's introductory section

is not merely something to be entered today
in this “Casket of Time” —
it deserves to be entered forever
in the accounts of history!

The introduction I wrote then
for that published book,

I now give again
in the very form it had at that time:

“Octavius wore the jewelled crown
as the first emperor of Rome!
The Roman Senate, swelling with delight,
bestowed on Octavius the title ‘Augustus’,
a shortened name drawn from the heroic phrase
‘the majestic great warrior’.

It was in that period, historians have determined,
that victory flourished in our ancient Tamil land
beneath the flags of the three great kingdoms,
and in the Chola realm Thirumavalavan,
the second Karikal Peruvalathan, was ruling.
Tamil scholars place this Karikalan's period
between 60 B.C. and 10 B.C.

Records are also found saying
that a Pandyan king sent his envoy
to Augustus Caesar, who ruled in Rome,
and that this was in 20 B.C.

If so, who was the Pandyan king
who sent that envoy
to Augustus, king of Rome?

The poet Karikkannanar of Kaveripoompattinam,
who sang a *Puram* poem about Karikala Chola,
joined a Pandyan king with him in that poem.
That king's name was Peruvaluthi.

<!-- scan 84 -->

‘You are the king of Uraiyur where virtue abides;
he is the ruler with the cool sceptre
in Madurai where Tamil flourishes!
You belong to the cool-watered Kaveri;
he is like a bull among the Pandyan clan,
renowned in war,
possessing the sandalwood of Podhigai,
the pearls of the wave-filled sea,
and the three great drums.
You two appear like two great gods standing together!
May our friendship remain forever as it is today!
May your spear, winning and winning,
rise higher on the battlefield!
Engrave tiger and fish,
and carve your victory
upon the hills of other lands!’

In the fifty-eighth poem of *Purananuru*,
Karikkannanar gives this beautiful thought
and blesses together
Chola Karikalan and Pandyan Peruvaluthi.

From this we conclude
that the Pandyan of Karikalan's period
was Peruvaluthi.
Karikalan's period was from 60 B.C. to 10 B.C.

It was in that same span
that Augustus Caesar exercised authority in Rome —
that is, from 39 B.C. to A.D. 14.

By examining this chronology,
we are led to the conclusion
that the Pandyan who maintained friendship with Augustus Caesar
and sent him an envoy
could only have been Peruvaluthi.

Literature bears witness
that Tamil kings called Greeks and Romans ‘Yavanas’
and kept those Yavanas
as guards in their palaces.
The *Silappathikaram* line,
‘the sword-wielding Yavanas,
excellent in guarding the gate of the fortified wall,’
is a fitting example.

In the fifty-sixth *Puram* poem sung by Nakkiranar,
the line ‘the cool fragrant wine
brought in fine vessels by the Yavanas’

<!-- scan 85 -->

shows that Yavanas
brought varieties of wine into Tamil Nadu
and sold them here.

Among the foodstuffs exported
from the South to Rome,
pepper was extremely important.
When a man named Alaric once conquered Rome,
it is said that the settlement of compensation
required three thousand pounds
of Tamil Nadu pepper to be supplied.

Near Korkai, the great port of the Pandyan country,
pearls and conches were found in great abundance,
and won praise from people
in every part of the world.

Of the pearl of Korkai,
an *Akam* poem beautifully says:

‘Korkai, guarded by the warlike Pandyas in righteousness,
and the pearl of its great port.’

Pearls, fine cotton cloth,
and garments woven from rat-hair and silk
were sent to lands such as Rome;
from there horses, varieties of wine,
and glassware kept arriving
at the great port of Korkai.

The Yavanas, it is said,
would come in beautiful ships loaded with gold,
and then return carrying pepper.
Sangam literature proudly says
that the great sound of those ships
filled the whole harbour of Muziris.

Tamil literature says
that traders and sailors from every land
would disembark at the port of Puhar,
and all of them would mix and live in unity
as though they were one people.
The single fact that many Yavanas
also served as bodyguards of Tamil kings
is itself a mirror showing
the goodwill that existed between the two sides.

<!-- scan 86 -->

As though proving such contacts today,
research at a place called Arikamedu
near the city of Puducherry
has uncovered many kinds of objects
belonging to the Romans.

Why are heaps upon heaps of Roman coins
found in the Coimbatore district?
Researchers examined this too.
To send goods from the eastern coast to Rome
through the western coast,
one had either to round Kanyakumari
or travel through the cross-route
where the Eastern and Western Ghats meet and open a passage.
Some cite that too
as one reason for the large coin-hoards
found in that region today.

Roman gold coins were discovered
in sixty-eight places across India and Pakistan.
When we hear that fifty-seven
of those sixty-eight places
lie south of the Vindhyas,
we think and think
of the old relationship between Rome and the South
and let out a great sigh.

That sigh has still not subsided;
you see it escaping even here,
in this Casket of Time.

Those traces of history from that age,
dug up and discovered,

may appear fit only for contempt
to those who black them out —
but they are not merely things to praise and cherish;

they are things that ought to sit enthroned,
with joy,
in the assembly of the world.

Crawling and crawling,
stumbling and tottering,
struggling at the threshold —

<!-- scan 87 -->

only when the mother lifts
this child called Tamil Nadu's history
and places it in her lap

will the account come right;
then a bridge will be formed
between the many national peoples!

Within the sweet fruit called ‘world history’,
one of its inner flavours is Indian history;

and within that flavour,
united as flavour within flavour,
is this history of Tamil Nadu!

## Item 17 — From Where Is World History to Come?

### English translation

<!-- scan 88 -->

Crores — crores — crores —
how many crores of years ago,
perhaps on one year, one month,
one day, one *naazhigai*, one second —

we knew nothing of what stood before us,
nor who stood before us;
darkness — darkness — darkness —

we know the colour of that darkness now:
black!
But then we did not know it —
because we had seen no colour at all!

Black, red, green, yellow,
blue, white —
for us to give each colour a separate name,
no such colour had yet come
and stood alone before us.

What we saw then,
we now understand,
was only the colour black.
Had we then named it ‘white’,
then even now
the colour of darkness
would have been white — white indeed!

A feeling —
as though someone had brushed past,
as though someone stood nearby!

<!-- scan 89 -->

Who was it?
Adam and Eve?
Or was it we ourselves?

Before an answer could be found,
something exploded!
What was it that scattered?
The heart trembled!

Such a colossal sound —
as though a thousand enormities
had been stacked one upon another,
and an enormity larger than all of them
had fallen —

surely we might have heard it
before our ears went deaf?
But we neither heard nor saw it.

Only the mark left by that sound,
like a line scratched across the surface,
became known —
as I said at the beginning —
crores — crores — crores
of years later!

The noise rising
from a basket packed with a crore chicks
can now be heard —
that same sound is heard from beside us,
and clearly from above us and below us too!

Those baskets floated in the sky then,
and they float there now!
The basket we inhabit does too!

There — another basket;
why does it circle us?
No — no —
we are the ones circling it;
we keep circling!

<!-- scan 90 -->

Ah! We ourselves
are spinning around ourselves;

are all those things
that appear far away from us
and keep circling
also baskets holding crores of chicks,
like ours?

Are all the baskets circling?
How are we to know?
We lie within darkness,
not even knowing the colour of darkness,
in a blind condition —

we revolve around ourselves;
it is a movement unknown to us!
No one obtained our consent for it!

Things that exploded and split apart like us —
do they too revolve,
under the name ‘universe’?
Do they circle something else,
or someone else?

Are we alone the universe?
Or is everything together the universe?

Whatever it is, let it be!
What is our task now?
What command has been laid upon us?

Task or command —
even if we do not come forward to fulfil it,
it is not left lying idle,
without even beginning to be carried out:

it moves! it moves!
it keeps on moving.

For however many crores of years,
for that many crores of years,
that ‘movement’ has continued —

and in between,
through some tremors or other,
who knows what, and what all,

<!-- scan 91 -->

changes and changes again.
Only afterwards
are we able to understand them.

Even if we knew beforehand,
we have not been given
the power to prevent them —

and when there is no one there
to do the giving,
how can it be given?
Who would give it?

I and you included — that is, we —
were, and remain,
chicks within one basket
that exploded and scattered
crores upon crores of years ago;
like our own basket!

Here — I shall give you an image.
On a seashore where a cool soft breeze blows,
children blow bubbles of air
and send them floating;

like balloons,
they fly until they burst!
One of them is this basket of sky
upon which we have climbed —
or have been made to climb.

Wind tosses that basket about;
sea-water strikes and rocks it;
and facing every fierce natural assault —
sea-catastrophe,
storm, cyclone,
violent gale —

we hang on,
reaching out and catching
the fragments and scraps
left standing.

And in that hanging,
what temporarily holds us up,
and what strikes and shatters us,

<!-- scan 92 -->

is nature itself,
as far as we have come to know it.

In its creation,
then and now,
we took form —
and so did our baskets;
yes,
baskets of a crore chicks,

baskets accompanied
by the chorus cry
‘kuk! kuk! kuk!’

Was the first basket Kumari Kandam?
The land of Africa?
Something else?

What was the age discovered there —
Stone Age? Golden Age?

When man — that is, you and I —
had not yet appeared,
when we ourselves had not appeared,
what meaning could Stone Age or Golden Age have?

How did we appear at all?
The chick became a hen!
The hen laid an egg!
From the egg came the chick!
Which came first — egg or hen?

Only the explanation called evolution
can unfold an answer to this!
Are not the seeds of plants,
in one sense, eggs too?

First, something called an egg takes shape —
and not merely the hen:
even the human being forms within an egg.

An egg does not mean only a hen's egg,
nor only the eggs laid by birds.
In every drop of semen
there are lakhs upon lakhs of ‘eggs’ —
and from among them,
using only those that are required,

<!-- scan 93 -->

any embryo takes form;
according to the place in which it forms,
on the basis of evolutionary development,
it takes shape as bird,
animal,
crawling creature,
or human being!

If we say man came from the monkey,
we must understand it
as stage-by-stage development,
as coming through evolution;

can we then ask,
‘Why have the monkeys we see today
not turned into human beings?’

As I have said once before:
we mix mud to raise a wall.
After the wall has been raised
from the mixed mud,
the mud left over
can only remain mud;

must that leftover mud itself
somehow become a wall?

All right —
if an egg becomes a hen,
how is the egg itself formed?

The answer is:
as mud is formed,
so too is the egg formed.

Just as clay — or wall-earth — and water
mix together to form mud,
so too certain mixtures are needed
for an egg to form.

From egg to egg,
the nutrients required,
the substances able to form an egg,
lend their help —
and from that egg comes the hen.

In the same way,
through evolution,
from the monkey grew the human being!

Did he first appear in Kumari Kandam?

<!-- scan 94 -->

Or in Africa?
This is a question needed
not only for human history;
it is needed for world history too!

‘The human who arose in Lemuria,
the great continent embracing India,
South-East Asia, Africa and Australia,
was the first civilized human,’

so, in 1974,
the Soviet scholar Kondra Rao is said to have written
in *The Riddles of Three Oceans*;

but because ‘Lemuria’ had broken apart
and today's continents had already taken
approximately their present form
one crore years ago,

it has been announced as the conclusion of research
that, about 12,000 years ago,
during the Ice Age when sea level was low,
the southern land that existed
south of Kanyakumari before the sea took it —
Kumari Kandam —
is more suitably regarded
as the birthplace of humankind,
or the birthplace of human language and civilization.

Therefore,
against nature itself —
which shows its mighty power
to alter the birthplaces of rivers,
the paths in which they flow,
the sea,
and the city,

through the hand of earthquake,
the speed of storm,
and sea-catastrophe —
against that nature
human beings have stood and lived
for all these ages.

The growth of human civilization
could not have taken shape evenly,
at the same moment,
in every place in the world.
In connection with this,
Mozhi Nayiru Devaneya Pavanar writes:

‘When skill in crafts
and the ability to speak a refined language
grew among the people of Kumari Nadu,
agricultural abundance,

<!-- scan 95 -->

commercial abundance,
and the arts of city-building
rose correspondingly to excellence’;

and also:

‘At this frontier of time,
when language was spoken in refined form
and civilization was developing,
the ancestors of the Scythians
and the ancestors of the Sumerians
may have separated and departed
from Kumari Nadu.’

Thus Kumari Kandam is shown
not merely as the place
where human beings first appeared,
but also as the cradle
of human civilization.

It was on this basis
that scholars such as Vincent Smith
and Professor Sundaranar said
that Indian history
should be written not from the north,
but from the south — from Tamil Nadu.

Now, turning that argument around and extending it,
we must say that the idea grows firm
that not only Indian history,
but world history itself —
the history of humankind
‘from the appearance of man to this day’ —
should be written from the south.

In accordance with that,
I have been opening and showing
the ‘Casket of Time’
with the ‘Key of Poetry’.

## Item 18 — We Seek What Remains After the Rubbing Away!

### English translation

<!-- scan 96 -->

Because no expert capable
of classifying, compiling,
and presenting history
came the Tamils' way,

have not stone inscriptions,
great epics and literary works,
and literary evidence that has not perished with time,
become the history of the noble Tamils
from that day down to this?

The rising Pandyan king Nediyon,
who stood before all others;

Chola Karikal Peruvalathan,
who strode as far as the summit of Mount Meru;

and Cheran Senguttuvan,
who went to the Himalaya
to bring stone for Kannagi's statue
and defeated the kings Kanaka and Vijaya —

these are men stamped by history
as our ancestors
from three thousand years ago.

They saw many battlefields —
Ganga, Vanga, Kalinga —
and defeated their enemies;

even if our eyes close,
the earth will not close over their names —
heroes of history!

Their descendants —
how many Cheras,
Cholas and Pandyas!

<!-- scan 97 -->

‘Tamil Nadu,
whose kings possessed the strength
to subdue the mountain Himalaya
whose head strikes the sky;
who fought in Kalinga
and destroyed its darkness!’

and

‘Tamil Nadu,
whose people sailed to Lanka,
Putpakam, Chavakam
and many an island,
and there saw
their tiger-banner and fish-banner
stand in dignity!’

and

‘Tamil Nadu,
whose fame spread through China,
Misiram, the land of the Yavanas,
and many other countries;
where knowledge, arts,
warcraft and commerce
grew splendidly!’

What Bharathi sang
are not merely poems;
they are unshakable seeds of evidence
that Tamil valour,
wisdom,
commerce,
and artistic sensibility
crossed the deep seas
and exercised influence beyond them!

In England, France, Spain,
and even in parts of America,
with Elam, Sumer and the Egyptians,
our Dravidians had civilizational contact —
who has ever denied it and prevailed?

Just as here we gladly praise iron
as the ‘black diamond’,
so, among the people who lived in Mexico,
they praise Maya civilization
as Dravidian civilization,
and say that their own firm blackness
is the colour in which beauty shines.

‘Nikito Sindo’, in their language,
is said to mean
‘black alone is the beautiful colour.’

<!-- scan 98 -->

Writing and civilization,
the Egyptians learned,
were gifts bestowed
by the Tamils of Kumari Kandam.

That Mesopotamian civilization rose to greatness,
that the people called Elamites rose in stature —
learned and impartial researchers of history,
men of good knowledge without deceit,
say the cause was the great fragrant rain
of civilization that Kumari Kandam poured forth
before that lost continent was destroyed.

‘Across the Mediterranean,
Kumari Kandam,
the Indus region,
Egypt,
Sumeria — everywhere it spread;
it was Tamil civilization,’

says the worthy Ramachandra Dikshitar,
offering what he presents as fitting evidence
in his book *The Origin and Spread of the Tamils*.

Even when clear evidence exists
that the civilization and culture
of the Dravidian Tamil people
had spread in every direction,
and that their capacities
had grown and flourished,

someone or other grinds down sandalwood,
smears it over the whole body,
and celebrates a festival;

from the sandalwood block used for that grinding,
after all that has been rubbed away,
we seek at least what remains —
we roam searching for it,
to put it to use for our Tamils!

That is this Casket of Time!

## Translator notes

- Item 16 contains a long embedded prose introduction from Kalaignar's earlier *Uromapuri Pandiyan* work. It remains visibly quoted as part of the poem rather than being reduced to summary.
- `யவனர்` is rendered **Yavanas** where the historical-literary identity matters; the surrounding Tamil prose itself glosses the term through Greeks and Romans.
- `நாழிகை` remains **naazhigai** elsewhere in the project when the source uses the traditional time unit; no modern-duration conversion is inserted into the translated body.
- Item 16's final fruit/flavour metaphor is retained rather than replaced with an abstract claim about national historiography.
- Item 17 deliberately preserves the repeated `கோடி - கோடி - கோடி`, `இருள் - இருள் - இருள்`, and `இயங்குகிறது!` sequences, the baskets/chicks/bubbles imagery, the egg–hen discussion, and the source's own evolutionary and Kumari Kandam arguments. The translation does not fact-check or modernize those claims.
- The compact biological wording in Item 17 concerning semen, ‘eggs’, embryos and evolutionary development is translated as the source presents it; no scientific correction is inserted into the verse.
- Item 17's citations to Kondra Rao, Devaneya Pavanar, Vincent Smith and Sundaranar remain source-attributed claims rather than independent editorial assertions.
- Item 18 is a documented title-witness discrepancy. The displayed English title translates the canonical/title-page witness `தேய்ந்தது போக மிச்சத்தைத் தேடுகின்றோம்!`; the contents witness `தேய்ந்ததுபோக மிச்சத்தைத் தேடுகின்றோம்!` is retained separately and not hybridized.
- Bharathi's quoted passages in Item 18 are translated as quoted verse and remain structurally distinct from Kalaignar's surrounding argument.
- `நிக்கிதோ சிந்தோ`, the Maya/Dravidian claim, and the claims attributed to Ramachandra Dikshitar are retained as source claims. No external validation or correction is introduced into the translation.
