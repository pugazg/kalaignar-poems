## Item 10 — The Ancient Tamils' International Connections!

- canonical file: `../../../sections/10.md`

- printed pages: **49–52**

### English translation

<!-- scan 50 -->

Without any evidence at all,
or setting aside the precious support
of scholars' views,
I have no habit of stubbornly insisting on anything,
of conducting an argument in arrogance.

And especially
when the subject is the Tamil language,
the Tamils,
and the history of Tamil Nadu,
I have no custom of behaving wilfully,
blurting things out,
and arguing before worthy people
as though whatever occurred to me were enough.

That is why,
gathering fitting evidence,
I have set myself to draw
the sweet history of the Tamils of that day —
who launched fine vessels,
displayed many skills,
crossed the rolling seas,
and gathered wealth.

For the growth of world civilisation,
and for contact among countries bound in relationship,
the great endeavour
of sending vessels over the ocean —

<!-- scan 51 -->

and the fame first won through it —
belonged to the Tamil;
that is a distinctive honour
befitting our worth.

Coins and gold pieces,
fine pearls, coral,
ornaments and jewels
might gather richly
from all four directions;

but not for that alone —
for the worthy exchange of goods and values
that we practised as civilisation,
there was good use too
in those world-renowned voyages of commerce
carried on through sea-going *naavaay* vessels!

The affectionate Aztec people
of Mexico in North America,
who receive with longing,
and likewise the peoples of the Maya,
and the people of the great lands living there —

long before the age of the white man,
had they not gained civilisation
and lived a good life,
with affection in their hearts toward us?

And is not Dravidian civilisation
the mother from which
Maya–Mexican civilisation arose?

Accomplished researchers of history
state this firmly;
and Tamil scholar Mathivanan
has gathered it together clearly —
I say this with gladness.

<!-- scan 52 -->

There is another piece of news too —
enough to leave us astonished and delighted!
What is it?

An ancient papyrus scroll, they say,
was found in Egypt;
instead of throwing it away asking,
“What use is this?”
Russian scholars who knew the script read it,
and later made it known to everyone.

A brave-hearted young man,
aboard what the source names compactly as a great vessel,
was caught alone at sea
in an unbearable storm wind.

In the middle of the sea the vessel overturned;
wave and wind scooped him up and flung him away.
Even then, without fear,
he gripped tightly and embraced
an orphaned log floating nearby,
and reached the shore of the surging sea.

He plucked and ate the fruits he found there,
quieted his fierce hunger,
and went to see
the king who ruled that foreign land.

At once that ruler showed him affection,
praised the young man with care,
gave him many costly objects as gifts,
and, bidding him farewell,
sent him away in another vessel.

That papyrus scroll records —
in the southern part of the vast Indian Ocean
lies a country called “Naga Nadu”;
and that it was a land connected with Tamil Nadu,
the Tamil literature of the late Sangam age

<!-- scan 53 -->

has discovered and tells us;
and that the Naga-country princess Peevalai
married a prince of our Chola country
is not merely an old story —
when we learn that history itself is there,
can there be any boundary to our joy?

Because a papyrus scroll
is evidence of the ancient civilisation of Egypt,
history, they say, explains
that the Tamils had commercial relations
with Sumeria from before 4000 BCE,
with Egypt from before 3000 BCE,
and with China from before 1200 BCE!

They also say that the word `naavaaygal`
itself later changed into `naaygal` —
so say good Tamil scholars
who know the history of our Navalandheevu!

That our people sailed ships
across the deep seas of Arabia, Egypt,
Greece and Rome,
and even beyond them —

and that this was as far back
as five thousand years ago —
is that not the pride of our civilisation,
known even then to many lands?

Papyrus = a written sheet made from paper
prepared from the aquatic plant called papyrus.

## Item 11 — Marks of Identity Here and There!

### English translation

<!-- scan 54 -->

Though he struggled and thrashed in mid-sea,
without giving way to trembling,
without a trace of anxiety
in the fierce storm and cyclone,
a young man reached shore
with the help of a piece of wood;

and afterwards, receiving the gifts
and warm-hearted praise
given by the king of Naga Nadu,
he went on his way —
I had pointed to that piece of ancient history
in the casket of time.

The moment I thought of Naga Nadu,
because the name of the woman Peevalai
had walked the great avenue of history
alongside the name of the Chola king Nedumudikilli,

I sifted and searched
the *Manimekalai* epic composed by Sattanar,
asking whether there was any evidence there;
without convening a Sangam,
testimony reached me through *sruti* — through hearing.

“Of Naga Nadu, whose ruler makes it tremble,
of victorious-spear King Valaivanan and his queen,
from the womb of that fragrant peahen-like queen
was born the maiden called Peevalai;
on the very day of her birth
a diviner foretold:
‘One of the Solar clan will touch her twin breasts;
she will come bearing his child.’”

<!-- scan 55 -->

As evidence in the epic section
*The Tale of Aputhiran Reaching the Land*:
to the ruler Valaivanan of Naga country and his queen
would be born the peacock-beautiful Peevalai;

she would enter the heaven of love
with a young prince of the Chola clan
and bear his child —
so, the poem says, astrology foretold.

After they had become husband and wife,
must astrology calculate
whether pregnancy would come?

In our country today,
is it not birth control
that families need?
Otherwise,
must we not fill in the sea itself
and turn it into land!

The famed Chola Nedumudikilli,
a loving husband,
had married Seethi, daughter of a Paanar;
for a long time he had wished
to see the festival of Poompuhar.

To see that sacred festival,
Peevalai too came one day
with her father Nagan;
the very next day, at the festival,
they saw one another —
and each stole the other's heart.

Peevalai, who had come to see the Poompuhar festival,
without worrying about whatever idle *puhar* — gossip — might arise,
fell swooning into the Chola's lap!
She sank deep,
caught in the grip of his love!

Only a mustard-seed's worth of evidence
came into my hands;
but saying that this evidence was enough
to write a continuing story,
my friend, poet Govi. Manisekaran,
has painted a captivating picture in prose.

<!-- scan 56 -->

I too read the old history there,
took from it only what we needed,
and thought with rising wonder
about the relationship
that Tamil Poompuhar had formed with Naga Nadu
four thousand years ago.

The beautiful-eyed Peevalai,
who had come to see the festival,
met eye with eye;
in a frenzy of love that closed the eyes,
the two lamps of royal lineages joined.

As the fruit of the seed thus planted,
Ilanthiraiyan at once came into the womb
of the daughter of Nagan,
as a banner of the Chola clan.

After conceiving, Peevalai,
her beauty grown fuller,
obeyed Nagan's command
and returned to her land —
with worry,
with tears!

When her son was born
and, as a little child, crawled upon his mother's lap,
Peevalai sent him by ship
to Kaveripoompattinam,
under great protection,
so that he might be joined with her husband.

By good fortune,
that child, caught in a sea-catastrophe,
escaped even when everyone else
went down with the ship;
he alone survived.

They say he was found upon a shore
with a *thondai* creeper wrapped around him,
and for that reason became Tondaiman!

He became ruler too of Kanchi,
the capital of Tondai Mandalam,
and bearing the continuing fame
of ancient history,
he was that very Tondaiman Ilanthiraiyan.

What does this ancient history explain?

From Kumari Kandam,
which became prey to the sea-catastrophe,
to the great Chola realm
on the banks of the Kaveri,

and the vast lands belonging by lineage
to the Naga people,
which vanished as victims of nature's fury —
all these had been dwelling-places
of civilised culture.

<!-- scan 57 -->

As signs of that even today,
we see the Nagercoil region;
we see marks of identity here and there
in names such as Nagapattinam,
Thirunageswaram,
and Nagavur.

When the dating is fixed
as earlier than 4000 BCE,
and when we add to that
the two thousand years of the Common Era,

we see a civilisation from six thousand years ago —
contact with foreign lands,
relations of sea trade,
love that crossed the sea
and lived across many *kaadams* of distance —

and that, for the ancient age
in which all this began,
the precious South itself stands
as the first letter, the `akaram`!

And therefore,
for those who begin to write history,
there is no need to wander here and there;
Sangam literature too is there
to dig out and offer many pieces of evidence,
like plentiful palmyra fruit —
and so we too bear
a great responsibility to contribute to that work!

## Item 12 — Valli's Marriage in the Garden of History!

### English translation

<!-- scan 58 -->

That the Avudaiyar-form is the Mother,
and the linga-form is the Father;

that this divine form belonged to Asura culture —
the scene in which two Aryans speak so
is shown by scholar Sankrityayan;

I had pointed to it in his own book
and shown you well
what I had written that day!

Making such a division —
“Aryan culture is one thing;
the culture of the Dravidians called Asuras is another” —

trying to create difference through that division,
and planning in those days
to laugh at and degrade our culture;

when they saw that this doctrine
would become a coin no longer accepted,
they sang the *sindhu*,
“Siva and Hari are one,”

and changed their approach:
the same divine form, they said,
was common to Asura and Aryan alike —
were they not those very preachers of difference?

From that day onward,
for those linga forms,
temple worship and rare festivals too
have been conducted
according to Aryan procedure
by those same men of affairs!

Those who mocked that linga-form
as uncivilised —

<!-- scan 59 -->

those who angrily called it
utter foolishness —
later approved it
and accepted the very same thing.

“That is Aryanism,”
would say scholar Anna.

After the whole world began praising
our master Valluvar
as a genius of the world of knowledge,
as the great benefactor
who spoke unfailing truth —

that crowd stamped its mark even upon him:
he too, they said,
was the son of the Aryan “Bhagavan”!

Whomever the world points to as a thinker,
whomever the earth seeks to praise
as a hero of unsurpassed courage,

they paint every one of them
with “Aryan colour”
and turn him Aryan —

how many dances never meant to be danced
have they danced to the beat of the *thappadi*,
making us their servants!

Here is one more example I shall tell.
The one shown as God,
the one they praise without cease —
from here they run carrying the spear —

and between all this
a voice kept sounding again and again:
“This Murugan alone is the Tamil god.”

From Thavathiru Adigalar
to Tamizhthiru Variyar,
none failed to raise that principle
without straying from its track!

Even that Murugan was slowly shifted from his place:
“Sit beside Mother Parvati,” they said;

and the beautiful Tamil name Murugan
was trained into Aryan Subramanian, Skandan —

<!-- scan 60 -->

trained, through Sanskrit,
in the flavour of devotion.

The cowherd-son Kannan
was called “Hare Krishna.”
That very Thirumal
was dressed in Sanskrit
and called “Mahavishnu”!

Only by immersing our culture
through language itself,
can the shaven-headed,
small-tufted clan live
by shaving us down too?

Bearing spear and bearing bow,
hunting and killing animals,
building huts in the forest
and passing their days —

the hunters of the above-mentioned
little settlement of Vallimalai
held a gathering,
chose a man named Nambirajan
as their leader,
and declared that henceforth
he was their king.

Throughout the good land of Tondai,
there was no one who failed
to praise Nambirajan.....

The maiden Valli,
born to that Nambirajan,
was a young woman of ripened age,
raised on the milk of her good mother Kodichi;

she was like a fresh shoot
from the tree of her rooted clan,
like millet flour kneaded
with honey that cannot be found even by searching.

Nambi and Kodichi appointed her
to guard the millet field there
and gave a strict command:
not for a single moment was she to sleep,
but must protect the crop
that had grown from the sweat
of the working people!

With the companions who circled around her,
Valli made her plan
to guard the millet field.

<!-- scan 61 -->

She built a high watch-platform
and, with a strong heart,
carried the tools called *thadai*, *kulir* and *thazhal*;

she drove away quail, parrots
and birds called *kavuthari*;
with sling-stones she chased off
deer, *marai*, *iralai*, elephant, boar
and other animals.

“O mynas! O doves!
O peacocks! O jewel-parrots!
O cuckoos! O *kurugu* birds!
Aalolam! Aalolam!”

Thus Valli sang
and drove them all away;
and if we look closely at Variyar's account,
told in Tamil cool
as though rolled in dew,

and gather together the thoughts
of the casket of time
that have not vanished into the wind,
we see not only a list
of the birds and animals
found then in our forests;

we are given a list as well
of foods that could satisfy hunger,
and in these we see recorded
the forest wealth of the Tamil land —
we are carried away in delight!

She stood upon the watch-platform
protecting the field,
not knowing that someone would come
to attack the fortress of her heart.

Murugan came —
and gave away his own heart;
without even realizing it,
Valli gave hers too!
What happened there?

While Valli was carrying out
the work assigned to her,
a forest hunter came running
at great speed —

<!-- scan 62 -->

“Who are you, sly girl?
Who threw a stone at me and wounded me?
Who is this forest-girl
who has made fresh blood stream?”
he cried,
showing the wound on his head
caused by the stone.

Like a bird falling into a net,
Valli fell for the beauty of his form.

Then the Sangam poem
seized command of the scene:

“What are you and I to each other?
How are my mother and yours related?
By what path did you and I come to know one another?
Like rainwater upon red earth,
our loving hearts have mingled.”

The golden leader Nambirajan
rose in anger,
scolded his golden daughter Valli,
and said:
“If you are my daughter,
forget Murugan.”

Looking all around her,
Valli said to her father:

“Father, that Murugan asked me:
‘At the sight of what are you afraid?’

I said I am afraid only when I see an elephant.
He replied:
‘That is all right —
you fear an elephant; that is natural!
Some people tremble in fear
even when a cat crosses their path.’

He mocked them so,” she said.

“What has that to do with anything now?
I am giving you an *aanai* — an order —
and here you are measuring out
*aanai* and *poonai*, elephant and cat!”

Valli explained to Nambirajan:

“*Aanai* and *poonai*
are not merely a pair of rhyming words;
those are the words with which
he drew me toward him!

<!-- scan 63 -->

Without fearing even an elephant,
people fear a cat that crosses their path —
he shines as a flame of knowledge
that mocks such ignorance.

What is wrong, father,
in giving myself as a gift
to that great light of reason?”
said Valli.

“There is nothing wrong,”
said her mother and father,
“but listen to one thing, Valli!

You said that you had given yourself
as a gift to Murugan;
that is a great wrong!

Exchange hearts with one another!
Only then will the conch of equal rights sound out,
proclaiming equality between man and woman!”

The couple said they agreed;
mother and father blessed them
and rejoiced!

*thadai* = bamboo
*kulir* = sling-stone
*thazhal* = staff
*marai* = a kind of deer
*kurugu* = heron

“Yaayum nyaayum yaar aagiyaro?” — *Kuruntokai* (Sangam literature)

## Translator notes

- Item 10 is translated as a record of the source's historical argument, not as an independent verification of the claims concerning Dravidian influence, ancient transoceanic contact, dates or trade relationships.
- Scan 52's source-supported compact form `மாக்கலத்தில்` is not silently altered in Tamil. The English reads it contextually as a vessel-setting; this interpretive choice is recorded here rather than used to repair the source.
- The source-visible broken form `குறிப்பிட்-` on scan 52 is represented by a dash in English rather than silently completed as a different Tamil reading.
- `நாவாய்கள்` / `நாய்கள்` and `நாவலந்தீவு` are kept visible through transliteration because the source itself makes a lexical/history claim about those forms.
- Item 11 retains the classical *Manimekalai* quotation as translated verse and then preserves Kalaignar's own prose explanation, family-planning satire and Peevalai–Nedumudikilli narrative rather than collapsing the two layers. The compact classical phrasing is translated conservatively in light of the poem's immediately following explanation; no new proper name is inferred from `வாச மயிலை`.
- `பூம்புகார்` / `வீண்புகார்` contains audible wordplay; the English keeps *Poompuhar* and explicitly marks the later *puhar* as gossip rather than erasing the play.
- `தொண்டைக் கொடி`, `காதம்` and `அகரம்` remain culturally/source-active as *thondai* creeper, *kaadam* and `akaram`; no outside historical correction is inserted.
- The scan-57 form `பன நுங்கு` is source-verified. English renders the contextual image as plentiful palmyra fruit, but the Tamil source is not normalized to another spelling.
- Item 12 preserves the source's Aryan/Dravidian, religious and cultural claims as Kalaignar's argument; the translation does not endorse, rebut or fact-check them inside the verse.
- `Avudaiyar`, `sindhu`, `thappadi`, `Aadigalar`/`Variyar`, `thadai`, `kulir`, `thazhal`, `marai` and `kurugu` remain visible where the argument, song or glossary depends on specifically Tamil terminology.
- The *Kuruntokai* verse is translated in the body and its source note is retained at the end of Item 12.
- The `ஆனை / பூனை / ஆணையிடுகிறேன்` sequence depends on Tamil sound-play. The English therefore retains *aanai* and *poonai* at the key moment while also giving “order / elephant / cat” so the joke remains traceable.
- The closing Valli argument is kept as an equal-rights exchange of hearts rather than modernized into terminology absent from the source.
