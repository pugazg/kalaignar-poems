## Item 43 — The Tamil Tradition of the Dravidian Race!

### English translation

<!-- scan 207 -->

Like the rays of daytime sun,
spreading wide and giving light,

were the ancient Tamil Cholas,
the Pandyas, Pallavas and Cheras —

like the plough-point that brings yield,
like the sword-point that fells the foe,

they won fame upon the earth.
Then, like the axle-pin
coming loose from a chariot,

unity across the land
fell out of joint;

they became like the thinning crescents
of a waning moon,

little chieftaincies made into topknots —
scenes like a palmyra fruit
set upon a sparrow's head —

and the “majesties” (?) fashioned
by petty rulers
whose only possession was a sceptre.

Thus, amid many divisions,
Kalabhras and Kallavars mingled,
with the confederacies
of the Three Crowned Kings and Muttaraiyars;

contradictory circumstances
butted and collided,

until at last all broke apart
into enmity, armies, war and destruction.

The condition became one
in which people might ask:

“Those who were here yesterday
are not here today —
where have those who stood with us gone?”

Though there were spear-armies,
sword-armies, elephant-armies
and cavalry,

some handed over
the work those forces had to do
to the warriors —

<!-- scan 208 -->

and ran away to wipe off sweat,
shake off weariness,
rest and enjoy pleasure:
such “noble” leaders!

Leaving them aside,
great victories came
through those who devised strategy
and acted upon it
on the field of war.

They were not kings merely in name —
they shone as emperors.

Those who think
that courage and victory
are goods sold in a shop,

and that even to obtain them
one needs divine grace,

lack firmness in their ideal;
therefore dignity, duty and discipline
fall away.

Without waiting long,
those who showed great ability
to remove all the causes
of such decline

brought, through the six hundred years
called the medieval period,

an age like the pans of a balance,
rising and falling,
up and down in turn.

The renaissance of Tamil Nadu,
which sprouted under Pallava rule
and flourished under Pandya rule,

took on a new form of history
so that the greatness of the Sangam age
might be won again.

Let us begin that history here.

Across the darkened Chola east,
the whiteness of the conch
will spread across the sky;

our history
will climb that sky
and shine!

★ ★ ★

<!-- scan 209 -->

The rule of the Three Crowned Kings,
which had once spread like a banyan tree,
began losing its fullness around CE 350.

Kalabhra rule in Chola country
continued until CE 575!
The Pandyas and Cheras too,
the source says,
lay bound beneath them.

If Pallava administration
is recorded as continuing until CE 875,
that makes six hundred dense years
of darkness for the Cholas!

It is that condition
I have shaped with a grieving heart
as today's preface.

Pandya, Chola, Pallava, Chera —
the south lost in between
the radiant age
in which they had ruled.

Only later, in CE 850,
did Vijayalaya Chola gain the chance
to sit upon the royal seat at Uraiyur!

Old history says
that at first he followed his predecessors,
remained a friend of the Pallavas
and moved in submission to them.

When the Pandya Varaguna
made war upon the Pallavas,
Vijayalaya stood
on the side of the Pallava Aparajita.

As reward,
he conquered Thanjavur,
then in the hands of the Muttaraiyars,
received it for himself,
and gave victory to the Pallavas.

The distinction of giving birth
to the Later Chola empire
belongs also to Aditya,
the Chola who fashioned a golden age.

Vijayalaya's heroic son,
Aditya Chola,
showed all his ability
in the battle of Tiruppurambiyam,
where heaven and earth seemed to shake;

<!-- scan 210 -->

all eight directions
praised the victory he won.

History then laments
that he made Aparajita turn his back,

defeated him,
took Tondai Mandalam,
and made him bow.

When self-interest
raises its head in politics,

is it not “statecraft” (?)
to leave no room
for love, friendship
or gratitude?

Here, let us see
what the “Alangadu charter” says!

Aditya had stood beside
Pallava Aparajita
against the Pandyas,

had made Thanjavur his own —

and later defeated that very Pallava
and seized Tondai country.

A Kanyakumari inscription,
the source says with tears,
adds this:

“Aditya Chola leapt upon
Aparajitavarman,
who was fighting from an elephant,

killed him,
and won the name Kodandaraman.”

To those who speak separately
of Pallava lineage,
Pandya lineage
and Chola lineage,

there is another explanatory piece of news,
with an inscription as witness:

when Aditya Chola's wife
is described as
“a maiden who came from the Pallava lineage,”

should not clarity arise
in the minds of historians
that every lineage
of the Dravidian race
is Tamil lineage?

## Item 44 — The Iron Pillar and the Wings of Flies!

- stable item: **44**
- canonical Tamil title: `இரும்புத் தூணும் ஈக்களின் இறகும்!`
- contents witness: `இரும்புத் தூணும், ஈக்களின் இறகும்!`
- canonical file: `../../../sections/44.md`
- physical scans: **211–215**
- printed pages: **210–214**
- title-witness status: **documented discrepancy — contents witness adds a comma after `தூணும்`; canonical/title-page witness does not**

### English translation

<!-- scan 211 -->

The iron pillar
and the wings of flies!

In the age of Aditya Chola,
the one who ascended the throne
as his son
was Parantaka I.

He had twelve wives —
and their sons were:

Rajaditya, Gandaraditya,
Arikulakesari, Uttamasili
and Arinjaya.

Of these,
Rajaditya died
in the battle of Takkolam;

therefore Gandaraditya,
who came after him,
received the crown.

He married Sembiyan Mahadevi,
daughter of Malavaraiyan,

and had Uttama Chola as his son.
That son also had another name:
Madhurantakan.

When Gandaraditya died,
Madhurantakan was still a child;

so his younger brother Arinjaya
took responsibility for rule.

But he too
met an untimely death.

Arinjaya's son,
Parantaka II,
next came to the throne.

He was granted
the distinction *Rajakesari*.
Though still young,
in war —

<!-- scan 212 -->

he faced Veera Pandya
at a place called Sevur,
won,

and, says the Thiruvalangadu copper plate,
sent the Pandya to the land of death;
that charter sings his praise.

Among the wives of Parantaka II,
two are especially mentioned.

One was Vanavan Mahadevi,
daughter of the Malaiyaman.

She was the one
who mounted the funeral pyre
with her husband.

She was not one
who deceived anyone by saying,
“I thought I would mount it.”

We too say
that such a custom
does not accord with rational thought;

then, now, and always,
we have never supported it.

Before Vanavan Mahadevi
and her life-partner Parantaka II
lost their lives,

Rajaraja —
the Chola prince,
pure gold,
who would become a cause
of the Chola empire's rise —

sat upon the lap
of his mother Vanavan Mahadevi
and played by whirling a sword.

Another wife of Parantaka II
was Cheran Mahadevi;

the town named after her
is “Cheranmadevi”!

The jewel-womb of Vanavan Mahadevi,
mother of Rajaraja,
Aditya Karikalan
and Kundavai Nachiyar,

was delighted to see
the Chola tiger-flag
fly across the world.

Knowing that the strength
of her own children
was the cause,

she became the very grammar
of the Kural:

“Greater than at the moment of birth
is the mother's joy
when she hears her son called a worthy man” —

<!-- scan 213 -->

and that mother
stood amazed at herself!

Vanavan Mahadevi's eldest son
was Aditya Karikalan.

Yet while his father,
Parantaka II,
was still alive,

he became the victim
of a criminal conspiracy —

a consequence of the question:
who had the right to rule the land?

Once Aditya Karikalan was killed,
Rajaraja had the right
to come next to the throne
and be crowned according to order.

So a slander arose against Rajaraja:
that he himself,
eager to rule,
had arranged the conspiracy
and committed the killing.

That accusation vanished at once,
like dew before the sun;

but the country,
the source says,
came to understand what had happened.

In truth,
the deceased son of Parantaka II,
Aditya Karikalan,
had been the one entitled to rule.

But Madhurantakan,
who had received only the title
“Uttama Chola,”

regarded taking life
as though it were a game
and, utterly unlike his own name,

completed a violent conspiracy
one day, it is said.

He became an example
of how, for some,
name and deed do not match —

and then joined
the assembly of the “worthy”!

<!-- scan 214 -->

There are also those
who construct another account:

that the charge upon Uttama Chola
was empty blame,

and that his own close relatives
were responsible
for Aditya Chola's murder.

Whatever caused
Aditya Chola's end,

whoever carried out
the conspiracy may remain a mystery;

but no one who argued against it,
the source says,
denied that conspiracy
had indeed taken his life.

Those who had thought,
“Now Rajaraja will be king,”

those who had immersed themselves
in sweet dreams,
reeled in disappointment.

But Rajaraja calmed everyone.
Though he himself suspected
whether Madhurantakan too
had a role in Aditya Karikalan's death,

he let that very paternal uncle
hold the sceptre.

See, then,
how fitting the name
“Arulmozhi Devan”
was for him!

Thus Aditya's grave itself
became the throne
upon which Madhurantakan
sat wearing the crown.

Yes — conspiracy had won!

The innocent people murmured,
“Alas — fate has won!”

and shed floods of tears.

Yet through all this,
not even a sesame-point of disturbance
stirred Rajaraja's heart.

Will an iron pillar
break merely because
the wings of flies strike it?

Rajaraja took responsibility
with this vow:

“I will remain prince
and watch over this government.”

And every step
his paternal uncle Madhurantaka took —

<!-- scan 215 -->

he watched
without allowing his eyes
to tire even for a moment.

Had he not fully understood
his uncle?

Had that uncle not,
it is said,
planned to make the crown his own
and brought Aditya Chola's life
to an end?

Thus, like the saying
“Murugan seated upon the peacock,”

Rajaraja sat
in the responsibility of prince,
while in his mind
Madhurantaka Chola himself
was that peacock beneath him.

When even great families
see contests
for leadership of a household,

could there be no contest
for leadership of a vast empire?

Rajaraja handled precisely that
through intelligence and tact,

through faith in the maxim
“those who endure shall rule the earth,”

and, above all,
through fear that confusion
inside the palace
would open the way
for outsiders to enter.

Even in youth
he understood the proprieties,
conducted himself accordingly —

and later
he ruled the country!

## Item 45 — Father Rajaraja and the Son Who Captivated Hearts!

### English translation

<!-- scan 216 -->

Father Rajaraja,
and the son who captivated hearts!

After the terrifying history
written in blood —
“the murder of Aditya Karikalan” —

while the sorrowful background music
still sounded
from Sundara Chola,
Parantaka II,

broken by love for his child
dying in the “Golden Palace of Kanchi,”

and while the causes
behind those tragic events
still loomed like giant question marks,

Madhurantakan ascended
the seat of rule
in CE 970.

One claim said
that he too had been a cause
of Aditya Karikalan's murder;

another denied
that he could have been.

While these remained
matters of argument
upon the pages of history,

an inscription at Kattumannarkudi,
the source says with tears,
lines up the names
of six said to have carried out
the treacherous deed:

Panchavan,
Brahmadhirajan,
Parameswaran,
Devadasan,
Soman,
and Ravidasan;

and identifies four among them
as Aiyars.

<!-- scan 217 -->

Since many scholars who researched it
offer differing pieces of information,

let us too recover ourselves
from the shock
of Aditya Karikalan's murder

and move forward
to the wonder
of how Arulmozhi Varman,
Rajaraja,
turned the wheel of government.

Let us learn
how he built
a golden-age empire.

If Madhurantakan,
called Uttama Chola,
truly had been responsible
for Aditya Karikalan's murder,

then once he established his rule,
would he not have appointed
his own son,

instead of declaring
Arulmozhi Rajaraja
crown prince?

So asks the Tamil Nadu History Committee.

But if Madhurantakan
had done exactly that —

would not the public then have said:

“He made Arulmozhi Varman prince
only to conceal
the great murder he committed;
that too is a stratagem”?

Can we not therefore also infer
that, as an eye-wiping drama,

he set aside his own son
and made Arulmozhi crown prince?

As though his dream itself
had come true,

Uttama Chola obtained
the office he valued as life itself —

and obtained as its shield
Rajaraja and his kin.

<!-- scan 218 -->

It was historian
Nilakanta Sastri,
the source says,
who began the argument
that Brahmins had treacherously
murdered Aditya Karikalan.

Rejecting that,
Sadasiva Pandarathar
cites evidence
that Madhurantakan was innocent.

The evidence both present
consists of inscriptions
and copper plates.

Let us move those witnesses
a little aside,
stand them outside,

and give as our judgment
the release granted
in a climate of doubt.

Then let us follow Rajaraja —

the golden son,
the lion-like Chola,
who established,
the source says,
a Tamil empire
not merely over the south
but across the Indian land.

We can then investigate
how he stabilised his rule —

and know it
with boundless delight.

While Rajaraja
was serving as crown prince,

Madhurantakan trained him
in every art:

administration,
political strategy,

how to embrace those who came near,
and how to draw into his own fold
those who stood in his way.

Perhaps,
the source suggests,
this too may have been
an act seeking atonement
for a wrong committed.

“Let us forget enmity,
let us cast away hostility,
let us strengthen Chola Mandalam.”

<!-- scan 219 -->

With such words turned into action,
with no enemy able to stand against him,

“Climb, climb!” —
advancing only toward
future victories,

Rajaraja took the crown
on the twenty-fifth day
of June, CE 985.

Before coming to the throne,
Rajaraja had already received
sixteen years of training
in administration
as crown prince.

If we calculate his age
from the year
in which he was crowned,

he began direct rule
at roughly forty years of age.

After that,
he ruled for another thirty years.

His son Rajendra too,
the source says,
ascended the throne
only at the age of fifty,
in the year 1012.

While father Rajaraja
was still alive,
Rajendra Chola —
the son who captivated
the minds of the Tamils —
became king.

History raises its praise to the sky
and bows before the claim

that the glory
of the sceptre in the father's hand
had spread northward
as far as the Krishna River;

that to the west
the waves of the Arabian Sea,

and to the south
the island of Lanka,

bowed before
the rule of these two!

## Translator notes

- Item 43 translates its political and dynastic chronology as Kalaignar's source argument. The CE 350/575/850/875 sequence, Vijayalaya–Aparajita relationship, Tiruppurambiyam narrative, Alangadu-charter framing and Kanyakumari-inscription claim are not independently corrected in the translation.
- Item 43 retains the rhetorical question mark attached to `மாட்சி` as “majesties” (?) and keeps the source's `கண்ணியம், கடமை, கட்டுப்பாடு` triad as “dignity, duty and discipline.”
- Item 44 preserves both title witnesses separately. The displayed English title follows canonical `இரும்புத் தூணும் ஈக்களின் இறகும்!`; the contents witness with the comma after `தூணும்` remains metadata only.
- The scan-211 source form `அவ்வர் மகன்கள்` is part of the final-cleared Tamil source. The English renders the contextual sense without altering that Tamil form.
- Item 44 preserves Kalaignar's explicitly stated rationalist rejection of the funeral-pyre custom, his accusation/counter-accusation structure around Aditya Karikalan's murder, the irony around “Uttama Chola,” the “Arulmozhi Devan” contrast, the iron-pillar/fly-wing image and the Murugan/peacock analogy. These claims and suspicions are translated as source claims, not historical adjudication.
- Item 45 retains the source's competing historical arguments rather than choosing between them: Madhurantakan is presented through accusation, denial, counter-inference and benefit-of-doubt language exactly as the argumentative movement requires.
- Names, dates and figures — including CE 970, CE 985, sixteen years, roughly forty years, thirty further years, CE 1012 and age fifty — remain source-traceable and are not reconciled against outside chronology.
- The Kattumannarkudi-inscription list and the source's identification of four named figures as `அய்யர்` are translated as claims attributed to the source; no outside classification has been added.
- No final-cleared Tamil canonical file or verified page record was modified while preparing this batch.
