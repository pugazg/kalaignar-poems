### English translation

<!-- scan 124 -->

Kannagi, they say,
was the very form of grief —
one who knew not even
a mustard-seed's measure of joy in life;

weariness, pain, sorrow,
suffering and anguish —
apart from these,
she knew nothing at all
of the happiness of married life,
right to the end.

Kovalan alone, they say,
swam and rejoiced with Madhavi
in a waterfall of pleasure,
ceaselessly tasting
the sweetness of kisses
in which lips clung together;

and Madhavi alone —
waist swaying,
gait swaying,
her twin fruits swaying —

tasted the delight of desire herself
and gave herself like a tender creeper
so that her lover too
might taste until his longing was quenched;

she alone, we men and women alike imagine,
was the woman who performed
such a great *tapas* of pleasure.

This is the stain produced
by the poison mixed into nectar
by those who turned literature into puranic tale:

Kannagi made into a doll
that stays behind and then comes forward,
her two eyes always swollen
from weeping and lamentation;

rising only after worshipping her husband,
drawing the threshold design,
sweeping the house —

nothing but a bonded drudge
inside Kovalan's home;

was there even a single moment left for Kannagi
to be caressed,
to mingle in love,
to come together?

<!-- scan 125 -->

In those days,
all the “Jains” who watched
the *Sampoorna Kovalan* drama
would become distressed;

among them,
we have seen women in particular
watching with great sighs.

Yet *Silappathikaram* itself
stands before us as the proper witness
that all such stories
are wrong from the root!

Did Kannagi not come together
with her bridegroom Kovalan
and rejoice in the bedchamber?

Did those young doves
never engage at all
in the task of seeking medicine
for the disease of desire?

Only after eight years
of married life, it is said,
did Kovalan leave Kannagi and depart the house —

this is no fabricated tale;
Dr. Su. Ve. Subramanian
has pointed it out
in a work of commentary.

Ilango Adigal,
the great one who gave us the epic,
extols the loving pleasure
of Kannagi and Kovalan

as though squeezing sugarcane for its juice,
sprinkling powdered rock-candy upon it,
and adding the blended flavour
of the three fruits until it melts and overflows —

so does he celebrate it
in *Silappathikaram*!

Like a new beauty
in which *puram* song and *akam* song mingle,
Kannagi, a new marvel of beauty,
and Kovalan, drunk with love,

embrace,
rejoice,
and lie forgetting themselves.
See the *venba*
that shows us that scene:

“Like two smoke-dark serpents
joining and steeping into one,
they joined hands
as lovers, husband and wife;

all pleasures whose names do not fade
they drew close and enjoyed upon the earth,
as though they had seen
that nothing here endures.”

<!-- scan 126 -->

Like enraged snakes
clasping tight and immersing together,
like Kama and Rathi,
Kovalan and Kannagi mingled;

thinking upon impermanence —
as though asking whether this world
would even exist tomorrow —

they tasted that very day
all the pleasures there were to experience
and rejoiced,
says this *venba*.

After this,
can anyone still say
that Kannagi knew no joy
of married life?

“O flawless gold!
O pearl of the right-turning conch!
O fragrance without blemish!
O sugarcane! O honey!
O gem not born within a mountain!
O nectar not born amid the waves!”

Kovalan praised Kannagi
with words such as these;
would he not have swept her into his arms,
seated her upon his lap,
and rejoiced in loving play?

Would he have stood far away,
like a poet upon the earth
gazing at the moon in the sky,
and merely showered words at her?

Kannagi too,
leaning upon Kovalan's shoulder,
would surely have listened and savoured
those rich words
that make the heart melt.

After the two had joined
and steeped together like intertwined serpents,
what other act remains to be described?

Kovalan breaks away
from that union
because another companion
comes into his life.

The beautiful Kannagi lies withering — yes:
flawless gold, upon the earth;
a right-spiralled pearl,
upon the wound of marriage.

Hearing of Kannagi's condition —

<!-- scan 127 -->

her friend Devanthi,
an Aryan woman,
wife of one called Sathan,
comes to console her.

“Do not grieve, Kannagi.
Where the Kaveri joins the sea,
in the grove-land of the *neithal* tract,
there are two ponds,
Somakundam and Suryakundam.

If you immerse yourself in them
and worship Manmatha
at the temple there,
your man will return at once,”
she said.

Though the Aryan woman
was indeed her dear friend,
Kannagi of Poompuhar rejected that path
because Tamil culture did not accept it,

and answered sharply:
“That is no distinction!”

Though such details
may not now circulate widely
enough to enter our ears,

they are unshakable evidence
of the emphasis Kannagi placed upon culture
and of her attachment to it!

★     ★     ★

“Those women of the world
who immerse themselves
at Somakundam and Suryakundam,
worship at the shrine of Kama,
and rejoice with their husbands,
will be born again even in the world of pleasure.

‘Let us go bathe there one day,’
said the ornamented woman;
that jewelled woman replied,
‘It is no distinction,’ and remained...”

(*Silappathikaram* — *Kanaaththiram Uraiththa Kaathai*)

**Note:**
“saying that immersing at a sacred ford
and worshipping a deity
is not our custom” —
(this is Adiyarkku Nallar's commentary,
as found in the edition published by
U. Ve. Swaminatha Iyer.)

## Item 26 — Awake; Here Is the Dawn of a Classical Language!

### English translation

<!-- scan 128 -->

No one has written
the history of Tamil Nadu in full
and left it behind for us.

As a spectacle found
in no other country of the world,
we have had to read our ancient history

in palm-leaf literature,
in an inscription carved by some king,
in a story told by some passer-by,

in an investigation conducted
by gathering bones,
in the skull of a body
placed long ago inside a burial urn...

Only through lines and traces
discovered from such things
could the history of antiquity be read.

From them we could infer
art, civilisation and life;
we could learn the pattern of the age —

but from the Stone Age
through the ripening course of time
to the present,
has not the domination of darkness remained?

★     ★     ★

Without knowing
how our ancestors lived
in each age,

we would have stumbled about,
unable to inscribe
the greatness of this sacred land
upon the register of the world;

we would have lain soaked,
like a matchstick useless
for lighting the lamp of history.

Flies have no teeth —
do they not swarm upon food?

<!-- scan 129 -->

Crocodiles have no tongue —
do they never satisfy their hunger?

A crab has no head;
a frog has no neck.

When well-frogs
that know nothing of the country's abundance,
and headless trunks like crabs,
can go on living,

what harm, after all,
would come to us or to the country
if we alone lived without “history”?

If one argues that it is perfectly proper
to live without head,
without neck,
without teeth,
without tongue,

then surely it is fitting too
for the Tamil to live without “history”!
Do we not set our own standards
and qualifications for ourselves?

Even if some Tamil men
have prepared themselves
to live without self-respect,
no Tamil woman has ever beaten time
to the destruction of Tamil!

As fitting evidence,
I shall give you a list to see.

A mother who had lost
her father,
her husband,
and her brother in war,

called back even the child
who had gone to school,
placed in his hand
a spear leaping to rise,

and sent him away saying:
“Go, my son, toward the battle-front;
go and conquer the enemy!”

Was it not a woman,
Masathiyar,
who sang this?
And was not the woman sung of
herself one born of a lineage of honour?

To the man who asked,
“Mother, where is your son?”

she answered that he had gone
to the battle-front:

“I do not know exactly
where the tiger has gone;
but here is the cave
where the tiger once lay” —

<!-- scan 130 -->

and she pointed
to the belly that had carried him.
Was it not a woman poet
who sang of such a mother?

★

Though there were other young men
like him,
the chieftain held an undiminished love
for his own son;

Avvai shows us a mother
who worries:
why does that son,
entering the field
and waging fierce battle,
not die there,
not mount the warrior's cot,
not go to the grave,
but linger on?

She too was a woman —
and was it not this very soil
that gave birth to both of them?

“We did not bury our children;
we have sown those warriors” —

those who uttered vows
in sword-ringing words
were women too!

The Tamil woman
who drove away a tiger with a war-drum,
hearing the false report
that “your son fell in battle
showing his back,”

wailed in anguish
and vowed:
“I shall cut away the breast
from which that son drank milk!”

She went to the battlefield,
learned the truth,
and returned.
Who made this into poetry for us?
Was it not Kakkaipadiniyar?

More than thirty women poets,
it is said,
lived in the old days,
shining upon this earth
like *paththarai maatru* gold,

so that the three crowned Tamil kings
themselves stood amazed.

The songs written
by those jewels among women
are themselves pages
of the history of Tamil Nadu.

I give you their names —
not so that grandmothers
who call poetry “song”
may merely swell with pride,

<!-- scan 131 -->

but so that the grandsons and granddaughters
yet to come
may become diamond-hearted warriors,
men and women alike!

★     ★     ★

Here they are:

Avvaiyar, Allur Nanmullaiyar,
Kakkaipadiniyar, Kavarp Penn,
Nakkannaiyar, Vennikkuyathiyar,
Kuramagal Ilaveyini, Kuriyeyini,
Thayang Kanni, Poonganuttraiyar,
Ponmudiyar, Ponthai Pasalaiyar,
Boothapandiyan Devi, Perungoppendu,
Perungozhi Naykan's daughter Nakkannaiyar,
the daughters of Pari, Peymagal Ilaveyini,
Okkur Masathiyar, Nal Velliyar,
Aathimandhiyar, Nettimaiyar,
Nappasalaiyar, Velli Veethiyar,
Kamakkani Pasalaiyar,
Kazharkkiran Eyittriyar,
Kachchipettu Nannagaiyar,
Oonpithaiyar, Marpithiyar,
Poongan Uttraiyar,
Veripaadiya Kamak Kanniyar,
Anji Aththai's daughter Nagaiyar.

★     ★     ★

The literature created
in cascading Tamil
by women and men
who became rare poets —

these are the sources
that teach the earliest history
of the Tamils;

the letters carved in stone
in those ancient days
are indestructible witnesses

to our knowledge,
our power,
our love,
our valour,
and our culture.

Come — let us broaden that work;
awake and rise:
here is the dawn
of the “Classical Language”!

## Item 27 — It Will Surely Be Opened, to Show the Way!

### English translation

<!-- scan 132 -->

As our journey through history
continues without faltering in its step,
let us sit here for a while
and speak of this very matter.

I have been opening and showing
the treasures packed inside the Chest of Time
with the Key of Poetry;

and while we remain immersed
in a joy touched with anxiety,
wondering whether scenes seen in dreams
will return again to the screen of memory,

we also carry an expectation:
not merely that they return to memory,
but that they stand firm
as history happening before us!

That is why,
up to this point,
I have fashioned twenty-six poems;

I have spoken of the time
when human beings appeared,
and of the time before that;

of the ways in which all living beings arose,
and of stage-by-stage development;

and through the thousands upon thousands of years
that followed one after another,
I have set before you
the friendship Tamil Nadu held with many lands,
its civilisational contact,
its bonds of art,

its clasp of culture,
its sea-trade,
its opportunities to meet many battlefields —

the fruit-sweetnesses
and the bitter tablets alike
that the treasury yields.

But do not rush to the conclusion
that I, your humble servant,
can offer all this
because of some extraordinary scholarship
or because I have attained
the eight great *siddhis*!

<!-- scan 133 -->

I gather evidence.
As the basic labour of that effort,
I honour the books
of my friend Mathivanan —
like a jewel of Kannan.

How many quotations he himself supplies!
How many views
of researchers and learned scholars!

Though I have taken many of them
and filled this chest,
from the very beginning of my giving,
it is his thought
that has stood as the companion
that never leaves my side.

To string jewels,
do we not need a thread of gold?
I have joined the views
of other learned people as well
to that very thread.

Thus far,
from the sprouting of life upon our earth,
we have also seen
that here, in this sacred land of India,
there was Dravidian history
before the coming of the Aryans;

that in the north,
in the Deccan,
in the Indus and Ganges plains,
settlement-cities were laid out in ordered form
even before a thousand years BCE;

that forests were cleared
and turned into settled lands,
cities rich in civilisation were built,
and in structural and architectural arts
the Dravidian Tamils possessed
a skill that astonished those who saw it.

The Aryans, enraged at seeing this,
called the Dravidians “Dasyus”
with hostile intent:

“Those Dasyus' palaces
shine in beauty
with gold and gems;
their mighty forts

<!-- scan 134 -->

and their walled defences
that touch the sky
captivate the mind.

The potter called Kovan
has two life-companions;
and because they bathe in milk —

sinners!
May they fall into the river Sipa!”

Thus, says the source,
does the Aryans' *Rig Veda* curse them.
When this is examined,
it is finally determined,
without even the smallest room for doubt,
that Indian civilisation
before the Aryan arrival
belonged to speakers of Dravidian languages.

In this manner —
a developed civilisation,
a way of life,
trade relations with the countries of the world,
an awareness of art,
bonds of love,

meeting the battlefield,
guarding honour,
honouring humaneness,
removing religious divisions
that breed strife —

this epic chest is formed
as a marvellous treasury
filled with many kinds of jewelled ornaments.

Whenever I open
this epic chest
with the Key of Poetry,

because what we see inside
are the jewels called
the histories of Dravidians and Tamils,

do not therefore think
that this is a political chest.

Inscribe it in your hearts
as a chest of history
that stands beyond politics —

beginning not with
the dawn of living beings,
but even before that dawn,

with the roaming
of the world itself
and the other planets —

<!-- scan 135 -->

how many peoples,
what kinds of minds;

how many kings,
what kinds of governments —

what ended;
what did not end!

what dawned;
what did not dawn!

what bore fruit;
what did not bear fruit!

what bent;
what did not bend!

Not from the Himalayas
to the Kaveri —

but from the banks of the Kaveri
to the banks of the Ganges,

across that long-stretched land,
we shall tell the chronicles
of those who finished their reigns,

and the histories
of those who finished their lives —

so that those yet to live
and those yet to rule
may be shown the way.

For that purpose,
the Chest of Time
will surely continue
to be opened
with the Key of Poetry!

## Translator notes

- Item 25 preserves the title-page/contents punctuation discrepancy exactly: the canonical displayed title has **no final exclamation mark**, while the contents witness does.
- Item 25 translates Kalaignar's argument against the popular sorrow-only image of Kannagi without sanitising its erotic vocabulary. The swaying-waist / “twin fruits” image, lovers' bedchamber questions, serpent imagery, Kovalan's praise and the Kannagi–Kovalan union remain visible because they are integral to the source's corrective argument.
- The Item 25 *venba* is rendered for sense in the flow of Kalaignar's own explanation. It is not presented as an independent critical translation of the classical poem.
- `பீடு அன்று` is rendered **“It is no distinction”**, preserving the source's ethical rejection rather than turning it into a modern doctrinal paraphrase. The concluding Adiyarkku Nallar / U. Ve. Swaminatha Iyer source note remains separate and visible.
- Item 26 preserves the canonical title punctuation: **no comma after “Here”** in the title-page witness; the contents witness's added comma remains separately recorded.
- Item 26 retains the source's satire built from toothless flies, tongueless crocodiles, headless crabs and neckless frogs, as well as its repeated argument that a people without history would be analogous to those bodily absences.
- `பத்தரை மாற்றுப் பொன்` is retained as ***paththarai maatru* gold** rather than replaced by an invented modern purity figure. Its function in context is a high-purity/praise metaphor for the women poets.
- The complete women-poet list on scan 131 is retained in source order. Names are transliterated for traceability rather than silently standardised to one external scholarly spelling system.
- Item 27 preserves the source's self-positioning: Kalaignar explicitly denies that the work rests on extraordinary scholarship or *ashtama-siddhi* and identifies evidence-gathering and Mathivanan's books as the foundation. The gold-thread/jewel metaphor is kept intact.
- The Dravidian/Aryan, Dasyu, *Rig Veda*, settlement and chronology statements in Item 27 are translated as the claims made by the source. This English batch does **not** independently verify, reconcile or correct those historical claims.
- The repeated `முடிந்தது / முடியாதது`, `விடிந்தது / விடியாதது`, `விளைந்தது / விளையாதது`, `வளைந்தது / வளையாதது` contrasts are preserved as active parallel rhetoric rather than compressed into prose.
- No final-cleared Tamil canonical file was modified while preparing this batch.
