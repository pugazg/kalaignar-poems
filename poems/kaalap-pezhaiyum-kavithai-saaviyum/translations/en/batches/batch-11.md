## Item 28 — The Ancient Civilisation That Spread Across the Whole World!

### English translation

<!-- scan 136 -->

The ancient civilisation
that spread across the whole world!

As sandalwood gives out fragrance
the more it is rubbed —
as a diamond throws out light
the more its facets are polished —

only by speaking again and again
of the antiquity of the Dravidian people
will coconut saplings rise like teak trees!

Banana saplings will grow like *vaagai* trees!
The moringa bud will become jasmine;
on the thorn, a rose will bloom!

Can a screw-pine frond
be stroked against its grain?
Yet the Tamil alone
can be trampled down and pushed aside!

The Bengali poet Tagore,
who gave the national anthem,
says:

“it was the prosperity and strength
given by the lion-like Dravidians
that removed the weakness
of Aryan civilisation
and kept its foundation from shaking.”

The Soviet linguist “Sagirap,”
who compares and arranges
the Dravidian languages of North India
with the Dravidian languages of the South,
argues forcefully and establishes
that the Tamils spread northward
from the South.

We know that Tamils conquered northern lands;
do we know that Tamil itself went there?

“On the contrary linguistic data indicate that the
Dravidian Languages spread from South to North
rather than from North to South”

<!-- scan 137 -->

G. Zograf
Soviet Indologist in a
Survey of Languages of
India, Pakistan, Ceylon and Nepal.

Even today in Kashmir,
the language spoken by some hill people
has, according to a Kannada historian,
been identified as one of the branch languages
of the Dravidian language family;
this is no empty imagination!

Among the Kurukkar
who live in Bihar's Rajmahal hills,
the folk songs and ancient stories
widely used by them

reveal that they were Dravidian people
who had lived in the far South;
no one has drawn a curtain over that testimony!

Professor S. K. Chatterji says
that the sari and the veshti —
marks of Indian civilisation —
are gifts of the gracious Dravidian civilisation,

in his book *Indo-Aryan and Hindu* (p. 134).

Mother-goddess worship
in the “Harappa” excavations;
a temple to Siva
on the banks of America's Colorado River;

the resemblance of the Adichanallur “skull”
to those of the Indus region;
and the findings of Harappa and Mohenjo-daro —

we shall call all these firm evidence
that even then Dravidian civilisation
held an “original” right in many lands
and spread far and wide.

The worship of the Siva-linga,
which the Aryans mocked,
was itself part of the culture
of the Dravidians they called “Asura”!

Among the world's ancient civilisations,
Professor “Kondratov” of the Soviet land
finds and declares
that the true division would be:

one share in three
to other peoples,
and two shares in three
to the Dravidians.

<!-- scan 138 -->

Following the history
that Australia and Africa
were in those days joined
to Kumari Kandam,

it is also said
that worship of God
and the introduction of weapons
to encircle and defeat the foe
formed a common civilisation
that had begun among the Dravidians
five thousand years ago.

Historians praise
the Egyptians' distinctiveness
in the system of writing,
then ask how they suddenly
came by such distinction;

and say that it was a gift
bestowed upon them by Tamils
through commercial contact!

In an ancient story
said to have been written
by a Babylonian priest,

one called “Onasu,”
a good man who taught civilisation
to the Mesopotamians,

came, surrounded by a well-trained group,
to the city called “Eridu”
and taught civilisation there —

giving them ploughing implements,
and teaching science and art,
architecture,
and the worship of God.

Because this is what is told,
the answer offered
for how that civilisation spread
not little by little,

but like a ball
that leaps the instant it is struck,
is this:
through the relationship
with the Tamils of Kumari Kandam!

That continent too,
even then,
became prey
to the vast mouth of the deep sea —

<!-- scan 139 -->

but afterwards as well,
the Dravidian cultural people
who had lived there
became the great people
of the whole Indian land!

From that mighty tree
of a great people,

many worthy peoples
such as the Tamil people,
and many languages of distinction,
flourished as branches of renown;

with worthy Tamil poets,
and the patrons praised by those poets —
kings who ruled the earth —

with the wealth of the lands
those kings ruled,
and with strength multiplied
to increase that wealth;

with warriors like striped tigers
who lived a life worthy of the world's praise,
and with the heroic Tamil clans
who came in their line —

how did they toss enemy countries
about like balls?
How did they raise valour
so that songs of victory could be sung?

From where are we to seek
and bring all the evidence for these things?

“The proper age for that
is the Sangam age,”
the learned will say;
I know it!

Even in fixing the dates of that age
there are differences of opinion;
yet, according to the evidence shown
by the Tamil scholar Raghava Iyengar,

if we take the fourth century BCE
as the beginning,

then, just as from the east
we name directions
as west, south and north,

we have begun
with the three crowned kings —
Chera, Chola and Pandya;

let us continue in that way,
without faltering midway!

## Item 29 — Mother, Give Us — Bear Us Treasures of Self-Respect!

### English translation

<!-- scan 140 -->

Mother, give us —
bear us treasures of self-respect!

At the very sound
of the place-name “Talaiyalanganam,”
there rises not merely the memory
of “Talaiyalangadu” in the Thanjavur land;

there rises too
the historical memory
of the soil upon which
one of the greatest battles
of the Sangam literary age was fought.

Those who stood
and fought on both fronts
were themselves
people of this sacred Tamil land.

The second Nedunchezhiyan —
a youth not yet of age —
did not tremble at war;
he came to the battlefield!

Two great kings:
the warriors of the Chera and Chola lines;
with them five Velir chiefs
with great armies,
and warriors beyond number.

The peerless Chera warrior Irumporai,
and the Chola Killivalavan,
strong enough to meet the oncoming foe;

and five enraged Velir leaders —
Venman, Porunan, Ezhini,
Erumaiyuran and Thithiyan —
with their armies.

They thought Nedunchezhiyan
would be stunned at the sight;

that, as though it were a child's game,
they could win a great victory
at the battle-front.

What became of those stout men
who came with such thoughts?
They accepted defeat with dignity —

<!-- scan 141 -->

“We must have done good merit;
we have been reborn,
and we are leaving the battle-front
still alive!” they said,

and reached their own lands
and their own homes.
On the field the Pandyas
spoke to one another with their eyes:

“Even if that fellow called Yama
were to perform penance for long,
he could not defeat
our Nedunchezhiyan!”

★ ★ ★

On the battlefield,
or on the sea called life,

those who possess the resolve
to board the boat called valour
and travel with courage as their oar —

like Nedunchezhiyan of Talaiyalanganam —
need not perform penance for a boon;

as long as Tamil lives,
they too can live!

Stroking his tender fingers,
kissing his broad shoulders,
Tamil poets blessed him.

The Tamil land itself
raised its head and blessed him!

Nakkirar and Mangudi Maruthanar,
lions among the foremost poets,
created *Nedunalvadai* and *Maduraikkanchi*,
works that live like epic songs
fragrant with art.

A day will come
to celebrate that sacred day of creation —
so this Tamil land itself
is said to have blessed them
with all its heart;
and it blesses them even now!

The *Puram* poet Kudapulaviyanar,
when praising this battle,
records that

though Tamils stood arrayed
on both sides of the battlefield,
and the forces of the enemy Erumaiyur
were mixed among them,

<!-- scan 142 -->

Pandya Nedunchezhiyan
won a victory praised by the world;

he made the enemy writhe
like worms on a fishing hook,
loaded defeat upon their shoulders
and drove them away!

He raised the fish flag
high into the sky.

He took into his own hand
the flag of Tamil honour.

When he left for the battle-front
he was a tiger cub —

on the battlefield,
the enemy before him
was only a kitten!

And so,
how does the lion among poets,
Idaikkunrur Kizhar,
sing of this with rising fire?

That is the sight
we must see here,
the splendour
we must rejoice in!

“He whose feet cast off the tinkling anklet
and put on the bright warrior's anklet;
whose brow cast off the child's topknot
and was garlanded with fresh neem
and the long-bannered *uzhinjai*;
whose hand cast off the little bracelet,
grasped the bow,
and stood radiant
at the front of the long chariot —
who is he?”

(*Purananuru* 77)

That is to say:

the little child
who played wearing tinkling anklets
untied them,

put on the warrior's anklet,
removed the small topknot
from his head,

wore the neem garland
and with it
the garland of battle,

<!-- scan 143 -->

and in the hand
that had worn the child's protective bracelet,
held arrow and bow
and stood at the front of the chariot —

that child was
Pandya Nedunchezhiyan,
says the lion among poets!

Is that all?

The Pandya,
like the waxing moon,
utters a vow as well;

his own *Puram* poem,
a song of uprising —
come, let us hear it!

“Those who praise the land
laugh and say,
‘He is young!’ ...

Those angry kings
who speak such belittling words —
if I do not strike them
and shatter the hard battle,

and seize them together
with their war drums,

may those who live
under my shelter,
unable to find another shade,
spread tears and cry,
‘Cruel is our king!’

may I become a ruler
whom the people condemn! ...

may the poets cease
to sing within the bounds
of my land! ...

may the disgrace be mine
of having nothing to give
to those who come in need!”

(*Purananuru* 72)

That is to say:

I shall scatter the enemies
and silence their drums!

If I do not,
let this world place me
on the list of tyrants!

Let the low word strike me:
“he gives nothing
to those who ask!”

<!-- scan 144 -->

Let none of these poets
raise me up in song!

As the Tamil Pandya line
once gave us
such a youth of honour,
such a son of the warrior clan,

let us ask Tamil Mother
to let us behold such sons
throughout this Tamil land!

Mother — give us,
bear us such treasures
of self-respect!

## Item 30 — The Measure of His Power: His Just Sceptre!

### English translation

<!-- scan 145 -->

The measure of his power;
his just sceptre!

The eight hundred years
between the fifth century BCE
and the third century CE —

here in Tamil Nadu
we call that span
the Sangam age.

The three crowned kings,
wearers of the diadem,
were garlanded in Tamil
with the flower-garlands of poets.

This Tamil land,
which had crossed many stages
of gradual development,

stood as a homeland
for the civilisational advancement
of many lands;

it stood too
as a master of the sea
and shone in commerce;

as the land of Chera, Chola and Pandya,
it rose as earth filled with valour,

with women of chastity,
warriors of splendour,
and a lineage that won victory
over mountain-like obstacles.

When it stood so high
that the world praised it,
the Chola country first
shone like the rising sun!

The first two Karikalans,
Killivalavan,
and Sengannan and Nalankilli,
who stood alongside those three —

these five are said
to be the early Chola kings.

Among them,
the second Karikalan
was Tirumavalavan,

born as the beloved son
of the daughter of the Alunthur Velir
and Ilanchetchenni,
and he ruled from Uraiyur!

<!-- scan 146 -->

The Arulalar and the Oliyar,
who opposed him at the beginning,
are said at last
to have accepted his rule
and submitted.

He carried out
many works of development;
he ruled the country
so that peace could spread;

he governed in such a way
that the world could praise Tamil Nadu
as a fertile land,
and poets could sing with joy.

Then,
to cross the deep sea
and maintain relations with foreign lands,
he made Poompuhar
another capital,

and with world-wide distinction
his sceptre became
the measuring rod
of Chola power!

Seeing everyone sing
the fame of that great king,
some northern rulers
burned and suffered in envy.

They tried their sword-play;
so Valavan himself
took up the sword and set forth
to cast down the foe!

“We have come to bow
and receive favour,” they said;
and the crowned heads
of Vachiram, Avanti, Magadha
and other lands,
who had once refused to bow,

bent low
and offered many gifts.

The ruler of Magadha
gave an assembly hall;
the ruler of Vachiram,
a victory pavilion;
the king of Avanti,
an ornamental gateway —

as gifts,
sharing their goodwill.

Seeking to diminish
the esteem of the crown-bearing
great king Karikalan,

a northern ruler called “Mukari”
cast a spell of counsel
from an idea born in his head:

“Guardian! King Karikala!
To raise and build
the banks of the Kaveri,

why not invade Lanka,
win there,
and bring back
a great number of prisoners?”

<!-- scan 147 -->

Karikalan invaded;
he captured twelve thousand
warriors of Lanka
and brought them back,

and the king built the banks
so that the Kaveri could flow
for the benefit of irrigation!

Mukari thought:
“Has not even greater glory
come to Karikalan
through my cunning counsel?”

and when he prepared
to carry out another plot,
Karikalan took up his spear,

and pressed its point
into the forehead
of the swollen, cruel Mukari!

Until then,
on Mukari's face
there had been an ugly third eye;

as though a drum
had split at its rim,
it tore completely
and became a wound.

The inexhaustible Tamil poet Ottakkoothar,
and Jayangondar,
blowing the trumpet of triumph,

made poetry of this story
and praised Tirumavalavan Karikalan;

the grace of those songs
is part of the glory
of Tamil history
that has won deathless fame!

Because literature,
inscriptions and copper plates
are what we must take
as historical records,

some confusion
is bound to arise.

Whichever of the two Karikalans
this may have been,
let us cool our thirst
with the thought
that the fame comes
to the Tamils in either case!

Let us wipe away too
the sorrow
of not possessing
a complete history!
