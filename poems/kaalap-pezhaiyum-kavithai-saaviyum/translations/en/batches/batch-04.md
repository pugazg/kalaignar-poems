## Item 7 — The Form of an Age in History!

- canonical file: `../../../sections/07.md`
- physical scans: **35–39**
- printed pages: **34–38**
- title-witness status: **contents/title-page match**

### English translation

<!-- scan 35 -->

The landlord gathered a hired force and waged war,
finished off the lover of that virtuous “maiden,”

and when the lover breathed his last
in his beloved's lap,
he said that the struggle for rights,
when it entered the field,
should be conducted on the path of virtue,

and that it should continue without slackening
until the troubles were gone;
with those words he closed his eyes.
If one is to tell that story —

even before then,
that region had stood as a dwelling-place of uncivilized life;
without the vigour and hunting power
that animals possessed,

humans fell one after another
as food for their bellies —
and that history itself
stands before us and shakes our confidence.

If a few examples must be told:
thinking it was the hard root of a tree,
one frog-brained man,
in the half-light,

sat down upon a python.
When the python writhed,
unable to bear the man's weight,

that mighty hero shrieked “Vee!” and convulsed;
his heart burst then and there,
and family and village alike
wept themselves out —

such a “Bakasura-sized coward!”
Was there only one such case, or two?
It was an everyday serial!

<!-- scan 36 -->

A man went on foot through the forest,
meaning to offer food to Kaliyatha
and sacrifice a goat-kid;

a rutting elephant blocked his way,
trampled him underfoot,
and did not leave him until they were bound for `Kaavur` —
that report
terrified those who thought of entering the forest;

and then there was the great terror
of hornets that would swarm even upon people
lying inside their houses and sting them to death!

What can one do? ........
At the next stage after the Stone Age,

the struggles were between animal-kind and humankind,
producing savage fields of death!

In the stages after that,
even when animals bore no hostility toward humans,

among humans themselves,
again and again,
the struggle came to be arranged
as though it were animal-kind against humankind;

and it went on in such a state
that one could no longer tell:
who is the animal? who is the human?

Among the disasters seen as a result,
I shall show you one scene
that those who carefully open the casket of time
with the key of poetry must see —

alas,
Stone Age paintings,
epics of tears!

For a newly married couple,
the noon meal was to be a feast of crocodile meat —

<!-- scan 37 -->

with fresh affection in her heart,
the wife said so,
and so did the others.

“Four brothers went to the river,
searched everywhere,
and have returned with a crocodile they caught.

I shall cut it up and cook the curry —
my hand's cooking will taste like nectar!”

Alamu sent her elder brothers
to bring her husband.

“One of us will come back with him at once,”
said one brother, flying off —
then he returned exhausted,
stood before his sister,
wept, lamented, cried “Ayyo!”

“What is it, brother? Where is he?”
asked the sister.
He was distraught:
“I searched everywhere;
he is nowhere in this village.”

“Why grieve?
He must have gone outside the village;
he will return now!
Come — first let us cut the crocodile,
cook the curry,
and set the feast ready;

he too will hurry back and eat — you'll see,”
she said.

“First give me the billhook to cut the crocodile,”
her brother asked Alamu of the silken body.

She handed him the shining billhook and said,
“Brother, cut it carefully.”

The brothers held the crocodile,
one on each side;
when the billhook was raised and brought down —

<!-- scan 38 -->

the crocodile split into two.
A Himalayan astonishment — everyone shook!

Alamu screamed,
fainted and fell:
inside that crocodile's belly
lay the body of her husband!

Yes — a lifeless body!
Alas! At dawn,

when he had gone to the riverbank,
the crocodile had swallowed him!

Then, like thunder in summer,
ten men came shouting “Ah!” “Oo!” —
forest-dwellers with the look of rhinoceroses,

mountain-like masses of flesh
in human shape,
with cattle horns fixed upon their heads,

thinking those horns their royal crowns,
wearing buffalo-hide garments
with swelling pride,

raising hands
that carried tridents,

clenching their teeth,
roaring in many voices,
throwing stones —

savouring the wailing of a death-house
as though it were fine music —

“We shall gore you with horns!
We shall kill you!
We shall set a furnace to your lives!”

with that bellow,
they entered the house.

<!-- scan 39 -->

Unable to resist,
everyone fled.

The arriving horde
turned their mouths into caves,

ate the crocodile flesh raw
with appetite,

and then ate with delight
the limbs of the young handsome man —
Alamu's husband —
who had lain inside the crocodile's belly.

Then they danced away
through the village.

Civilized culture shining on one side,
and the manifestation of an age of destruction
growing on the other —

this is the form
of an age in history!

## Item 8 — Sweat Falling from the Brow; the Ribcage Breaking!

Source metadata:

- stable item: **8**

### English translation

<!-- scan 40 -->

In Sankrityayan's collection
of short stories with a historical background —
*From the Volga to the Ganga* —
under the title “Amritashva,”

I recently read on page 54
of the distinctive rule of life
set apart for the wives of that age;
I write with that memory before me.
Historian “Rahul” has drawn it thus:

“When guests and friends came to the house,
it was the custom not only to feast them
and make them happy,

but temporarily to give them one's wives as well,
as an honour.”

The culture that entered in this way,
through the mountain pass,
seems to have taken another degraded form
in the story where Iyarpakai Nayanar
sent his own wife into the bedchamber
for a guest who came presenting himself
as an incarnation of God.

I am only saying what is there;
whether you bear with this,
or refuse to bear it and hate me for it,

<!-- scan 41 -->

you must decide firmly
after reading both books.

The coming of the Aryans,
their clash with the Dravidians
who had lived here from the beginning;

an eighteen-year war without rest
between the Dravidians called “Asuras”
and the “Devas,” the immortals —

learned great men — Iyers, Iyengars,
Samas and Shastris —
wrote such histories even then!

That the Ramayana war itself
was an Aryan–Dravidian struggle
was written by Nehru
before he became Prime Minister of India.

Absorbing all this within itself,
there took shape

the historical work
*From the Volga...*!

I have not bent it to suit my own course;
am I mad enough to call the sky the earth?

Like the ever-flowing life-river Ganga,
I would only undertake penance and ask a boon
that Kaveri too should swell in abundance
for Tamil Nadu;

would I dress myself up
as a distilled liar
and call a dry great river “Ganga”?

That is how I state it emphatically —
always on the strength of evidence —

the story of Aryan culture
coming to swallow and bring down
Dravidian culture!

By a culture that laid siege
three thousand years ago,

<!-- scan 42 -->

we have for many years
been enduring the cruelty
of the fire then kindled.

Thousands upon thousands of acres of land —
said to belong to temple,
said to belong to the Aadheenam —

and upon those lands,
labouring day after day,
pearls of sweat falling and falling from the brow,

ribcage breaking and breaking,
growing crops while the stomach
burns with hunger —

for the poor peasant,
that working labourer,
there is neither means nor opportunity
to cook even a morsel of rice.

If he raises a question and asks “Why?”
there is no one to answer!

Dreaming is of no use;
neither God nor the government
is going to attend to us.

Even when this worry upon worry
takes aim at us
like stones from a sling
and strikes us —
let us bear it
and try waking God himself:

morning, noon and night,
three times a day,

after the cost of food
for you and your family,

save every coin left over each day
in good banks and let it earn interest;

O Lord, spend it every day
so that your children may gain health
and rise step after step
in education rich with knowledge —
then our country will advance well, we shall say.

From today,
let us dig a pit and bury narrow aims;
to climb the hill of fame and stand there —

<!-- scan 43 -->

we need new thought after new thought.
To fulfil our goal,
we need the capacity to act.

To gain that capacity and act,
our gaze must travel in every direction.
As the manure for it,
we must understand our ancient history.

Only when history is in our hands
as sword and shield
can we face the hostile field
that comes crashing against us.

So that our history may shine
as a great lamp of the world,
as a sacred lamp,

it must stand clear in everything,
leaving no room for doubt.

Because the casket of time
will stand beside us for that purpose,
I shall continue to tell
some more stories in poetic form,
from my imagination!

I know there is no gain
in speaking merely of old tales —
yet by speaking of the stories
of our grandfathers and forefathers,
the old stories of history,

we may ward off harm from society.
From the events of past history,

what must be discarded?
What among those memories must be forgotten?

Nothing among them need be destroyed —
only if historical monuments are allowed to live

can they serve to show us the way
toward both the good and the bad.

Recognizing the good,
we may walk further;
if it is evil,
we may turn back at once!
We may reform ourselves too!

## Item 9 — What Truth Does the Conversation Reveal?

### English translation

<!-- scan 44 -->

I myself have sometimes wondered
whether the previous day's casket of time
was a little too long;
when telling history,
one must travel by the proper road.

Five and three make eight;
even if we add it as three and five,
only the manner of addition differs —
there is no contradiction
in the total!

As an example,
in Sankrityayan's stories
with historical backgrounds,
I fold my hands and invite you:
come see the portrait called “Purudhanan,”
painted against the screen of the year 2000 B.C.

Show me your kindness;
after seeing it,
pour out your opinion too!

From the highly regarded book
*From the Volga to the Ganga*,
I shall show you a conversation drawn there;
that is my duty to you!

A conversation between two Aryans —
I give it exactly as it stands,

<!-- scan 45 -->

without adding or subtracting,
without correcting what he has drawn;
value this work of mine in presenting it!

The conversation between the two Aryans in that book.
(Book — pages 101, 118, 119, 120)

“We have done away with the office of Indra.”

“You have done away with it?”

“Yes; for us Aryans of the southern region,
this office had begun to cause fear.”

“Why fear it?”

“Do we not call the commander of the army ‘Indra’?”

“Yes.”

“Yes; Aryans do not regard the commander-in-chief
as master over all their affairs.
In wartime it is true
that we accept his order exactly and act upon it.
But we Aryans consider above everything else
the social assembly in which each has authority
to speak his opinion openly.”

“Yes, yes.”

“But the Asuras hold the opposite principle.
The Indra, or king, of the Asuras
possesses every authority.
He recognizes no social assembly above himself.
Every word that comes from the Asura king's mouth
is law.
Every Asura must accept it exactly.
For one who disobeys,
the punishment is death.”

<!-- scan 46 -->

“Yes, I too have seen it.
Their priests have turned the Asuras into donkeys.”

“Yes — lower even than donkeys,
the Asuras perform linga worship.
The shape of that linga is like
the male organ joined with the female organ.
I accept that these are instruments
for the pleasure of union between man and woman,
and that through that union
our future generations grow.
But is it not sheer foolishness
to make that shape in earth or stone,
set it up and worship it?”

“Yes; what doubt is there in that?”

“That Asura king is a great devotee
of this linga deity.
But I think this too is a disguise.
That Asura king and his priests are not fools.
They are cleverer than we Aryans.
It will take us a long time
to learn to build cities like theirs.
Their market streets,
tanks filled with lotus flowers,
the high flights of steps beside them,
and broad royal avenues —
we cannot see such arrangements
anywhere in our Aryan land.”

What you are seeing is the casket of time
from roughly four thousand years ago —
from 2000 B.C.!
Rahul's *From the Volga to the Ganga*
photographs, as it were, the Aryans of that day
regarding the philosophy of the linga-form
worshipped by the Asuras
as the summit of uncivilized life,
as utter foolishness!

<!-- scan 47 -->

If the divine form we saw four thousand years ago
was a cultural form of the Dravidians called Asuras,
and today it is accepted in temple after temple,

does that mean Aryan culture
has now approved the philosophy of the divine form
found by the Asuras?

Is the union of the Avudaiyar and the linga
appropriate only now?

Every day we see deities
with great clustered moustaches
and fearsome, fierce forms —

celebrated as gods of the Dravidian Asuras:
Theevattirayan,
valiant Madurai Veeran,
Kathavarayan,
Muneeswaran,
Saneeswaran,
Kaadan, Maadan,
the dark Idumban —

and clans that celebrate such fierce deities
as their clan gods,
standing as marks, we are told,
of the Asura clan;
we see temples and tanks
bearing their names everywhere,
without exception —
we see and rejoice in them
even at celebrated sacred sites.

Though we may have taken darshan
thousands of times,
have we even once remembered
the story we read
and twisted our faces at what we saw?

My purpose is not to mock gods;
is this not an example proving
that, in this world,
culture remains distinct
even in the question of God?

Even in worshipping the divine,
two different cultures;
that the linga-form belonged to the Asuras,
that the Aryans did not accept it —

yet those who four thousand years ago
said it was uncivilized,
why do they today, across the whole country,
sing namavali to that very form?

Cultures were different —
even in delighting in God there was difference;
between Aryans and Dravidians called Asuras,
there was no agreement in this.

<!-- scan 48 -->

Has that condition now changed?
If not, did Sankrityayan himself
fail to observe the cultural difference correctly?

Is there any temple today
without the linga that the Aryans mocked?
On massive temple chariots —
on temple towers —
which great believers forgot
to set those divine symbols?

In temples of fierce-deity worship,
who came forward boldly
to install an image in linga form?

Even in the making of divine images,
there is cultural difference —
between Aryans and Dravidians called Asuras;

and even there,
one form in the northern region,
another form in the southern region.

There, the linga that marks male and female —
in the Aryan dictionary.....
ignorance, foolishness!

Here, in every temple,
that very form becomes Ammaiyappar,
the Mother-and-Father form;

a form worthy of reverence,
a deity to be worshipped.

Do not roll up your sleeves
and rise to quarrel with me.
Do not leap and bound toward me
and abuse me as a sinner —

this conversation between two Aryans
about the form of God
openly declares
that Aryans did not accept
the culture of the Dravidians called Asuras,
even in the very form of God —

<!-- scan 49 -->

and when we examine
how even then they stood divided,
one way in the north,
another in the south,

we also see that,
in changing themselves to suit circumstance,
Aryans are formidable masters.

Scholar Sankrityayan,
in his own way,
has already taught us this;

and this truth will live
not merely as long as
*From the Volga to the Ganga* lives,

but as long as there remains
such a thing as history
in this world.

Certain! Certain! Certain!

## Translator notes

- From this batch onward, the iteration cadence is **three complete items**. Batches 01–03 remain historically intact as two-item reviewed batches.
- Item 7 `காவூர்` is source-sensitive and was not silently normalized. It is retained as **Kaavur** in the English line and should be revisited only through source-backed lexical review if a later editorial pass requires it.
- `பகாசூரப் பயந்தாங்குளி` is rendered **Bakasura-sized coward**, preserving the comic exaggeration and mythic scale rather than reducing it to plain “coward.”
- The crocodile episode retains the source's escalation from domestic feast to discovery, invasion, cannibal violence and the closing civilization/destruction contrast.
- Item 8 retains the Iyarpakai Nayanar comparison and the Aryan/Dravidian historical framing as claims made by the source; translation does not fact-check or soften them.
- `ஆதீனம்` is retained as **Aadheenam** because it is a culturally specific institutional term in the landholding passage.
- The working peasant imagery — sweat as pearls, ribcage breaking, stomach burning — is preserved rather than replaced by abstract economic description.
- Item 9 contains a long quoted conversation attributed by the poem to Rahul Sankrityayan. The English preserves it as quoted dialogue and does not present it as independently verified historical fact.
- `இலிங்கம்`, `ஆவுடையார்`, `அம்மையப்பன்`, `நாமாவளி` are retained or closely transliterated where the argument depends on specifically Tamil/Saiva religious vocabulary.
- The named deities in Item 9 remain individually visible rather than being compressed into “folk gods.”
- The triple ending `உறுதி! உறுதி! உறுதி!` is retained as **Certain! Certain! Certain!** to preserve rhetorical repetition.
