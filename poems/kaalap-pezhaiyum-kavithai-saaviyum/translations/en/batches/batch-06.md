## Item 13 — The Unbroken Alliance That Made Kharavela Tremble!

### English translation

<!-- scan 64 -->

A Stone Age, a Golden Age —
ages have been classified so;
and within those ages history has been drawn,
showing how civilisational growth,
culture and custom differed
from country to country,
from people to people.

In that line came the great guiding book
*From the Volga to the Ganga*!

In that book its author,
according to each period and each region,
introduces characters
and follows the method of telling history through story.
The aim of the *Casket of Time* I write is also that;

but I do not wish completely to cast aside
those already introduced to us
through literature, history and ancient legend.
So that, in the history of India
advancing stage by stage through many fields,
the part belonging to the Tamil people
of the Dravidian race is not left out,

I seek to mark their presence.
Even if I cannot bring in all of them,
I present at least some,
so that the distinction of a balanced history
may not be broken.

Here then —
to show that the ancient age
that came to the Tamils
was indeed a golden age,
I shall set before you a few poetic lines.

<!-- scan 65 -->

As support for them,
I also wish to give notes
written by the multilingual scholar Appathuraiyar:

“The Sangam works repeatedly say
that the three crowned Tamil kings,
competing with one another,
engraved upon the Himalaya
the bow, the fish and the tiger emblems.
Many scholars and most historians
regard these as mere inventions.
But the many references,
at the horizon of known history,
to south–north relations,
to Tamil naval warfare from Tamil Nadu,
to wars against peoples of the far north,
and to victories won in wars with Aryans,
do not permit us to dismiss them as inventions.”

“*Pattuppaattu* says
that Neduncheralathan himself ruled
from the Himalaya to Kumari:

‘The famed Himalaya where the Aryans faltered,
and southern Kumari — between the two,
he crossed beyond those who claimed kingship over the earth.’

This may have been an extended rule,
a supremacy acknowledged by all kings,
or a victorious progress made throughout the region.”

“A Chola king, says the poet Paavaik Kottilar,
defeated the Aryans
in a battle at Vallam near Tanjai:

‘Like the Aryan army shattered at Vallam,
where the rain-cloud-like Chola won,
may the bracelet swelling on my straight forearm break!’
(*Akam* 336)

The lover-girl turns the anger she bears toward her beloved
upon the bracelet she wears:
‘Let my bangle break,
like the Aryan army that broke at the battle of Vallam!’”

“Crossing the army of the northern Aryans,
and beholding all the southern Tamil land,

<!-- scan 66 -->

with the goddess of unswerving chastity,
the Pandya Nedunchezhiyan
slept upon the royal couch.”
(*Silappathikaram*, concluding note to the Madurai canto)

Do not these pieces of evidence
show the condition of India
as a land where two great peoples —
Aryan and Dravidian — stood?

And further —

to open the casket of time
with the key of poetry
and show the Kalinga emperor Kharavela
would itself be a significant historical note.

He was one who,
to push Ashoka and the victories attributed to him
into second place,
and to bring his own name into first,
to secure his throne
and extend the reach of his rule,
tried every political stratagem required for it.

The period in which he gained fame
is placed between 163 BCE and 153 BCE —
that is, 2,158 years before the time of this writing;
yet he was a king of kings
well practised in setting up inscriptions
even to proclaim as his own
the achievements he claimed to have won.

As one asks,
“Why seek a mirror to see a wound already on the hand?”
so, many have said — and written —
that inscriptions themselves stand as witnesses.
This is no tale;
no imagination;
but evidence shown before the eye:

“An inscription of Kharavela records
that he broke an alliance of Tamil kings
which had continued for 113 years,”
says the multilingual scholar.

<!-- scan 67 -->

As Ashoka, praised by the world,
set out to spread Buddhism
across the globe,
Kharavela, in rivalry with him,
set out to spread Jainism
and even received the title
“the Jain Ashoka.”

Around a king who had gathered such victories,
would there not be a thousand men?
The Mauryas had never ventured
to wage such a war upon Tamil Nadu;
he ventured to collide with it.

(His army advanced with drums sounding;
and so that the Tamil land
would not be ruined by its entry,
so that the Tamil people
would not stumble and fall
under the wars he drove forward,

the three crowned kings,
the Velir rulers,
and Ilanchetchenni —
who crossed Seruppaazhi like a spark of fire —
formed a war-alliance,
met Kharavela fiercely in the field,
and sharpened sword and spear!

Kharavela dreamed
that he would dig a pit
and bury that alliance;
that dream did not come true.
It is this, Appathuraiyar shows
in his book *Wars of the South*,
that Kharavela “boasts of through his inscription.”

If we read it sharply,
we understand how rulers leap and rage
when they confront an alliance
that stands as death itself
to arrogant rule.
This is not merely a truth of yesterday and today;
nor news carried casually upon the wind!

History warns us
what form the Kalinga king Kharavela took
when he saw opposition rise against him;
to speak it forth is my duty!

**Seruppaazhi** = a battlefield encampment.

## Item 14 — The History of Kanaka and Vijaya Carrying the Stone!

### English translation

<!-- scan 68 -->

Even if the history of ancient Tamil Nadu
was once silk cloth,
what has come down to us for reading and enactment
is only a patched remnant of that cloth —
there is no use hiding this today.

If we speak even of what remains,
our graceful form will be seen;
that, in this world,
we are among the most ancient;
our full form will be understood
in literature, grammar,
and in the wealth of language
sweetening as *iyal, isai, natakam*.

No one has drawn and kept,
firmly and clearly,
all the historical notes of the South.
That is why,
with literature as companion,
and with the help of inscriptions
seen here and there,
we continue to gather and present as history
the truths filtered from them.

History points, approximately,
to the Pandya king called Nediyon —
also called Nilamtharu Thiruvil Pandiyan —
as one connected with the age of Tolkappiyar,
as one who lived some three thousand years ago.

<!-- scan 69 -->

History praises the Chola Karikalan
as a king whose valour never declined;
yet researchers say
that the Karikalan who marched north to the Himalaya
was the second Karikalan.

The peerless warrior Senguttuvan,
son of Imayavaramban Neduncheralathan,
went north twice.
When an astrologer foretold
that his younger brother Ilango would rule,
that brother made the prophecy false:
he became an ascetic on the path of virtue
and gave us the epic of the noble Tamil Kannagi.

The elder brother went to bring stone
for a statue of that queen of chastity;
he broke the crowns of Kanaka and Vijaya
and made them carry the stone back.
See that tale now —
as a one-act play!

### Scene 1

**The palace of Kanaka and Vijaya on the bank of the Ganga.**

**Herald:** O unsurpassed heroes! Compassionate to the poor! Divine men! Oceans of Vedanta! Ornaments of the royal race! Kings of kings of the northern land! Incarnations of God — Kanaka and Vijaya! *Parak! Parak!*

**Kings:** Namaskar!

**Kanaka:** Namaskar!

**Vijaya:** Namaskar!

**Kanaka:** It seems they are all waiting
for the beauty of the swayamvara — he! he!...

**Kings:** Yes! But now that both of you have come,
if one of you wins the swayamvara contest,
how will the two of you divide the woman between yourselves?

<!-- scan 70 -->

**Tamil poet:** We shall see that afterwards....

**Vijaya:** Who are you, fellow? What are you saying?

**Poet:** Tamil! Classical Tamil!
Tamil born with the world!
Ancient Tamil that arose first,
in the age before stone appeared
and before earth appeared!

**Vijaya:** What business have you
in a swayamvara hall?

**Poet:** Hmm — a strange question, O king!
So in a swayamvara hall
there is business only for old men like you,
and none for young men like me....

**Vijaya:** Enough mischief. Tell us who you are!

**Poet:** I am a Tamil poet —
I have come travelling through the northern land....

**Kanaka:** A poet? Do you know how to compose poetry?

**Poet:** A poet who cannot compose poetry —
a warrior who cannot lift a sword —
Tamil Nadu has never yet seen such men.....

<!-- scan 71 -->

**Kanaka:** We shall test that too.
Come then — say a poem at once!

**Poet:** On the bank of the Ganga
the Tamil fought;
on the bank of the Kaveri he gained fame.
If you come in our time to wage war,
we shall break your legs — beware.....

**Kings:** Ha! Ha! Ha!

**Vijaya:** What Kanaka said is worth
hundreds of thousands for every letter, poet!
When your kings engraved
tiger, bow and fish
upon the forehead of the Himalaya,
it seems our kings were never here at all!

**Poet:** Why? Were they to fall at your feet
and offer tribute?
You men of deranged minds, listen!
Today itself I return to the South.
I shall tell the Chera
what Kanaka and Vijaya,
with tongues they cannot guard,
said together with the banquet kings,
not knowing the might of precious Tamil.
You drunkards of arrogance!
Tamil valour has not died!
Aryan kings — we shall meet again.
The one who rules is Cheran Senguttuvan!
Cheran Senguttuvan!!

<!-- scan 72 -->

### Scene 2

**The Chera royal assembly hall.**

**Chera:** Tiger-like warriors,
who bear the sword
and bear fame through Tamil Nadu!
The summons has come for you all!
A fine chance has come
to establish our valour once more
in the northern land!

Some small-minded men, it seems,
have mocked the emblems
engraved upon the Himalaya.
A fool who has never seen a battlefront
mocked the tiger.
A worthless man who puts a price on courage
insulted the bow.
A boor who has never heard tales
of entering the field
mocked the fish.

Uthiran — Visithiran —
Chithiran — Sivethan —
Bhairavan! Do not stare!
These are merely human names!
Kanaka and Vijaya, they say,
are chiefs of these frog-brained kings —
guardians living on the bank of the Ganga
who have thrown a challenge
to the bank of the Kaveri!
We shall teach them revolution —
set out!

**A minister:** O king,
why does an anger not found
in the Chola or the Pandya
burn only in you?

<!-- scan 73 -->

**Chera:** Because I am Tamil —
because my sense of self-respect has not diminished!
The Chola or the Pandya
may be my enemy;
but for that reason
I will not allow northern Aryan kings
to wag their tails,
so long as I have life!

Did they not insult my Tamil?
Those arrogant men!
Ah... O king who gave us the Kural — Valluvar!
They insulted you....

O Sattanar,
who speaks substantial thought
in the royal hall!
O lord of the arena!
They insulted you!

O sea of pearls!
O jasmine grove!
They insulted you!
O lion-land that lives
with drums sounding through all eight directions,
they insulted you!

Mother Narchonai!
They insulted the victory
of Senguttuvan,
son of Imayavaramban Neduncheralathan.

Ilango! Beloved younger brother!
They insulted you!

O Pandya who gave life for justice!
O Nedunchezhiyan who crossed the Aryan army!
They insulted you!

Lion-like bulls
whose classical Tamil wealth never diminishes!
Scholars! Poets! Artists!
They insulted all of you!
They insulted you —

<!-- scan 74 -->

and those who insulted you
are still alive,
as kings in the northern land!

O beautiful capital Vanchi,
surrounded by the Porunai river
and fertile abundance,
they insulted you! They insulted you!
Men of low nature insulted you!

With minister Azhumbil Vel here,
with warrior Villavan Kothai as commander,
and strength waxing like a crescent moon
upon all your broad shoulders —

some creatures who should have remained
by the riverbank muttering mantras
have shouted in fury, it seems!
Warriors — let us show our hand
to those wanderers
who do not know how to guard the tongue!

Let every warrior
in the forts of Thumbi, Vaadi,
Thoranangarpattu and Vettamangalam
set out!

Young bulls!
Tell your beloveds
this may be the final kiss,
and leave!
Are we a society afraid to die?
Are we a people who fear battle,
to surrender to those fly-whisk bearers?
No! No!
Our heads may be cut away in the field —
but they will be cut
with eyes that spit fire!

<!-- scan 75 -->

Our flesh may be torn apart —
but it will be torn
before the heat of self-respect blood has cooled!
Beat the war-drum!
Strike the Himalaya!
The Tamil has not been defeated!
Has not been defeated!
Set out!”

### Scene 3

**Kuyilaluvam.**

*(In the battle between Cheran Senguttuvan and Kanaka–Vijaya, Senguttuvan wins.)*

**Chera:** Villava! What is the name
of this place where victory resounds?

**Villavan:** Kuyilaluvam.

**Chera:** Kuyilaluvam — mark it down!
Every one of these rocks
is a manuscript upon which
pure Tamil valour has written!
Victory alone is not enough, Villava;
we must not let those vain men go.

**Villavan:** They say Kanaka and Vijaya
have fled somewhere,
wearing saffron robes like rishis....

**Chera:** Nothing new....
That is the one habit
that truly belongs to them!

*(Kanaka and Vijaya are brought in dressed as holy men.)*

<!-- scan 76 -->

**Chera:** Who are these men?

**Villavan:** Kanaka and Vijaya.

**Chera:** Kanaka and Vijaya... Kanaka and Vijaya....
Heroes among heroes!
Splendid indeed is your art of war!
Showing your backs —
pulling a veil over yourselves —
attacking from concealment —
turning yourselves into holy men —
now you stand dressed as sages!
Answer me this:
in the Puranas,
how long did the Deva–Asura war last?

**Kanaka:** Eighteen years!

**Chera:** The Rama–Ravana war?.....

**Kanaka:** Eighteen months!

**Chera:** The Bharata war?

**Kanaka:** Eighteen days!

**Chera:** Warriors, remember this:
the war Cheran Senguttuvan waged
against Kanaka and Vijaya
ended in only eighteen *naazhigai*.

**Kanaka:** O king, forgive us....
Forgive us!

**Chera:** Forgive you? I should forgive you?
Come to the soil of Tamil Nadu
and kneel there.
Let that soil forgive you.....

<!-- scan 77 -->

**Villavan:** O king,
should we not return to Tamil Nadu?
It has been thirty-four months
since we set out.....

**Chera:** We shall leave, Villava.
But before that,
do we not have to take stone from here
to raise Kannagi's statue?

**Villavan:** Yes....

**Chera:** We have found
the proper bulls to carry that stone.
Kanaka — Vijaya!
You cast a word against Tamil valour;
now lift the stone!

Tamil scholars,
qualified researchers,
poets of self-respect,
and those eager to know true history
must wander everywhere,
search everywhere,
bring back the hidden lineage of gems,
and set those gems into the crown.

## Item 15 — Let Us Drink This “Aryan” Tea!

- canonical Tamil title: `பருகிடலாம் இந்த “ஆரிய” தேநீரை!`
- contents witness: `பருகிடலாம் இந்த “ஆரிய” தேநீரை!`
- canonical file: `../../../sections/15.md`

- printed pages: **77–80**

### English translation

<!-- scan 78 -->

Before the Chera entered the battlefield
on the bank of the Ganga
to take stone for carving Kannagi's statue,
he had gone there once before,
accompanying his mother
with a great army as her support,
so that she might bathe in that river.

On that first journey itself,
he broke the Aryan kings
who surrounded him,
won victory,
and made his mother's face bloom
like the full moon!

“When, on that day,
in the fierce flood of the great river Ganga,
our king bathed his mother,
the Aryan kings,
who rose against the one man that he was,
beheld the cruel form of battle —
black-eyed Death itself opened its eyes!”
(*Silappathikaram*)

When the casket of time is opened
and past history is told thus
through stories and poems,
I seek only to draw aside
the great curtains that until now
have hidden truths skilfully from our sight,
and show them.

Yet some condemn the act
of explaining that the people called “Dravidians”
are not a race absent from this sacred land,
as a continuing “act of hatred”
meant to awaken once again
a forgotten feeling of racial hostility.

<!-- scan 79 -->

They write letters;
they abuse from hiding.
I have a heart that can bear anything;
yet I do not intend merely
to raise a counter-voice against an opposing voice.
I wish to give a gentle voice of explanation.

That is why I write this.
During a morning walk,
or in a fast running race,
if a small stone or thorn
strikes the foot,

though it may not stop us altogether,
do we not pause,
remove the obstruction,
and then go on?

I think of contrary views in just that way.
My journey continues;
here I stop for a little;
then, with vigour,
the journey will continue again.
This is the explanation I offer
those who seek to stop it!

“Aryan, Dravidian —
why put this tea, long gone cold,
back upon the stove and heat it?
What business is there to accomplish now?”

Some pierce me
with that impudent question.
What can I do?
In a poor man's house,
that very tea is “divine nectar”!

But if one is a landlord
of four thousand acres
of wet and dry fields,
then without a care
for race, language, literature,
art, culture or country,

he may spend his time
looking only upon mounds of money,
hills of wealth,
mountains of gold,
and ranges of diamond and vaidurya!

<!-- scan 80 -->

Coffee, tea or any other drink —
in his mansion it will be stock
that never goes stale,
remaining forever hot!

The moment I say Aryan and Dravidian,
some grow angry
and perform an *archanai* upon me,
as though I had invented something that did not exist.
Why does such wicked anger arise?

Excavation digs out buried skulls and bones,
classifies and orders the histories
of peoples who lived,
and bears witness through deep research.

Iyers, Iyengars, Samas and Shastris —
learned historians themselves —
have published views on these matters.
For those who refuse to see though they have eyes,
who do not wish to hear though they have ears,
who possess awareness yet declare,
“We shall not understand,”

whatever place is fitting for them —
is that not the place they will receive!

What answer will all these great intellects give,
I ask,
to the following statements of their own?

“The Tamils were not merely
the aboriginal people of Tamil Nadu;
they may have been among
the earliest people of the world itself.”

I tell you that this was stated
by P. T. Srinivasa Iyengar
in his book *History of the Tamils*.

“Tamil civilisation is far more ancient
than Aryan civilisation,”
says Sesha Iyengar
in *Dravidian India*.

<!-- scan 81 -->

“Let scholars examine
whether the coming of the Aryans to Tamil Nadu
brought good or evil to the Tamils,”
said Parithimar Kalaignar —
Suriyanarayana Sastri —
in his book *History of the Tamil Language*.

When, with explanation of cause and effect,
I speak world history
from the past to the present,
and when true history is not hidden,

opening the casket of time
will not create some new battlefield.

It is enough
if the old memory
that birth itself creates high and low
withers and dies.

Then, without narrowness of mind,
let us drink this “Aryan” tea!

## Translator notes

- Item 13's Appathuraiyar passages are presented as quotations inside Kalaignar's poem. The English preserves their status as quoted historical argument rather than independently validating the claims.
- Classical citations in Item 13 are translated for meaning while the work titles and the explicit `அகம் 336` reference remain traceable.
- `செருப்பாழி` is retained as **Seruppaazhi** in the narrative and its source glossary meaning, “battlefield encampment,” is preserved.
- The Kharavela inscription, the 113-year Tamil-kings' alliance and the chronology are translated exactly as source claims; translation does not substitute modern historical adjudication.
- Item 14 is structurally a one-act drama. Scene headings, speaker labels, heraldic exaggeration, repeated insults, war-oratory and the comic turn from boast to disguise are kept as dramatic speech rather than flattened into narrative prose.
- The herald's `பாரக்! பாரக்!` is retained as **Parak! Parak!** because the performative court-cry is part of the theatrical register.
- `நாழிகை` is retained as **naazhigai**, a traditional Tamil time unit, because replacing it with a modern approximate duration would erase the source's 18-year / 18-month / 18-day / 18-naazhigai rhetorical sequence.
- Item 14's north/south, Aryan/Tamil and self-respect rhetoric is preserved as Kalaignar's dramatic argument, without softening or external correction.
- Item 15's title and central image depend on the repeated “cold tea / reheated tea / poor man's nectar” metaphor. The quoted word **“Aryan”** therefore remains visible in the English title and close.
- `நன்செய் / புன்செய்` is rendered as **wet and dry fields**; `வைடூர்ய` is kept as **vaidurya** rather than silently substituting a specific modern gemstone identification.
- The statements attributed to P. T. Srinivasa Iyengar, Sesha Iyengar and Parithimar Kalaignar are translated as source-attributed quotations, not independently verified claims.
- No Tamil canonical or verified page record was changed during this batch.
