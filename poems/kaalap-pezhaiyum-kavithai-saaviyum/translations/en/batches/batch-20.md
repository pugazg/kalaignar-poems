# Batch 20 — items 55–57

## Item 55 — Nedunchezhiyan and Nedunalvadai!

Source metadata:

- stable item: **55**
- canonical Tamil title: `நெடுஞ்செழியனும் நெடுநல்வாடையும்!`
- contents witness: `நெடுஞ்செழியனும் நெடுநல்வாடையும்!`
- canonical file: `../../../sections/55.md`
- physical scans: **277–284**
- printed pages: **276–283**
- title-witness status: **contents/title-page match**

### English translation

<!-- scan 277 -->

Nedunchezhiyan
and *Nedunalvadai*!

“The Pandyas saw three Tamil Sangams
and rendered good service to Tamil” —

when, eager to read many works
about the history of those praised Pandyas,

I was troubled by the question,
“What was the capital of the Pandya country?”

I would say it was scholar
“Ayyamperumal” Konar
who cleared that doubt.

In the book he wrote,
he says that in the age of the First Sangam,

many *kaadams* south
of today's Madurai,
ancient Thenmadurai
stood as the capital
of the Pandya country.

After Thenmadurai
was destroyed by the first sea-deluge,

the capital that blossomed
was Kavatapuram —

and there, he says,
stood the Middle Sangam.

When that too
was destroyed by a sea-deluge,

the king Mudathirumaran,
who had not drowned and died in it,

founded the capital
that is today's great city of Madurai!

Now let us see
the spectacle and splendour
of the Pandyan kings
who ascended the throne
and ruled from that capital!

★ ★ ★

<!-- scan 278 -->

The race that,
from that ancient day,
beautifully divided life
into *agam* and *puram*

and has lived by that order —

our precious Tamil race alone,
our thousand-year crop —

is witnessed by the literature
created by the wise!

★ ★ ★

There are many examples,

but Nakkirar strung one
like a garland:

among the *Pattuppattu*,
a song shining like a pearl —

the Tamil nectar
called *Nedunalvadai*!

It speaks of the courage
of Pandyan Nedunchezhiyan;

it speaks too of the heart
of the slender-bangled Kopperundevi,

burning like fire
as she longs through his long absence;

and it becomes a work
that tells how the life called
*agam* and *puram*

stood in Tamil Nadu of that day
as a counter to the doctrine
of this world and the other world!

★ ★ ★

At the battlefield of Talaiyalanganam,
seven kings miscalculated,

thinking a lone young man
had become trapped;

and they lost,
their broad shoulders ruined!

After that,
Nedunchezhiyan,

wishing to live
like inseparable *anril* birds,

<!-- scan 279 -->

accepted as his queen
a beautiful jewel-like dove....

Though the honour-wounds
struck in his youth
by spear and sword
from seven armies

had healed
and turned to scars,

the lovely queen
thought they shone
upon the Pandyan's chest
like golden medals —

and without even thinking,
she kissed them,
every day!

After joining hearts
upon the bridal bed,

time did not grant him
space to hold,
all through the night,

his heart-stealing Anjugam
without letting her leave
the corner of his eye....

The summons
of battlefields
was the great trouble!

Even after
the battle of Talaiyalanganam,

under his leadership
he conquered
Mizhalai Kottam
of the king “Vel Evvi”!

He seized
the crown belonging
to the sword-warrior Muthuvelir,

and captured
their Muthu Kootram as well!

He destroyed the army
of the great warrior-king
“Cheraman Yanaikatchei Irumporai”

and shut him away
in prison!

Thus —

<!-- scan 280 -->

because he heaped
mountain upon mountain
of victory-garlands,

and set out roaring
with army after army
like wave after wave,

Pandimadevi trembled,

like a bird
with clusters upon clusters
of ripe fruit before it,
yet unable to eat,

losing its balance in anguish....

Unable to move
day and night forward,
she shed tears!

The *vaadai* is a cold wind,
is it not?

Even without wrapping cloth,
a woman may wrap herself
in a man's body,

and a man
in a woman's body,

and so defeat
that cold wind!

But what was the condition here?

The Pandyan king,
surging with force,
had gone to the battlefront!

In the heat
that grows courage,

the sky-threatening cold wind
could not prevail
against the king!

But the queen's condition —
alas! pitiable!

Poet Nakkirar sings:

see the suffering
of household fowl
unable to bear
the cold of the *vaadai*!

The hen and cock,
which once wandered
the wide outdoors,

searched and ate food
with delight,
and passed their days in pleasure,

now, unable to tell
night from day,

confined inside
wooden cages,

shift their aching feet
one after another,
it is said!

In such a killing cold,
with love leaping in her heart,

<!-- scan 281 -->

the spotted peahen
rolls upon her bed!

Taking up Tamil
that none can defeat,

Nakkirar fashions
*Nedunalvadai*!

A tall tower
like a mountain-peak —

its flags displaying colours
like rainbows!

Upon stout pillars
of blue-gem hue,

has silver itself
been spread as white plaster?

And the rampart wall —

as though cast from copper,
built with indescribable strength!

Thus Nakkirar,
through classical Tamil,
paints the palace
of Sezhiyan.

And see how,
through song,
he shows the bed
upon which lies

that hibiscus-lipped Pandyan queen,
her whole mind fainting
from thoughts of him!

A fierce war-elephant,
with legs like drums,

after reaching
a full forty years of age,

dies while engaged
in the work of war.

Its two sharp tusks
are taken,
shaped with beauty
and made into a bed.

At its four sides,
beautiful rounded finials,

like the breasts
of pregnant women
swollen with milk!

<!-- scan 282 -->

Hanging strings
of pearls and gems!

Paintings
of mullai and jasmine flowers!

A cover of rare artistry
showing a lion-hunt!

A five-fold bed
with support-cushion,
pillow and reclining cushion,

all fashioned
from swan feathers!

But there is no *avan* —
no “he” —
for her to say *anai*,
“embrace”!

And so the flood
of the queen's grief
breaks through its *anai*,
its dam,
and swells!

Nurses stroked her feet,
saying,
“Mother, sleep!”

“Get away, girls —
have you no other work?”

cried the queen,
furious!

In the painting
of the sky nearby,

she saw the moon
and Rohini
standing close,
never apart.

“Why have I
not received such fortune?

Why do I suffer
uselessly like this?”

A drop of water
peeped out
upon her eyelash;

she flicked it away
with her finger —

but could not
erase and rewrite
her grief;

she suffered on!

Thus,
in the palace at Madurai,

the queen languishes
like a flowering vine
holding nectar,
waiting for its bee.

What, then,
is the condition
of Pandyan Nedunchezhiyan?

<!-- scan 283 -->

Come —
let us see it
in *Nedunalvadai*!

Love did not become
an obstacle
to the Pandyan's sense of duty!

Because of thoughts
of Kopperundevi,

he did not stray
from the goal
he had taken up!

The *vaadai* wind
came to his war-camp too!

Yet he did not forget
the sword-bearing face of war
and run in search
of a bangle-bearing arm!

Here —

at midnight
the Pandyan set out.

For what?

To see the chaste wife
writhing upon the five-fold bed?

No!
No!

In the cold season
that makes even hills tremble,

with the chill wind
blowing from the north,

even though torch-flames
leaned toward the south,

the king,
inside a northern war-camp,

did not turn south
for the sake
of his yearning Pandimadevi!

To perform
the duty of the day,

with sword-bearing warriors
carrying lights
around him,

his upper cloth slipping
from his left shoulder,

he lifted and held it
with his left hand;

and upon the shoulder
of a strong sword-warrior,

he placed his right hand
in comradeship.

At midnight,
with raindrops falling,

he went through
all the places
where many great warriors
lay wounded —

<!-- scan 284 -->

asked after them
with affection,

thanked them,
and embraced them!

All the suffering
they had undergone
through the daytime battle —

dust upon their feet,
hands and bodies
as they drove back
the enemy army —

was forgotten,
it is said,

because the caring gaze
of their leader
fell upon them!

The great queen,
distraught,
searches for her lover;

he,
his legs aching,
walks around the war-camp
fulfilling duty!

If evidence is needed
that the race
which has lived from that day onward

beautifully dividing life
into *agam* and *puram*

is our precious Tamil race —

ah!
is not this one *Nedunalvadai*
enough?

★ ★ ★

(Based upon the literary work *Nedunalvadai*, composed by the Sangam poet Nakkirar in praise of Nedunchezhiyan, victor of the battle of Talaiyalanganam, this has been written in a simple style.)

## Item 56 — When Attachment Goes Beyond Its Bounds, It Burns as Frenzy!

Source metadata:

- stable item: **56**
- canonical Tamil title: `பற்று கடந்தால், பற்றி எரியும் வெறியே!`
- contents witness: `பற்று கடந்தால், பற்றி எரியும் வெறியே!`
- canonical file: `../../../sections/56.md`

- printed pages: **284–287**

### English translation

<!-- scan 285 -->

When attachment
goes beyond its bounds,
it burns as frenzy!

The Pandyan kings
are said to have conducted
the First Tamil Sangam,
which the sea swallowed,
at Madurai.

From Kaichina Vazhuthi
to Kadungon,

research commentators say
there were eighty-nine of them!

Because the First Sangam
and Thenmadurai,
where the Sangam had stood,
were submerged,

the Second Sangam —
the Middle Sangam —

was begun and conducted
at Kavatapuram
by fifty-nine,

from Vendher Chezhiyan
to Mudathirumaran.

Then, as a continuing shock,
the sea-deluge
swallowed Kavatapuram too.

As though born again
from that vast destruction,

Pandyan Mudathirumaran
survived!

Because the fierce blow
of the sea-deluge
had crippled the king
and left him without the use
of his leg,

Sangam scholars,
the learned,

and history itself,
with affection and much courtesy,

began calling him
Mudathirumaran!

Was not that title
the gift of land and sea
to the land-giving Pandyan?

Among the Sangam-age Pandyas,
after Mudathirumaran
came Mudukudumi Peruvazhuthi,

who conducted
many sacrificial rites.

<!-- scan 286 -->

He received the epithet
“Palyagasalai” —

and stands, the source argues,
as evidence
that Aryan civilisation
had already invaded
many centuries earlier!

With his long kudumi tied,
in sacrificial halls
he performed *yagas*
and the Rajasuya sacrifice.

History says
that three poets —
Karikizhar,
Nettimaiyar,
and Nedumpalliyathanar —

sang blessings
upon that king.

Karikizhar,
the source says,
was from the place Kari
in Tondai Nadu,

now called “Ramagiri”!

The poet called Nettimaiyar
received that name
because of his long eyelids;

Nedumpalliyathanar too
is said to be
a descriptive name,

because he had learned
many arts
and was skilled
in sounding music.

These poets,
addressing Mudukudumi Peruvazhuthi,
blessed him:

“May your fame rise
beyond the snow-covered Himalaya
in the north;

beyond the Kumari river
in the south;

beyond the sea dug by Sagara
in the east;

and westward,
beyond the ancient ocean!

May your rule grow!”

They also praise him,
the source says,
as one possessing
a naval force.

Seeing the blessings
uttered by the court poets,

and the words embedded in them —

“your crown belongs
to the four Vedas” —

Ayyamperumal Konar
states firmly:

“It is understood
that Vedic religion
had spread
in the age of that king!”

<!-- scan 287 -->

After Mudukudumi Peruvazhuthi
finished a war
and gained victory,

how did he,
in frenzy,
devastate the enemy country?

Let us hear
Nettimaiyar sing it.

“O Kudumi,
who rides an elephant
beneath the shade
of the royal umbrella!

After taking an army
and seizing another's land,

is it just
to tear that land
from them

and use it
so that the bards
of your country
may wear golden lotuses,

and so that chariots
with elephants harnessed to them
may be driven there?”

Thus he continues
the song of questioning!

“In streets
rutted by chariots,

you yoked donkeys
to ploughs
and ploughed them!

You left
the enemies' beautiful
jewel-like mansions
ruined beyond use!

In their cultivated fields,
where birds once cried,

you made the hoofbeats
of horses sound!

And their elephants,
with broad feet
and terrifying tusks,

you drove
from battlefield
to pond!”

On one side,
historical notes may draw
both the poets who sang
and the kings who were sung

as people devoted
to the Saiva faith;

but if we think
of the various cruelties
they used
in taking revenge
upon enemies —

<!-- scan 288 -->

then, crossing
attachment to land
and attachment to religion,

they became people
of land-frenzy
and religious frenzy,

people without humaneness,
who did not desire
religious harmony.

That truth,
one can understand,
shines like a lamp
set upon a mountain!

★ ★ ★

We also gain a chance,
through the introductory verse
of Adiyarkku Nallar's commentary
on *Silappathikaram*,

to know that another Pandyan,
named Madivanan,

held deep attachment
to dramatic Tamil!

Madivanan
was one of the Pandyan kings;

he stood in the line
of those who founded
the Last Sangam.

He climbed
the poetry-stage
so the Tamil world
might praise him —

and through nectar-Tamil
*venpas* and sutra-verses,

Madivanan's dramatic Tamil
shone and gained fame,
the source says!

Pallava Mahendran
wrote a drama
in the northern language;

but long before that,
Pandyan Madivanan
wrote an exquisite Tamil work
called *Nadaga Tamil*

and placed a signet-ring
upon Mother Tamil.

Until the time
of Adiyarkku Nallar,

that signet-ring
had not slipped
from Mother Tamil's finger;

nor had anyone
removed it.

Yes —
literary history says
that until that age,
that dramatic Tamil
was still in use!

## Item 57 — What Prize Is Fitting for the Beauty of a Simile!

Source metadata:

- stable item: **57**
- canonical Tamil title: `உவமை அழகுக்கு உரிய பரிசு என்னவாம்!`
- contents witness: `உவமை அழகுக்கு உரிய பரிசு என்னவாம்!`
- canonical file: `../../../sections/57.md`
- physical scans: **289–295**
- printed pages: **288–294**
- title-witness status: **contents/title-page match**

### English translation

<!-- scan 289 -->

What prize is fitting
for the beauty of a simile!

When a king sets out
with armies for war,

besieging the enemy's
capital fortress
is called *uzhinjai*.

Holding fast to one's own fort
and resisting the siege
is called *nochchi*.

When two armies
face one another
and wage battle,
that act is called *thumbai*.

Whoever wins,
the victory is called *vaagai*!

These, we may say,
are the names
the poets gave

to each stage
of the warfare
of the Three Crowned Kings.

There was a king
named Pandyan Ukkira Peruvazhuthi.

Let us see,
in this part
of Pandyan history,

the event in which
he went with a sky-rising army

to wage siege-war
against the fortress
of “Kaanapper Koyil,”

and how that affair
was brought to its full stop.

Was it the enemy's wonder?
Or the poet's wonder?

Read —
and we shall know!

★ ★ ★

Chinna Marudu and Periya Marudu
ruled the Sivaganga country —

two lion-hearted great warriors
who bloomed as red-blood flowers
in the history of red soil.

At Kalaiyar Koyil,
where statues stand for both,

in ancient Tamil Nadu
there was another name:

<!-- scan 290 -->

“Kaanapper.”

Even now,
it is a land of *Maravars*
that launches wars of honour!

There,
a minor Vel chieftain
named Vengai Marban

built a fortress
of towering fame

and issued a challenge:
who could conquer it?

One might pierce an atom
and enter inside it —

but to enter
that fortress
was impossible,

and warrior after warrior
stood bewildered!

At that time,
the king ruling Pandya country
was Ukkira Peruvazhuthi!

A lion
upon the battlefield!

Strong enough
to strike elephants
and toss them about!

For enemies
to escape his sword
and remain alive
was rare — rare indeed!

With overflowing strength
and a learned artistic heart,

he gained fame,
the source says,
by compiling
the poems of *Akananuru*.

The presentation-festival
of the good verses
of the undying lamp,
*Tirukkural*,

is also said
to have taken place
in his presence!

One day,
Avvai saw him seated
with Cheraman Marivenko,

strung and placed upon them
a garland of refined Tamil verse,

and blessed there
the unity
of the Three Crowned Kings.

The fish-bannered king
Ukkira Peruvazhuthi

was lover
to a fish-eyed maiden.

One day,
while reading
a bedchamber epic
with that beloved,

she hung a question mark
from the corner of her eye
and said:

“My lord of my eyes!
Clear one doubt for me.”

<!-- scan 291 -->

“What is it, my dear?
What doubt do you have?

Are you asking me
how you came to possess
such softness,
like the feather
of that swimming swan?

Or do you wish to know
how honey came
to your lips,

though bees never
brought and poured it there,

yet I scoop it up
and drink it?

Was your face made
by looking at a red lotus?

Or, my radiant one,
was the red lotus itself
created only after looking
at your face —

is that your doubt?

Unable to bear
the two round globes,

will your waist,
writhing like the worm
upon a hook
cast for a kendai fish
in a pond,

continue to stand?

Or will your shining
ivory thighs
strike it
and hold it up?

These are all
the doubts growing
in your mind,
are they not?”

So asked Vazhuthi.

Before him,
she bowed her head
with modesty,
like a curved ray,

and said:

“I did not raise a question
so that you might write poetry
upon me and win a prize!

Tell me:
in delightful Tamil,
what does *eyil* mean?”

“Ah, foolish peahen!
Do you not know even this?

To stop an enemy army,
we build a fort
around the capital —

that is called *eyil*,”
said the king!

“I know that too.
But there is one thing!

There is said to be
an *eyil*
that no one can break;

and a king,
bubbling with pride,

is said to boast
that even ‘this man’
cannot come near it.
Do you know of that?”

<!-- scan 292 -->

When she said
“even this man,”

she pointed at Vazhuthi
and held him tight!

“That news
too has reached my ears.

The fort is said
to have been built
at a city called Kaanapper.

Our court poet
Aiyur Moolankizhar
has made a rare description
of it!

I shall tell it to you.
Listen:

in the order of years,
it deserves first place!

A long moat
deeper than the depths
of the earth;

high walls
rising to touch
the blue sky;

upon them,
missile-platforms
working swiftly
like the stars
crowding that sky;

and a guard-forest
so dense
that even sunlight
finds no place to enter;

surrounded as well
by countless little
supporting forts —

such is the Kaanapper *eyil*,
which will not move
even if a thousand elephants
butt against it!

Many times
I have rejoiced
hearing our poet
describe it
in poetic Tamil,”
said the king!

If, after laying siege,
one goes down
into the moat
surrounding the fort,

defying the resistance,

stones from slings
and rain-showers of arrows
fall from the walls above.

If one gets past those,

boiling oil
and molten lead
come rattling downward!

<!-- scan 293 -->

If one overcomes all that
and begins climbing
toward the top
of the fort,

the ladders are smashed.

Even after that,
metal-bird machines,
with beaks,

stab at the crown
of one's head.

Escaping all of those,
one must still descend
inside the fortress
on the other side!

“The Pandyas can see
enemies far away;

you bring foes
like tigers inside cages.

Yet nearby,
in the city called Kaanapper,

Vengai Marban
has built a fortress,
raised his sword,

and cries so loudly
the town shakes,

saying no one,
with any army,
can come near him!

After he has indirectly
mocked our courage,

would it not be shameful
for us to remain saying,
‘What is that to us?’

The doubt
that had lain for many days
at the bottom
of my heart's depth

I have brought
to its full stop today!

For a king
who can confront
even a rutting elephant
and split its forehead,

what is the wall
Vengai Marban has built —

the Vindhya mountain range?

Even if the moat
he has dug
is a deep ocean,

for my Pandyan
it is merely
a distance to cross!

Then why,
seeing the arrogance
of Vengai Marban
who issues the challenge,

should he lie quiet?”

<!-- scan 294 -->

“Let the king
clear this doubt.
After that,
we may issue the decree,”

she said,
sulking!

The Pandyan
caught the woman
who tried to move away,

held her tight,
interlaced their legs,

and said:

“Let morning blossom!

I shall order
our infantry,
elephants,
horses and chariot-force

to set out
toward the Kaanapper *eyil*.

Leave your worry!

Now,
take away this hand
that is trembling.”

She took it away!

Within the blink
of an eye,
the sulking was forgotten —

and union continued
until the rays
of morning spread!

The next day,
just as he had promised,

the ranks of the army
set out
so that Pandimadevi's wish
might be fulfilled!

They struck
the heavily guarded fortress
called Kaanapper *eyil*,

waged a fierce war,

and brought the siege
to its end.

Under the determined attack
of Ukkira Peruvazhuthi,

the defences
of Vengai Marban's fort
were shattered!

The Pandyan king
returned to tell
the victory-news

to the queen
who had placed
the victory mark
upon his forehead
and sent him forth.

Upon the wounded chest
of the king,

she gave pressing comfort
again and again
with the mound
of her own body!

Every honour-wound
from which blood still seeped

received her kisses
and healed completely!

★ ★ ★

Some days later,
Aiyur Moolankizhar
came to the Pandyan court.

<!-- scan 295 -->

“King!
What greatness —
that enemies who did not respect you
now respect and praise you!

O king
who knows nothing
but victory
when you enter the field!

From today,
let the epics sing you
as:

‘Ukkira Peruvazhuthi
who crossed the Kaanapper *eyil*!’

Let the poetry-stage
praise you!

As long as the sun exists,
let that name endure!”

So spoke the poet.

“Thank you, poet!”
said the king with humility,

and asked him:

“Vengai Marban,
who has been diminished
in this war —

will he,
spitting sparks of anger,

make another battlefield
to recover the Kaanapper *eyil*?”

The answer
Aiyur Moolankizhar gave

presented Mother Tamil
with a rare simile!

What was it?

If one drop of water
falls upon iron
heated by a smith
in the furnace-fire,

can that water
ever be recovered?

(In the same way),

the Kaanapper *eyil*
that Ukkira Peruvazhuthi
has conquered

has become
like a drop of water
absorbed by forge-heated iron!

“Vengai Marban
stands grieving in his heart,
speaking of your greatness,”
said the poet.

What prize
could the Pandyan give
worthy of the beauty
of that simile
bestowed by the golden poet?

I say
he must have struggled
not knowing!

Is there an error
in my thought?

You who know
the taste of Tamil —
tell me!
