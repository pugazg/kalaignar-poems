## Item 31 — The Mother Full of Dignity and the Stainless Son!

- stable item: **31**

- contents witness: `மாண்பு நிறை தாயும், மாசற்ற மகனும்!`
- canonical file: `../../../sections/31.md`
- physical scans: **148–151**
- printed pages: **147–150**
- title-witness status: **documented discrepancy — contents witness adds a comma after `தாயும்`; canonical/title-page witness does not**

### English translation

<!-- scan 148 -->

The mother full of dignity
and the stainless son!

All the beauty of nature —
as darkness and light;

as sky and cloud;
as sun and moon;

as sea and land —
thus appearing

in unlike pairs,
two by two,

and then seeming
to mingle without distinction —

not taking on some giant form,
but taking graceful form and smiling;

with beauty that sways like waves,
with hair flowing long like a waterfall,

with two buds
upon the front of her body,
with skin like silk,

with coral-red lips,
and teeth like a string of pearls —

whatever further beauties
might add beauty to her beauty,

all those beauties
arrayed together and fashioned
into a woman's form —

<!-- scan 149 -->

when we call that green parrot Madhavi,
and speak of the girl born to her

with the many-skilled Kovalan,
who shared her as half his life,

what words could there be
to celebrate the beauty
of their daughter Manimekalai,

even if ranks upon ranks of poets
were summoned to praise her?

Not only they —
even the Tamil they had learned
would stand astonished
before such immense beauty!

★ ★ ★

In that splendid household
where joy poured like a waterfall,

the lovers' quarrel
that arose between Madhavi and Kovalan
at the Indra festival

left no place thereafter
for them to come together again
in life as wife and husband;

Madhavi became
like a world without night,
her heart broken,
and turned renunciant.

Even after deciding
that household life was no more for her,

as though a thorn
had pierced her heart,

a young bull,
intoxicated with love like liquor,
crossed into the life
of Madhavi's daughter Manimekalai;

Madhavi withered,
not knowing what to do.

Declaring that his very heart
loved Manimekalai,

Udayakumaran,
the Chola prince,
wrote a letter to Mekalai:

“O coloured statue worthy of worship!
O nectar of heaven!
I shall come this evening
to meet you;

O jackfruit ripened at the root,
I come to place my request
before you face to face.”

<!-- scan 150 -->

He sent that letter to Mekalai —
and Mekalai's body trembled!

Before he could arrive,
she longed to alter
her bodily beauty completely,
and did so;

made up in an illusory guise
like her Kali-like companion
named Kaya Chandikai,

she came and stood before Udayan
inside a darkened hall —
in the corridor where food was served.

Though the prince
had learned the many arts,
in his haste he failed to discern

the marks that distinguished
Kaya Chandikai from Mekalai.

He came near Manimekalai,
who was there giving food
to people without support;

he called her “Beloved,”
he wept and spoke to her;
he asked,
“Why have you become so dishevelled?”

Just then
the true husband of Kaya Chandikai
arrived there.

In an unexpected calamity,
her husband Vidyadharan,
thinking that his own wife
had entered into an improper relationship
with the prince,

violently pulled Manimekalai aside;
then, drawing the sword
already fastened at his waist,

he cut off the prince's head
before anyone could stop him,
and escaped.

“I have ended the story
of that rogue Udayakumaran
who played with the chastity
of my wife Kaya Chandikai!”
he roared.

Poor Manimekalai fainted!

<!-- scan 151 -->

When Sembiyan Nedumudikilli
heard the news,

he did not let his heart convulse
because his only son's life
had ended in this way.

“The punishment
I myself ought to have given —
as though the country itself had given it,
someone has given it,”
he said, consoling himself.

Nor did it stop there.
In the confusion of mind
that made him think
Manimekalai herself
was the cause of all this,

he imprisoned her too;
his wife's words,
it is said,
were one cause of that as well.

Then Aravana Adigal —
a man of discipline
whom wise elders and learned people
revered and worshipped —

advised the king.
Listening to him,
the king not only released Manimekalai,

but also enabled her once more
to continue the humanitarian work
of relieving the people's hunger.

Mekalai,
who had become a mother of compassion,
was welcomed by Ilangkilli,
king of Kanchi;

he even built for her
the Buddhist pedestal
that had lived as her dream.

Is not the history
of Madhavi and Mekalai —
that mother and daughter
full of dignity —

a stainless and glorious history
that proclaims the greatness
of the honour-filled Tamil land?

## Item 32 — Kovoorar Questions — Heads Bow Down

- contents witness: `கோவூரார் கேள்வியும் குனிந்திடும் தலையும்!`
- canonical file: `../../../sections/32.md`
- physical scans: **152–156**
- printed pages: **151–155**
- title-witness status: **documented substantive discrepancy — canonical/title-page witness is verbal and has no final exclamation mark; contents witness is differently worded and ends with an exclamation mark**

### English translation

<!-- scan 152 -->

Kovoorar questions —
heads bow down.

The grammatical discipline
of the Tamil language,
and its matchless literary density,

are lamps that stand illuminating
the good conduct of the Tamils
and their civilisation and culture
of worthy distinction —

so the scholar
Olvai Duraisami has explained.

That is not a mere opinion;
it is a valuable opinion —
a fragrant thought born of knowledge.

I shall give only one or two examples
as proof of it;
seeing our stature and pride,
you will say,
“Good! Good!”

★ ★ ★

Poets and bards —
when we speak of them,
they are masters
at composing verse and shaping melody;

in general,
they are good to everyone.

Is it *puram*?
Is it *akam*?
Is it the mode of the battlefield?

Taking up cool Tamil
mixed into their very blood,
they will call like cuckoos;

at times,
they will also spring like tigers!

Resolved,
they will swim forward
through the flood of their own blood,
longing for final victory
to belong to the Tamils!

Such poets today
may be very few in number;
but in those days —

<!-- scan 153 -->

they stood high
both in number and in thought.

Poets who could set a poem to mode
in the blink of an eye —

the songs they fashioned
in many-hued Tamil
could turn all our thoughts
into blazing fire
and send enemies running.

Young Nedunchezhiyan,
who led the battle of Talaiyalanganam,
was himself naturally a poet;

and what a vow he uttered,
so that the fame of his youth
would never be forgotten!

“If I do not bring down the enemies
in this war
and crown the honour of our people
with victory,

let the people beneath my parasol
revile me, saying,
‘Our sovereign is a cruel man!’

Let the great learned ones —
the great poets —

refuse to sing in wonder
of the land that I rule!

Let this country disgrace me
as one without the virtue of giving,

as one who said ‘No’
to those who came saying,
‘We have nothing!’”

For this *puram* song,
which could turn even a worm
into a tiger,
to thunder forth,

Nedunchezhiyan,
who ruled the earth,
made himself a poet as well.

★ ★ ★

<!-- scan 154 -->

Not only when war had already broken out —
whenever royal elder and younger brothers
stood opposed to one another in the field,

poet Kovoor Kizhar
would be there!

Was it not that great poet,
that master of the threefold Tamil,
who first prevented the brothers
Nalankilli and Nedunkilli
from clashing with one another?

When Nedunkilli,
failing to recognise the poet Ilanthathan,

mistook him for a spy
and hurriedly ordered
that he be put to death,

it was the learned Kovoor Kizhar —
versed also in the art of politics —

who earned the distinction
of preserving, for the time being,
both the justice of Nedunkilli's rule
and Nedunkilli himself
without his head bowing!

★ ★ ★

After defeating
Malaiyaman Tirumudi Kari,

when King Killivalavan ordered
that Kari's children — his treasures —
be put beneath the feet
of a rutting elephant and killed,

it was Kovoorar
who rushed in like lightning
and intervened.

That is why,
for people such as us,
he is accepted even now
as a poet who lives forever
in the history of the sweet Tamil people;

we praise him:
may he live forever!

★ ★ ★

<!-- scan 155 -->

In another war that followed,
afraid and trembling to face the attack,
Nedunkilli fled
from his capital at Avur
to Uraiyur!

Experience warned Nalankilli
that it was dangerous
to leave deceit behind
like a snake that had been struck;

he accepted that counsel
and followed him.

One Chola laid siege to the fort;
another Chola set his seal upon the fort!

Between the two armies
stood the eminent Tamil poet
Kovoor Kizhar.

He spoke outside the fort;
inside it too,
he opened his pure heart!

What did he say?
What argument did he place before them?

That is what now sounds
in this poem!
It sounds gently,

striving to put an end
to the enmity of the two:

“You are not one
wearing the white-spathed palmyra flower;

nor is he one
wearing a garland
of dark-branched neem.

The garland upon your chest
is the same as his;
the garland of the man
who fights you
is the same as yours.

If even one of you is defeated,
it is your clan that is defeated.

For both of you to win
is not in the nature of things;
therefore this deed of yours
is no good to your clan.

This quarrel will bring
heart-swelling delight
to other kings like you,
kings of bannered chariots.”

<!-- scan 156 -->

Its meaning:

“You two stand
face to face
on the battlefront.

One of you
is not the Chera king
who wears a garland
of palmyra flowers!

Nor is the other
the Pandya king
who wears a neem-flower garland.

Both of you wear
the same *aathi* garland,
one like the other;

yet you have come arrayed
against one another.

Even if one of you loses,
it is the Chola clan
that loses!

Can both of you
win this war?
Tell me!”

At Kovoorar's question,
the heads of both kings bowed.

They bowed —
but if we ask whether
their frenzy for war subsided,

Tamil soil was drenched once again
by the flood of their blood!

Only for a short time
was there a season of peace.

So long as people refuse
to honour Kovoor Kizhar's voice —

“If even one of you is defeated,
it is the clan that is defeated” —

so long as they refuse to honour
that “humanitarian feeling,”

we shall say
that ceasefire talks
have no meaning
in human society!

## Item 33 — Is “Seruppaazhi-Erindha” an Honorific Title?

### English translation

<!-- scan 157 -->

Is “Seruppaazhi-Erindha”
an honorific title?

Scholars marvel with delight
at the distinguished Chola empires
that stood upright
in the history of Tamil Nadu —

at a Chola history
that gained a foremost place
in Tamil history;

that was filled with flavour,
and, to the same degree,
filled with sorrow;

that praised and celebrated
the Tamil language,
and later pushed Tamil aside
and accepted another language;

that guarded art and civilisation;
that offered distinction
in justice and administration
and showed the way;

that accepted with victorious joy
the gifts poured out
by kings of the northern lands;

that crossed the sea
and entered battlefields,
and ate the fruit of victory
on every one of those fields;

that established reigns
lasting many years
and made them flourish in fame.

★ ★ ★

With the special appreciations
of Navalar Somasundara Bharathiyar
and the historian Nilakanta Sastri,

<!-- scan 158 -->

I had the opportunity to read
Dr. Ma. Rasa Manickanar's
history of the Cholas.

He says the age
of the earliest Chola kings
belongs in relation
to the age of Tolkappiyar,
more than a thousand years BCE.

Among the first line,
he places the Cholas
before the third century BCE —
Sibi, Musukundan, Kandan and Sembiyan —

and in the third century BCE,
one called
“Ilancetcenni who was Seruppaazhi-Erindha.”

Accepting the second century BCE
as belonging to Manu Needhi Cholan
and the first Karikalan,

he calls the first-century Chola
the second Karikalan;

then calls the third-century Chola,
a crowned king,
“Ilancetcenni who was Seruppaazhi-Erindha,”

and joins him with Cholas
such as Nalankilli and Nedunkilli,
whose period he places
from CE 1 to CE 150.

Seeing him set it out this way,
only one doubt arose in me!

You ask what doubt that was?
It was nothing very great;

but because the thought
that came to this humble man
should not trouble someone else next,
I shall explain it.

When all the kings
are called with epithets
that overflow with distinction,

<!-- scan 159 -->

what is this?
Why, for Ilancetcenni Chola alone,
has the title
“Seruppaazhi-Erindha”
been deliberately attached
as though it carried
slightly less worth?

It seemed to me
like a lowering,
a “slide” downward —

as though its meaning
somehow belittled Ilancetcenni.

All this caused confusion in my mind.

Later I looked at *Akananuru*
and cleared up my own confusion;
then I came running
to clear up yours
before it could arise.

There is no need
for even the smallest confusion
about “Seruppaazhi-Erindha.”

Do not think of the word
as `seruppu + aazhi` —
“shoe + ocean.”

Instead,
`seru` means “war,” does it not?

Join that with the dictionary meaning
of `paazhi` as “military camp,”

and with the Tamil explanation
that `erindha` means “conquered.”

Put them together:
`seru + paazhi + erindha` —
Ilancetcenni.

The joy I felt
when I understood this —
was it not joy discovered
in our Tamil,
which is dear as life itself!

The history of sacred India —
particularly because it began
from the middle,

and more particularly
because it failed to begin
from Tamil Nadu —

became like the story
of trying to grow a tree
by pouring water
upon the tip of a branch,
without reaching trunk or root!

Or else it became
like an event in which
hot water was poured
and the root itself was boiled!

<!-- scan 160 -->

It was in Ilancetcenni's time
that the Chola army
had to enter the field
against the Mauryas!

The Mauryas dreamed among themselves
that, with Chandragupta's valour
and Chanakya's cleverness
as favourable ground,

Tamil Nadu too
could be brought down.

They even broke through
the mountains that stood
as obstacles before them
and advanced toward Mogur!

Near the Athur pass
of South Arcot district
lay Mogur,
where the powerful Chola army
had its military camp.

The Chola saw that
if the place established as the `paazhi`
were “struck down” and conquered,
the enemy Mauryas
would bow and flee.

When the enemy host
came with its army,
he sprang upon it
like floodwater plunging
into a hollow,

and attacked on many fronts.

The Mauryas yielded;
they hid with their army!

They withdrew
from the Mogur camp;

they fled
with their heels striking
the backs of their heads!

It was on seeing
that victory won by Ilancetcenni
that Tamil poets,
with rising pride,

fashioned the resounding distinction
“Seruppaazhi-Erindha Cenni”
as a glorious honorific,

crowned that lion-like Tamil
with the title,
and rejoiced,
singing a song of delight!

## Translator notes

- Item 31 preserves the canonical/title-page punctuation witness without the contents comma after `தாயும்`. The English displayed title also preserves the canonical lexical reading `மகனும்` as **son**. This is source-sensitive because the body and closing lines speak of Madhavi and Manimekalai as mother and daughter; the verified Tamil title is not silently changed to `மகளும்`.
- Item 31 retains Kalaignar's sensual descriptive imagery for Madhavi/Manimekalai, Udayakumaran's love-letter imagery, Manimekalai's disguise as Kaya Chandikai, Vidyadharan's fatal misrecognition, and Nedumudikilli's severe response without sanitising or harmonising the narrative with outside retellings.
- Item 32 preserves the substantially different title witnesses separately. The displayed English title follows the canonical/title-page witness `கோவூரார் கேள்வியுறும் - குனிந்திடும் தலையுறும்`, not the contents wording `கோவூரார் கேள்வியும் குனிந்திடும் தலையும்!`.
- The compact classical verse on scan 155 is translated as a distinct quotation and followed, as in the source, by Kalaignar's own `இதன் பொருள்` explanation. The source's verified compact forms are not silently normalised.
- `அகம்`, `புறம்` and the `ஆத்தி` garland are retained in transliterated/culturally traceable form where an ordinary English substitute would erase the literary or dynastic signal.
- Item 32 preserves the repeated star separators and the sequence of Kovoor Kizhar's interventions: Nalankilli/Nedunkilli, Ilanthathan, the children of Malaiyaman Tirumudi Kari, the Avur–Uraiyur siege, the quoted poem and the closing humanitarian/ceasefire argument.
- Item 33 deliberately retains **Seruppaazhi-Erindha** in the displayed English title because the poem's central movement depends upon decomposing the Tamil sound-form. The false segmentation `செருப்பு + ஆழி` is rendered “shoe + ocean,” while the source's preferred segmentation `செரு + பாழி + எறிந்த` is preserved as `seru + paazhi + erindha` with its source-supplied senses “war + military camp + conquered.”
- The chronology, Maurya/Chola account, Mogur identification and historical claims in Item 33 are translated as the final-cleared Tamil presents them. No outside historical correction or reconciliation has been inserted.
- No final-cleared Tamil canonical file or verified page record was modified while preparing this batch.
