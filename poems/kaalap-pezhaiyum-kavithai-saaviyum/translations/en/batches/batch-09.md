## Item 22 — “Why, Then, the Question? — That Is My Question!”

- canonical file: `../../../sections/22.md`

### English translation

<!-- scan 112 -->

I, I, I alone —
I keep moving forward.

When the tongue bends and straightens,
saying I, I, I alone,

it is not with arrogance —
but with humility,
with great humility.

How did this “I” come to be?
Where did I come from?

Above all these questions
there is one fundamental question —

that is the question,
“Why did I come?”
Only because I came
can I ask, “Why did I come?”

Only by asking
does an answer arise.
Then did the question come first?

Or did the answer come first?
Ask “Why?” — the answer is “Because of this!”

Ask “How?” — the answer is “Like this!”
Which appeared first — the egg or the hen?

An explanation for that
has already been given;
what stands before us now —
is it the question, or the answer?

<!-- scan 113 -->

Can an answer arise
without a question?
What is this called?

Is this the question?
The universe — this is the answer!

Only because this answer already exists —
or because it existed —

could we look at something
and ask, “What is this?”

When there was nothing,
no question arose.
Since no question arose,
no answer appeared.
And so,

there was a state
in which neither question nor answer
was needed —
and I imagine that I too
was in that very state,

clinging to it like a shadow,
not one year,
not two,
not a thousand,

not a lakh,
but crore upon crore —
when, five hundred crore years ago,

this world is said
to have come into being;
and when, eighty crore years ago,

life is said to have appeared —
among all that arose,
I imagine myself as the first life,

and through those eighty crore years
I have floated as air with air,
mingled as water with water,

been heat with heat,
and cold with cold;

<!-- scan 114 -->

been darkness with darkness,
and light with light;

playing with sky and earth,
crawling, writhing,
going on my knees,
creeping,

walking,
and moving forward.
Who can say
that a full stop
has been placed upon my progress?

Even of eighty crore years ago,
Manikkavasagar,
melt and melted in song
of life's growth:

“becoming grass,
becoming shrub,
becoming worm...”

If the full stop
had been placed even then,
what conversation could there be
between you and me now?

In my journey,
shall I call it a walking journey?

A moving journey?
A crawling journey?
A flying journey?
Since “journey” in general
fits them all,

I say only this:
my journey has gone on
for many crore years —
five hundred crore years —

and that within it
I appeared as the first life
eighty crore years ago:
how, and to whom,
can I prove that?

Who among you was there then?
And who among us now
will be there hereafter?

So is “many crore years”
not itself a compromise agreement?

It lacks an exact reckoning;
but is not “an uncountable span of years”
itself an acceptable reckoning?

<!-- scan 115 -->

In the Bhagavad Gita,
Parthaman piled up examples
saying, “I am everything”;
no one should misunderstand
this “I” in that manner.

Perhaps Manikkavasagar,
to travel safely backward
through the stages of development,
began first by saying
“becoming a worm”!

Across these many crore years,
how many, how many lists
of stagewise development
have I been able to see
upon my journey!

A bridge-like land link,
said to have existed until 12,000 BCE,
joining North America
and East Asia,
was submerged in the Ice Age;

and because East Asia
had been connected with North America
until then,
marks of Maya civilisation
having spread there —
these too I can see
as one part of my journey!

From worm to human,
from monkey to human,
I have been carrying this journey
from the time I was a worm —
crawling, writhing —
so that I may tell you too
the meaning of stagewise development,
leaning not on one or two supports,
but on many!

The universe,
where countless planets
including our world wander,
keeps no family planning;

who knows how many more planets
it will bear and bring forth
as children!
Only as the journey goes on
without stopping
will we come to know.

<!-- scan 116 -->

In this journey
of crore upon crore of years,
I have seen
worms writhing in the soil,

birds spreading wings in the sky,
animals roaming in the forest,
creatures that crawl
and creatures that swim,

and the human race —
younger in age than all these,
yet regarded, within our own measure,
as the complete one —

and the divisions within that race,
and the cultures
each of those divisions honours;

seeing,
rejoicing,
grieving,
being darkened by sorrow,
shedding tears,

I carry many kinds of feeling
and continue the journey —
together with you!

As I travel forward,
I turn for a moment
and look behind.

The learned call this
an *arima nokku* — a lion's backward glance;
this humble servant's glance
may even be an *ariya nokku* —
a glance hard to know.

There is no selfish aim in it;
there is a view toward Tamil history.

So do not take it
as a narrow view;
take it as the view
of my principle!

The reason for that view
stands spread before us
as the answer —
“Why, then, the question?”
That is my question!

## Item 23 — Kundalakesi, Who Speaks the Strength of Feminism!

### English translation

<!-- scan 117 -->

Kovalan, great lord among merchants;
his beloved companion,
Madhavi, the truthful mistress of dance;

Kannagi, queen among women,
who came to him
as the sacred lamp of life;

and, born in their line,
the woman of the noble clan of language,
of inexhaustible fame —
Manimekalai,

an unattached ascetic
who gave no room in human society
for anyone's reproach —

with these come the sounding pair,
*Silambu* and *Mekalai*;
with them shines as a twin
*Seevaka Chintamani*;

there is the rare Tamil literary work
*Valayapathi*;
and *Kundalakesi*,
about the woman
who brought patriarchy down.

Jainism, Buddhism,
Saivism and Vaishnavism —
when great conflicts arose among them,

though the poets nurtured by the Sangam,
and learned masters
joined to every art,
needed time
to come to honour equality,

before that time arrived,
alas,
Tamil land had lost
two of the Five Great Epics.
What a wonder!

How did *Valayapathi* bend away from us?
How was the form of *Kundalakesi* broken?

Did the termite called time devour them?
Or did the carelessness of the Tamils
stand as the cause?

For more than sixty years
this humble servant searched,
wandering without tiring —

<!-- scan 118 -->

and afterwards,
a torn leaf came into my hands.
In it was a favourite son
of the palace preceptor,

one who had made murder,
robbery, rape and abduction
into the arts by which
he spent his life.

When he was captured,
his hands and feet shackled,
and dragged through the street
to be kept under guard,

she saw him —
the maiden bearing two hills
upon her breast,

the virtuous woman called Kundalakesi,
whose other name was Pathirai.

Her father,
the great minister,
would call her “Amudham” — nectar —
pouring out his love.

That nectar came to desire poison:
she fell and lost herself
in the beauty of the beast
who passed before her in chains.

She bowed at the feet
of her father the minister,
won his favour,
and had the king's punishment
set aside.

Pathirai,
whose heart was stolen
by the glitter of the serpent,
became the maiden of his bedchamber;

together the two
drained deep
the drink of pleasure's nectar.

One day,
in a lovers' quarrel in the bedchamber,
while they held each other close,

“You are a thief, aren't you?” she said —
not as a harsh word,
but as a word of love.

Black anger rose in him,
though he did not show it;
he embraced her
until her full breasts pressed deep,

and said,
“Beloved, come;
shall we go see
the nearby mountain peak?”

When he called,
she clung to him and went;

the two winged birds
reached the summit —

<!-- scan 119 -->

“Shame! Shame!
You mocked me as a thief;
for that I am going
to push you from this peak —
you will be shattered!”
he said.

Within a few moments
that woman,
whose heart was a garland
of cool flowers,
understood the truth.

Deceit and intrigue,
she resolved,
must be defeated
not with a sword,
but by deceit itself.

“Life of my life!
Rare medicine of my being!
Before you take away my life,
let me have a little time
to worship my husband
and rise,” she asked.

The robber —
the man without virtue —
gave it to her.

With great skill
she acted out the sham drama
of a woman bound by devotion,
full of worship toward her husband.

When half her circling was done,
she planned
to take the sinner's life;

while he waited
for the other half of the circuit,
she pushed him
from the mountain rock's summit.

That great sinner died.

Though the literature of *Kundalakesi*,
which declared the strength of feminism
against arrogant patriarchy,
no longer exists,

this Kundalakesi
remains as its grammar —
its living rule.

She went on
to spread Buddhist doctrine,
winning many debates,

and later
brought her life
to fulfilment.

## Item 24 — This Tamil Land, Two Thousand Years Ago!

### English translation

<!-- scan 120 -->

If we turn and look back
at the history of roughly
two thousand years ago,

we see, as though ceremonial lamps
had been lit upon the sea,
ships of every country
standing there in rows;

both eyes shine at the sight.
In art, civilisation and culture
we may stand victorious —

and I give you news of Tamil Nadu
that cools the ear with delight:
maritime trade,
overseas relations across the sea,
art and literature,

a rule and splendour
that entered many battlefields
and wore the garland of victory —

as these gather like clusters of fruit,
I too savour them together with you!

Kannagi's chastity,
Madhavi's dignity,
and, through the words
of the great poet Karikkannar,

the attachment, affection,
friendship and relations between states
that grew between Pandya and Chola kings —
we have come to know them all
and rejoice.

In defensive fortification,
in the skill of regulation,
there was no contradiction
in Kaveripoompattinam.

And when it comes to wealth of art —
Madhavi, of fame beyond imagination!
The very list of jewels
with which she had adorned herself
is enough to make us dizzy;

let us see at least a few of them
in *Silappathikaram*.
Here they are:

<!-- scan 121 -->

Imagine the moon herself,
adorning herself with jewels,
standing before a full-length mirror —

and hide yourself away
and look!

Upon the feet,
fittingly,
*pariyagam*, *silambu*, *padagam*,

*ariyagam* and the ornament called *sathangai*;
upon her beautiful thighs,
tightly set,
*kurangu-seri*.

A pearl-string
with thirty-two *virisigai*;
at her waist,
a gleaming *mekalai*
showing the beauty before it.

On the shoulder,
a bracelet joined with pure gems,
and the *kandigai*;

earrings of ruby;
a *sudagam* set with diamond.

Upon the neatly ordered forearm,
many kinds of bangles —

diamond bangles,
conch bangles,
gold bangles,
nine-gem bangles,

coral bangles —
so many kinds upon her arm;
and upon her face,
a smile that outshone
all that gold!

A curved ring
like the split mouth of a vaalai fish;
a ruby signet-ring
glowing in rich red;

upon fingers lovely as *kaanthal* flowers,
the radiant emerald-finger ornament,
surrounded by fitting diamonds.

Firm and delicate chains;
on the ornamented arm,
flower-worked *savadi* and *sarappani*;

upon the pearl-garlanded neck,
a chain falling elaborately behind;
a clustered *Chandrabani*
diamond ear ornament;

between indra-neelam stones,
a beautiful conch appearing here and there —

magic or marvel,
these ornaments continuing
as *thoyyagam* and *pullagam*,
beautifying Madhavi's beauty,

are they not truly
emblems that reveal
the refinement of Tamil civilisation?

Is it not like the feeling
of breathing in
a gentle flower-scented breeze
within our hearts?

<!-- scan 122 -->

Is that all?

What was the arrangement
of the stage
upon which that beauty, Madhavi,
danced?

A place was chosen
that did not differ in the least
from the principles
laid down by masters of sculpture.

Bamboo born and grown
on Pothigai hill
was cut to the proper measure,
leaving a *saan* of space
between each node;

it was trimmed precisely,
reckoning four finger-breadths
from the thumb,

and that itself
was used as the measuring rod
for the stage.

Seven rods in width,
eight rods in length,

one rod in height,
with a good interval
of four rods,

with the “standing lamp”
and the “figure curtain” —
all of it arranged
without diminishing artistic beauty —

thus was the dancing stage made,
the chapter declares;
yes,

*Silappathikaram*,
which captures the mind,
declares it!

Ah!
For the mere act of mounting the stage,
there was the dance-master,
the music-master,

the master of *iyal* Tamil,
and then
the flute-master,
the yaazh-master —

how many flawless masters,
how many accomplished experts
in the fullness of art!

<!-- scan 123 -->

That Tamil Nadu
was a land where art flourished;
that it held to honesty
without wavering from its station;

that it upheld the lofty, singular principle
that conduct must be guarded
even more than life —

Madhavi stood
not merely as one example of these.

She also stood as proof
that the field of art itself
is no enemy of chastity;

that after her man
left her and went away,
she embraced renunciation
and stood alone;

that she raised her daughter Manimekalai,
like herself,
as a queen among women
with purity of mind.

This literary history
is not merely an example of all this;

it tells us that
two thousand years ago,

when so many countries
lay immersed in darkness,
Tamil Nadu stood established
as a lighthouse,

casting light
upon the shores of all those lands,
spreading art, literature,
civilisation and culture!

It is as though
tears were sprinkled
upon the face of the sleeping Tamil
to wake him!

*Padagam* = foot ornament

*Ariyagam* = the ornament called *kaarsari*

*Virisigai* = women's waist ornament

## Translator notes

- Item 22 preserves the canonical quoted title-page witness `“பிறகேன் வினா? என்பதே என் வினா!”`; the unquoted contents witness `பிறகேன் வினா? என்பதே என் வினா!` remains separately recorded and is not hybridized with the displayed title.
- The repeated `நான்` / “I” in Item 22, the question/answer chain, egg/hen return, cosmic time figures, evolution imagery, family-planning satire about the universe, and the final backward-glance argument are retained rather than compressed into explanatory prose.
- `அரிமா நோக்கு` is rendered as ***arima nokku* — a lion's backward glance**. The following `அரியா நோக்கு` wordplay is kept as ***ariya nokku* — a glance hard to know** rather than silently losing the sound relation.
- The Manikkavasagar, Bhagavad Gita/Parthaman, Maya, Ice Age and cosmic-age references in Item 22 are translated as the source presents them; translation does not adjudicate or correct those claims.
- Item 23 retains Kalaignar's sensual and polemical imagery — including the “two hills” breast image, nectar/poison and serpent metaphors, bedchamber passage and the strategy by which Pathirai/Kundalakesi defeats the man who intends to kill her — without sanitising the source or expanding it with outside biography.
- `பெண்ணியம்` is rendered **feminism** and `திண்மை` as **strength**, preserving the title's explicit gender-political force.
- Item 24 retains the extensive ornament inventory and stage-measurement sequence instead of summarising them. Named ornaments are transliterated where an English substitute would erase the source's material vocabulary; the six source-supplied glossary definitions are preserved after the star separator.
- The historical, literary and cultural claims in Item 24 — including the image of Tamil Nadu as a lighthouse while other countries lay in darkness — are translated as Kalaignar's argument, not rewritten as an external historical assessment.
- No final-cleared Tamil canonical file was modified while preparing this batch.
