## Item 52 — A Culture That Announces an Invasion in Advance!

### English translation

<!-- scan 257 -->

A culture
that announces an invasion in advance!

“You keep circling and circling
only through Chola history;

we are those attached
to the history of the Pandyas.

Once the histories
of Pallavas and Cholas are complete,

come a little quickly
to the Pandya side” —

not merely those I favour,
nor merely those who do not favour me;

many dear to my heart
called upon me with that wish.

Among the old histories
I come carrying as verse,

I ask loving hearts
to read the poem I shaped
with the Pandyas placed first —

published as early as 1945,
when I was working
at Ayya's *Kudi Arasu* office.

In a moment
you will feel
the turbulence within me!

“Along Kaveri's bank,
        in Pennai's rushing flow,

there is no history,
        there is no history!
Alas, Tamil Nadu —

the beginning, they say,
        is at the Ganga!
Who saw the Pandyas rule?

Who carved the Cheras' prosperity?
Has anyone told
        the abundance of the Cholas?”

<!-- scan 258 -->

I wrote another poem too,
with still greater speed:

“Where is the glorious place
        where the Chera lived?
Why did the brave Pandyan's rule fall?

In the tireless land
        where the Cholas moved,
how did a mass of companionable ideals gather?

Questions exploded
        in a heart that burned......

O bulls possessed by zeal for the land!
Throw down the men seized by fear!

At the summit of the tiger's life
        let us engrave the new!”

How could one
who wrote poems like these
forget the Muthamizh Pandya,
one among the Three Crowned Kings?

★ ★ ★

Where should Pandya history begin......
with whom should that history begin first?

Those who stood
as the root-substance
for the three Sangams to sprout,

for them to blossom
as First, Middle and Last,

and those who supplied
the substance of thought as well —

were the Pandya kings of old.

Among them,
the first, the source says,
was Mudathirumaran.

There is a list
of Pandya kings said
to have founded and conducted
the First Sangam,

from “Kaisinavazhuthi”
to “Kadungon”;

but the history offers
none of the evidence required.

<!-- scan 259 -->

Even in the *Iraiyanar Kalaviyal Urai*
on the Middle Sangam,

however hard one searches
in the light,

no details of the earlier rulers
who conducted it,
apart from Mudathirumaran,
seem to emerge.

What does shine clearly
is the news that Pandyan Mudathirumaran
founded the Last Sangam!

Historians say
that this was also the time
of the second sea-deluge,

when Kumari
and the Pahruli river
were utterly destroyed.

R. Raghava Iyengar says
Mudathirumaran himself
was the Pandyan Nilandaru Thiruvin.

“After him came
the Pandyan Mudukudumi Peruvazhuthi” —

so, the source says,
U. Ve. Sa. Aiyar records it.

Poet-lords
Karikizhar,
Nettimaiyar,
and Nedumpalliyathanar

wrote poems
praising this king.

Among them is this:

“When a king comes
with his army
to besiege and attack a country,

first, so that the innocent people
of that land may know,

he announces the army's coming
through the beat of the drum —

what culture
in the conduct of war!

And now —
where has that culture hidden?”

★ ★ ★

There is a history
like a purana
about the Golden-Handed Pandyan!

I shall praise it briefly;
understand it well!

Every day,
in a changed disguise,

and even at midnight,
the Pandyan king
would come to inspect the city.

One night,
he heard a Brahmin
saying farewell to his wife
before leaving town —

<!-- scan 260 -->

“The king is there
to guard all of us.”

A month passed.
The husband returned.

Standing by the door,
the king heard
husband and wife speaking inside.

Without realising
that it was the husband,
he thought some thief
had come,

and knocked upon the door.

Then, learning
that they were husband and wife,
the king hurried away.

In the Aiyar's house,
suspicion fell upon the Ammami!

“Who was that man
who knocked at the door?”
he asked,

and the husband
came to doubt severely
his wife's chastity.

The king worried:
“Has confusion entered
that Aiyar's house because of me?”

To remove the doubt,
he went about knocking
upon the doors
of every house in the town.

The next day,
the townspeople came
to the palace
and told the king
the story of a thief
who had knocked at doors
in the middle of the night.

The king asked:
“What punishment
shall we give that thief?”

The council of ministers said:
“Cut off the hand
of the thief who knocked.”

At that very moment,
the king raised his sword
and cut off his own hand —

then opened his heart
and told the assembly
what had happened.

<!-- scan 261 -->

For the severed arm,
the townspeople fashioned
a golden hand
and fitted it to the king.

From that day,
he won fame
as the Golden-Handed Pandyan.

They wrote the puranas
with at least some order;

it was the histories
they buried away
like treasure!

★ ★ ★

Among the Pandyas
of the Last Sangam age:

Arivudainambi;

Boothapandiyan,
the incomparable poet-king
in the line of the fine poets
who gave Ollaiyur;

Ilamperuvazhuthi,
who vanished into the sea;

Nedunchezhiyan,
who crossed the Aryan army;

Pandyan,
who won the battle of Talaiyalanganam;

and rulers such as
Nanmaran
and Vetri Vel Chezhiyan —

all have already
found places
in many of my portraits in writing.

So rather than expand again
upon all that Pandya history,

let us continue
with the history of the Pandyas
who created a golden age

during the Last Sangam period
and afterwards.

★ ★ ★

An epic ancient in time;

an epic that does not begin
with an invocation to God;

an intellectual treasury
presented in the noble assembly
of Pandyan Nilandaru Thiruvin,

before elders and learned men,

<!-- scan 262 -->

with the affectionate,
sweet blessings
of the teacher Athankottasan;

that rare work
raised high and praised
by scholars —

*Tolkappiyam*.

On the day
of its presentation,

I did not merely enter
that garden of epic learning;

I invited you too,
softly saying,
“Come —
this is not the heat of summer;

it is the coolness
of the Courtallam falls.”

So,
starting from Madurai
of the Golden-Lotus Tank,

I shall speak in a series
of the great warriors
who ruled the other regions
of Pandya country as well!

## Item 53 — Tamil Escaped the Sea-Deluge; It Found the Last Sangam!

Source metadata:

- stable item: **53**
- canonical Tamil title: `கடற்கோளில் தப்பிய தமிழ்; கடைச் சங்கம் கண்டது!`

- canonical file: `../../../sections/53.md`

- printed pages: **262–269**

### English translation

<!-- scan 263 -->

Tamil escaped the sea-deluge;
it found the Last Sangam!

The sacred land of India,
it is said,

once stretched seventy *kaadams*
south of today's Kanyakumari point!

With the Kumari mountain-range
standing guard as a boundary-fort,

and beneath it
the Kumari river
and the Pahruli river,

protecting it firmly
like the double bolt
on an Ottakoothar song;

from the Santha Islands
in the southeast,

westward past Africa,

up to Madagascar Island
along the lower coast —

that vast spread of land,
it is said,

became prey
to the monstrous hunger
of the deep sea.

Wearing the attractive name
“the great disaster of the sea-deluge,”

it overturned from the roots
the Tamil people's land,
welfare,
wealth,
and strength.

The research of historians,
the source says,
untied this knot.

Across the far-spread earth
of the ancient Tamils,

Pandyan Nilandaru Thiruvin
was the one who cut
the Pahruli river,

so that hostility itself
might tremble —

a heart-gladdening piece of news!

Upon the bodies
of men and women who died
and lay buried

in thousands,
lakhs,
crores —

<!-- scan 264 -->

we are born,
we grow,
and seek a new life.

We seek our history
as manure
so that the youth of the future
may flourish.

Sun, moon,
Venus,
Rahu, Ketu,
fierce Saturn;

Aquarius,
Capricorn,
zodiac signs —
we studied them all.

We said the cycle of years
was sixty:
Prabhava, Vibhava,
and so on.

We even said
Narada and Krishna
gave birth to them as children!

All right —
all right!

But deceived,
we forgot our own history!

We failed
to tell it,
to write it;

and so today,

are we not driving crowbars
into stone-hard earth
and digging out

the history
of the good,
the able?

★ ★ ★

Along a long street
beside the Pahruli river,

Pandyan Nilandaru Thiruvin
comes at the speed of a storm
in a chariot drawn
by harnessed horses.

A guard riding toward him
dismounts,
bends,
and bows.

The king asks:

“Has everyone gathered
in the hall?”

“Your Majesty,
all the poets
are waiting
in the Athani Mandapam.”

“Has the queen departed?”

“She sits in the palace,
waiting for the king!”

<!-- scan 265 -->

“Is that so?”

After that,
the chariot did not run —
it flew down the street.

Though her face
was not red with anger,

there was no abundance
of smiles upon the queen's face!

The Pandyan understood.

“It has grown late —
shall we leave?”

“No matter;
at least after this much delay!”

The queen spoke calmly,
but the weariness was heavy.

She climbed the chariot,
beauty swaying with her.

“The work on the Pahruli river
was completed;
that delayed me a little.”

“Oh!
So the next task
is the presentation
of *Tolkappiyam*?”

“Both are the same task!”

“You say
publishing a palm-leaf manuscript
and regulating a running river
are the same?”

“Not one;
but not two
in the way you say!”

“Are the tasks
not different?”

“Understand one thing,
Tamil Queen:

what is to be presented
is not merely
a palm-leaf manuscript —

it is a manuscript
for the world!

The task may look like one thing;
its benefits are different!”

<!-- scan 266 -->

“The Pahruli river
creates the means
for people's prosperity;

*Tolkappiyam*
shows the path
for people's very lives.

What came before,
they called
the first sea-deluge.

Even so,
Kumari and Pahruli
still stand as protection.

If another sea-deluge comes,
can the great destruction
of the Pahruli and Kumari rivers
be stopped?

My heart suffers
with that fear!”

The queen answered
with pride:

“However many sea-deluges come,

none will be able
to approach and touch
*Tolkappiyam*,
which is being presented today.

Rivers,
seas,
even countries
may change direction;

but Tamil works
like *Tolkappiyam*
possess a strength
that does not perish.

No one,
nothing,
can destroy them;

forever —
*chiranjeevi*!”

Mudathirumaran,
Pandyan Nilandaru Thiruvin,

filled with boundless joy,
wanted to embrace her tightly
and put his gratitude into action.

But because they were
in the middle street of the city,

he embraced her
only with his eyes.

★ ★ ★

The richly harnessed horses,
as though understanding
the urgency of the royal family,

reached the entrance
of the Athani Mandapam
where the presentation
was to take place.

<!-- scan 267 -->

“One more sea-deluge
may come,”

that sense of fear
was present too
among the poets of the assembly;

one could feel it
from their conversation.

Athankottasan introduced
the assembly-poets
to the royal family.

Everyone called him simply
“Teacher,”
with affection overflowing!

Tolkappiyar,
Agathiyar,
Irungaiyur Karungozhi Mosi,
Vellur Kappiyan,
Sirupandirangan,
Thiraiyanmaran,
Thuvaraik Koman,
Keerandhai —

all these sat there
as poets of the Middle Sangam.

They presented
the incomparable epic
*Tolkappiyam*

in the presence
of Pandyan Nilandaru Thiruvin,

received the blessing
of Athankottasan,

and dispersed happily.

Without knowing
that within only a few months
another sea-deluge
was to come!

Though this day
shone as a golden day,

a wicked day
would soon enter,

and that day
would bring another destruction.

If the Middle Sangam poets
had known it,

would they have sung
and rejoiced
through the musical works
*Kali*, *Kurugu*, *Vendalai*,
*Viyazha Maalai Agaval*,
and many more?

They were people
accustomed to struggling
even with nature;

hearts of steel —

not merely this:
they would face anything!

<!-- scan 268 -->

Those Tamil masters
were warriors too,
experts of courage!

Their joy did not last.

As though issuing
a challenge,

six months later
nature's frenzy began
at Kavatapuram!

Nature,
possessed like a “demon,”

with a gigantic iron hand
struck dark blows
upon the waves
of the great sea.

The whole region
became a forest of darkness —

roaring like thunder,
descending like lightning,

shaking like an earthquake,
entering the sea itself
as a quake beneath the waters;

Kumari river
and Pahruli river
became one,

lost their tracks,
shifted their place,

and vanished
as though they had never existed.

Who can calculate
the result
of nature's fury?

Only after completing
all its work of destruction,

and after the sea-deluge's
“demonic dance” subsided,

did peace show its “head” —

and yes,
in that vast open stretch,

it showed the ruin
that had taken place.

There was no movement
of people.

Where had everyone gone?

Bewilderment — bewilderment!

Wherever one looked,
the laughter of death!

The Athani Mandapam,
where *Tolkappiyam*
had been presented
six or seven months earlier,

alone remained there;

all the other mansions,
upper buildings,
even the towers,

had been buried
inside the earth!

<!-- scan 269 -->

According to Adiyarkku Nallar's
commentary on *Silappathikaram*:

“Between Pahruli,
the northern boundary
of the southern face,

and the Kumari river,
across a distance
of seven hundred *kaavadams*,

because of the abundance
of their waters,

there flourished
seven coconut countries,
seven Madurai countries,
seven front-desert countries,
seven back-desert countries,
seven Kurumpanai countries” —

forty-nine small countries
in all.

The savage sea-deluge,
the source says,
completed them all
and wrote the concluding line!

Mudathirumaran
searched until exhausted
for where his queen,
Tamil Queen,
had gone.

He marked her down
as one among the people
who had disappeared
in the sea-deluge
and shed tears.

Yet he sprang up,
blazing,
with the thought:

“My Tamil Queen
may not have escaped;
my Tamil has escaped!”

He vowed:

“Let us preserve
the Tamil that escaped.”

Joining at once
with the surviving poets
of the Second Tamil Sangam,
the Middle Sangam,

he set himself
to establish
the Third Tamil Sangam —
the Last Sangam!

Thus the distinction
of having lived
at the end of the Middle Sangam,
under nature's devastation,

and at the beginning
of the Last Sangam,

belonged to Mudathirumaran,
Pandyan Nilandaru Thiruvin.

We can understand it;
we can rejoice in it too.

★ ★ ★

<!-- scan 270 -->

**Note:**

The work *Rajavali*,
a history received from Lanka,
says that a sea-deluge occurred
in the region adjoining Lanka
between 265 BCE and 161 BCE.

Another history,
*Mahavamsa*,
says there is a difference
of sixty years.

Therefore researchers say
that the second sea-deluge
of the Middle Sangam period
occurred in 150 BCE.

## Item 54 — He Who Won the Battle of Talaiyalanganam!

- canonical file: `../../../sections/54.md`

- printed pages: **270–275**
- title-witness status: **documented discrepancy — canonical/title-page witness joins `செருவென்றான்`; contents witness separates `செரு வென்றான்`**

### English translation

<!-- scan 271 -->

He who won
the battle of Talaiyalanganam!

I had written:

“Since some Pandyas
have already found a place
in many of my portraits in writing,

rather than narrating
that Pandya history
again in full detail,

let us continue
with the history of the Pandyas
who created a golden age
in the Last Sangam period
and afterwards.”

Seeing that,
some younger brothers
became angry:

“No matter —
however many times you write it,

ancient Tamil history
never tires us when we read it!

Even if many years have passed,
write it again —

do not pass beyond
our request.”

They pierced me
through the telephone
with those words.

Finding pleasure
even in that trouble,
I begin again.

★ ★ ★

From my youth itself,
I had a fascination
for the names Anbumagan
and Nedunchezhiyan.

At a time
when they had not yet
entered the movement directly,

I, as a young man,
was already involved.

Accepting the invitation
to the anniversary
of *Murasoli*,
which we had issued
as a handbill in Aarur,

both of them captivated me that day
with speeches matched in splendour!

<!-- scan 272 -->

Like Anbumagan
and Nedunchezhiyan,
I too wanted to change my name
and fulfil a long-held desire.

On the advice
of dear elder Annal Thango,

I sought Anna's permission
to change it to
“Arutselvam.”

He said:

“The name has already
become known and spread;

so do not change it now.”

I thought it over too.

Because my name
was common to people
of every religion,

and was not the name
of any particular deity,

I decided
not to interrupt matters now
with a change.

So “Karunanidhi”
remained unchanged;

and I too remained
unchanged in the Kazhagam.

In thought as well,
strong and clear,

having acquired
in the Erode and Kanchi camps
the temper to struggle,

I became
a “permanent fighter.”

Even when those
who grew the party with us
later stood apart,

I have wished them
life with dignity and distinction.

So too was the friendship
of Nedunchezhiyan,
who captivated my thought.

When I heard
that he had died,

I left the capital Delhi
that very night,

and with a heart broken apart
hurried toward his home.

A crowd without principle
surrounded the doorway,

mocked me,
raised shouts:

“Have you come now?”

Little men
with hateful hearts
like Kutilan,

barked like curs
and tried to leap forward
and bite.

<!-- scan 273 -->

Without knowing
the depth of our friendship,

they shouted thanklessly
and babbled.

Even there,
I showed patience,
paid my final respects,
and returned.

Though Navalar
had gone over
to the enemy camp,

my heart held
an incomparable affection

for the individual friend
Nedunchezhiyan,

and for that Tamil name.

So whenever the names
Nedunchezhiyan,
Karikalan,
Senguttuvan

appear in the pages
of Tamil Nadu's history,

I want their honoured names
to find a place
in my classical-Tamil verse.

To fulfil the request
of the younger brothers —

instead of a veena
plucked with the cry
“Let them return again” —

here are the extended lines
of verse,

in two parts
inside the Chest of Time!

★ ★ ★

Chera,
Chola,
Ezhini,
Thithiyan,
Irungo Venman — five;

with Porunan
and Erumaiyuran joining them,

seven armies
stood on the battlefront.

Young-bull Nedunchezhiyan
set out to meet them,

saw the battlefield,
won it,

seized the enemy drums,
fulfilled his vow,

and became
king of kings —

so sings the rare poet Nakkirar,
with delight,
in *Akananuru*!

“As though sun and moon together
had fallen upon the earth,

the great Chera and Chola kings
fell upon the battlefield” —

through a *Puram* poem,
poet Kalladanar
praises the learned young Pandyan!

<!-- scan 274 -->

“For one man to fight another,
and for one of the two to fall,
is natural!

But that one man
fought without fault
to bend and subdue seven,

that all seven lost,
and he alone won —

I call this
a wonder in this world!”

So poet Idaikkunrur Kizhar
shaped in writing
the victory of the young Pandyan.

Unable to bear the grief
of Tamil kings
colliding among themselves,

poet Kudapulaviyanar called it:

“Talaiyalanganam,
where Tamil leadership
was thrown into confusion.”

The broad-shouldered Pandyan
won in the end;

so the poet immediately
longed to embrace him.

His heroic chest,
spread wide
like a hunter's tiger-trap —

“I wish to rush forward
and embrace it,”

cried that poet
in expansive Tamil,

and embraced
the pearl-garlanded chest!

See how that Tamil poet
describes
the battlefield of Talaiyalanganam!

Arrows flew
against fierce foes;

suddenly
the enemy elephants fell.

When warriors
cut with swords
the trunks of fallen elephants
along with their mouths,

those severed pieces
lay scattered across the field,

looking,
it is said,
like ploughs
along cultivated land!

Many commanders
died and fell.

Beside them,
their sons too
lay dead.

Old women,
with wrinkled brows,

stood around,
wept and lamented;

<!-- scan 275 -->

and yet took pride
that they had borne
such warrior-sons:

“What victory
could be greater than this?”

Mangudi Maruthanar,
great poet
of Nedunchezhiyan Pandyan's court,

marvelled at the skill
of the guardian-king
who came crowned
with battlefield victory.

He rejoiced —

but when he saw
the king surrounded
by an unending desire
for battle,

he grew weary of it!

To reduce
the great Pandyan warrior's
thirst for war,

Mangudi Maruthanar
composed the work
called *Maduraikanchi*.

In a *Puram* poem
that Maruthanar sang:

“O Pandyan,
whose elephant-army
cuts through the field
like a wooden ship
cutting through the sea!

Behind it,
your spear-bearing warriors
cut off the heads
of crowned kings
and make them the hearth;

in a furnace
where blood-water boils,

they cook the flesh
of those killed in war,

stirring the food
with shoulders as paddles,

and conduct
a battlefield sacrifice!”

After praising him
in many such ways,

Maruthanar praises
his enemies as well:

“Though they received
the name ‘foes’
and were defeated by you,

because they fell
bearing honour-wounds,

they live forever
in the world of warriors!”

Thus he raised up
even those who opposed the king
and scattered pearls of verse.

“Let the king now
leave war behind
and live while sword-warriors
praise him!” he said.

<!-- scan 276 -->

“Stand as a generous giver
and give life
to those who have nothing!”
he said.

“Drink the clear liquor
given by bangle-wearing women;

let all life hold
pleasure and peace;

shine so that the world
may praise you!” he said.

“Know this:
those who guard good conduct
are the ones who live exalted;

those without it
are not to be counted
among those who live
in the world!” he said.

As the gathering
of Tamil poets praised him,

as every Tamil letter
honoured him,

the man of Talaiyalanganam lived!

Receiving *Maduraikanchi*,
which Maruthanar composed
and gave him,

the great king lived!

And when Nakkirar
sang and gave him
*Nedunalvadai*,

the young Nedunchezhiyan
lived long
and planted enduring fame
upon Tamil soil!

★ ★ ★

**Note:**

*Akananuru* — poem 36 — sung by Nakkirar.

*Purananuru* — poem 25 — sung by Kalladanar.

*Purananuru* — poem 76 — sung by Idaikkunrur Kizhar.

*Purananuru* — poem 19 — sung by Kudapulaviyanar.

*Purananuru* — poems 24, 26 — sung by Mangudi Maruthanar.
