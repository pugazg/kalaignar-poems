# The Casket of Time and the Key of Poetry

## காலப் பேழையும் கவிதைச் சாவியும் — English Translation

<!-- Reader-facing assembly from reviewed Phase 4 translation batches 01–21. -->

## Item 1 — The Common World

<!-- scan 10 -->

Space! Space! Space!
With no one watching —

with no one able to watch;
with no one at all there to see,
space lies spread everywhere!
Light came first —

from which direction?
Unknown; utterly unknown.

We gave a name to the direction it came from!
We worship the direction!

We had never seen space,
never looked upon light —

when we ourselves did not yet exist,
what power had we to see?

In the empty vastness, countless sounds!
At countless moments!

With no one hearing;
with no one able to hear —

with no one at all there to listen!

Crores upon crores of thunderclaps
crashed at a single corner;

red planets,
balls of fire,

<!-- scan 11 -->

rushed roaring,
scattering as they flew.

They say there may be nine, ten planets;
there may even be one or two more.

The world became established as one among them;
only afterwards did conflict begin!

None of this was known then;
only after so many crores of years had passed —

the thirst-stricken world asked for water!
Nature, saying “No,” poured out tears;

calling those tears “rain” instead,
the Earth found consolation!

Cooling the fever of its body,
it gathered the rainwater into ocean expanses.

Water, land, air, outer space,
fire — among the five great elements,

there is endless enmity; there is enduring kinship.
Without even a kiss, new life forms keep arriving;

change even there, change within them,
discovering the new and entering into union —

worms, insects, multiplying birds,
hordes of fierce animals —

bands of patient domestic creatures,
all drawn into the splendour of evolutionary growth.

We humans, who slowly, slowly leapt from the monkey —
let us stand upon the public stage and raise our voice of principle:
“Ownership in common — rights in common;
to call the world private property is wrong!”

## Item 2 — Stagewise Development

<!-- scan 12 -->

The age of this world in which we live,
they say, is five hundred crore years.

On the basis of the growth of civilization,
and in the stage-by-stage reckoning of living species upon the earth,

from five lakh years ago
down to roughly ten thousand years,

our old-tiger forebears divided and viewed it
as the B.C. age;

they called precisely this the Lemurian age...
The time that remained became the glorious age of civilization

when, with the civilization brought forth by Kumari Kandam,
people of the Indus expanse

had risen to a distinguished civilization.
Since there is no opposing view — if this is taken as

the ten-and-a-half-touch view,
and stamped upon the mind;

then we must accept, line by line,
the notes of the great historians who chart for us

that life appeared in this world
eighty crore years ago,

and that forty lakh years ago
our ape-man walked upon the earth.

<!-- scan 13 -->

We must indeed accept those historical scholars' markings,
line after line.

The age that appeared
five lakh years ago was the first Stone Age!

As far back as fifty thousand years ago,
the beautiful civilization of Kumari Kandam —

yes, that Kumari Kandam civilization —
budded there; and yet,

until five thousand years ago,
it smiled and flourished in the Indus expanse!

How did another name,
“Lemuria,” come to our dear Kumari Kandam?

Seeing that lemur monkeys,
which set out at night in search of food,

filled this continent everywhere —
and because, in Latin,
`lemur` was also a name for a ghost that roams by night —

it was by that name that a man called “Clater”
named the continent.

Or perhaps, changing form and raging beyond measure,
it drove a ghost-dance across great Kumari Kandam and shrank it,

shifting the land itself — and so, O Nili,
“Lemuria” alone became the one perfectly fitting name for you —

was it thus that the great professors of history
bestowed that name? I too have such a doubt, I would say!

<!-- scan 14 -->

For all this, I stand upon the historical path
clearly set out by Devaneya Pavanar,
poet-scholar of ethnic consciousness;

and with the help of the excellent book
*From Lemuria to Harappa*,

presented to me in the year ’75
by my dear friend and polyglot scholar R. Mathivanan;

with the writings of Rahul Sankrityayan as background,
and after studying many buried treasures of history —

I squeeze out their essence and offer
this continuing epic in the gait of poetry.
If those who hold differing views tell me by letter,
I shall rejoice; I shall praise them!

No change arrived merely because one woke the next morning.
For the human-ape kind to rise and stand,

not one or two Mahamagam spans —
crores upon crores of time had to pass.
Can an ape become human merely by holding a conference
and passing a resolution?

Evolutionary growth means, through many thousands of years,
keeping the form in the workshop and letting a chisel shape it — does it not?

Lemur monkeys had thirty-six teeth;
the monkey that appeared after them had thirty-four —

this is no tooth-count changed by human arithmetic;
even for that change in number,
how many centuries lay between!

Stone Age humans sprouted thirty-two teeth;
in later times, he says, the number would become only twenty-eight.

<!-- scan 15 -->

Between the ape-creature that stood propped on its tail,
learning to stand and walk upon its feet,

and becoming the human creature
that runs upon an Olympic track —

how many thousands of centuries ran by!
Is this not what they share with us under the names
“stagewise development”
and “evolutionary development”?

(Stagewise Development = Evolution)

## Item 3 — A Story of the ‘Magnet Stone’!

<!-- scan 16 -->

Egypt, Maya, Mesopotamia —
pointed to as the beginnings
of ancient civilization —

are pushed behind,
for at the very first glance
Indus Valley civilization

winks and captivates;
and so, asking where its embryonic state lay,
those who conducted severe research

found the region of Kumari Kandam
that had sunk beneath the sea
and declared, “That was its womb-land”;
we too behold it.

Mathivanan raises
a question that leaves us astonished,
and gives the answer as well.

Evolution says
that man arose from the monkey;

why then have the monkeys of today
not become humans as they did then?

When a little plant grows into a great tree,
only the topmost branch keeps climbing higher;

the side branches remain
where we saw them before —

<!-- scan 17 -->

so too does evolution,
this stagewise growth,

become a lesson
and, when reasoned through, give its answer.

Mud trampled and kneaded
becomes the wall we value and preserve!

The mud left lying where it was kneaded
does not become even a little wall —

so it is with the monkey-kind:
though one branch rises
into the human race that sits enthroned,

like the leftover kneaded mud
that remains upon the earth,
the human-ape too remains among humankind.

Before raising a mansion,
must we not dig the earth and lay a foundation!

Scholar Rahul Sankrityayan,
planning in just that way,
did not begin ancient history
with the alphabet's first lesson;

he began from six thousand years ago —
and *From the Volga to the Ganga*,
with its twenty stories,
became a treasury of thought.

Thus, taking the ideas, stories
and incomparable imaginings written by many,
and striving to gather and offer them together,

from earlier days onward,
I have told a few pearl-strung tales;

<!-- scan 18 -->

without sinking you in weariness,
I shall shape the journey onward — come travel with me!

Do not float along in a dream,
thinking that the instant your foot moves,
the casket of time will appear before your eyes

and that we can place the key of poetry in it
and simply open it — do not come with me so!

Do not leap in delight thinking,
“We can scoop up Ganga and Kaveri water,
drink deep and dance” —
only to droop in disappointment afterwards!

There is much evidence
that the threshold where civilization first learned to walk
was our Kumari Kandam!

That is why Anna, explaining Vincent Smith's statement
that even the history of India
should properly begin not from the bank of the Ganga
but from the bank of the Kaveri,

spoke under the title “By the Riverbank.”
Is it enough merely to savour the beauty of that speech?
Should not the Tamil's sense of self-respect rise up,
so that its heat and nerve do not fade away?

There was a time when man lived as a savage —
a Stone Age when men and women alike,
not knowing that showing the whole body could be called wrong,
lived with the whole body exposed;

we see too that they conveyed their thoughts
through gesture.
The first language of early man, then,
was the language of drama;

at the next stage
he began to speak the language of music;

<!-- scan 19 -->

and *iyal* came only after these —
though today we list them as *iyal, isai, natakam*;

in those days drama held first place
and gave expression to thought,
music stood forever second in that order,

and only later did *iyal* move ahead
to claim first place —
that is the history of language!

In that age when only the language of *koothu* existed —
that dark age,
the Stone Age of the past —

without hiding the condition of its people,
I shall continue by telling
a story of the “magnet stone.”

## Item 4 — The Stone Age That Was — A Better Age If It Does Not Return!

<!-- scan 20 -->

“Along the road of evolution,
where the human-ape
becomes the ape-man,

as a change to be welcomed with joy,
the ape-man who stood upright and walked
became Neanderthal man;

across the long distance of a long history,
we may grade the traveller who crossed it,

classifying and arranging him as
Stone Age man, Golden Age man,
Copper Age man, Bronze Age man —

and, carrying the construction onward
to Iron Age man,
we may write many stories!

With what exists before us,
and hiding what can be hidden as far as possible,
I have come to dance in the hall —
a dance of the pen.”

Speaking thus, the pen strode with majesty
and, swelling with pride,
opened its cap.

The master of enchanting writing
pours down a rain of words — be drenched!

<!-- scan 21 -->

Let us reckon the time as 6000 B.C.
and consider the civilizational knowledge
the world possessed then!

In the first story set against the history of the “Volga,”
the characters open their mouths and speak;
in my story they will not speak —

because Stone Age humans
had not yet acquired the power of speech!
Only after much time passes
will speech, first no bigger than a mustard seed,
later roar to the measure of an ocean!

I too saw the same rising sun
that the heart-winning writer Rahul saw.

So that my friend too might receive the joy I receive,
I said, “Come, friend — walk with me.”

The two of us went together;
we saw a woman approaching us.

I am posing no riddle;
there was no sari upon her body.

Not even tiger-skin or fox-skin
covered her golden form.

A parrot-beak for her;
a parrot's stare for us!

Why had she worn no garment at all?
Had her waist itself imposed some ban?

O woman walking at such speed,
what answer will you give, dear one? —

I hurried forward to ask;
she grew angry and walked away
like a statue itself set in motion.

<!-- scan 22 -->

Following the woman who had gone,
we hid behind a sheer-standing rock
and watched closely.

An open house with no thatch,
a dwelling lived in with no concealment,
a wasteland that did not even know
how to raise a screen for privacy!

Along the curving street the maiden went;
she entered that house,
bent and rose again,
her pointed breasts quivering as she stood!

Like trunks stretching toward grown sugarcane,
in frantic haste to satisfy hunger,

three tuskers stood with mouths agape;
three men surrounded her.

One tenderly stroked
the maiden's dishevelled head!
Another stopped the man who stroked her,
gesturing angrily,
“Do not ruin the chance we have found” —

and pressing upon her,
“Now satisfy my desire,”
he seized her slender ginger-like waist
and clasped her tightly;

she too wilted and yielded
like the stem of a water-lily!

She forced the banana-blossom bud upon him
and made it into a sharp sword
placed in a coward's hand;

then flung him away with her hand
like a frog that knew not the way.
The third man, who knew the way,
drove him off,

hunted her too,
played out the hunt to its end.....
We two friends watched all of it as spectacle...

<!-- scan 23 -->

A savage scene indeed;
yet even in this age that speaks
of a sacred grammar for chastity,

there are those who practise Stone Age civilization;
they accomplish their business in hiding,
like the molars at the back of the mouth.

Whether they speak everything openly,
or are operators who crouch, spring and gain their reward,

if even a little conscience remains,
they will not stage fearless demonstrations
in the middle of the assembly —

The Stone Age that existed six thousand years ago —
a better age is one in which it does not arise again
even in the year to come!

With a pearl-like woman, this three-man procession —
there is no count of how many times it has occurred.

Even if the woman loses every sound
and becomes a corpse, there is no case;
society itself seems to issue
a certificate of approval for that too;
there is no reckoning at all
for the loss of dignity!

Blessings upon you;
let none of that worm-ridden “revolutionary” talk
be needed here!

Let us gain the wisdom
to place chastity as a common standard for both sexes;
let no one sharpen a knife
so that people may be made to think it a petty thing!

Periyar, Anna and Bharathi
blew like a great storm insisting
that the chastity spoken of for woman
must be spoken of for man as well;

but did they issue an order
that neither sex should care about chastity at all?

<!-- scan 24 -->

The two friends,
after conducting this inquiry of ideas,
finally came to a conclusion.

Equality between man and woman
does not mean giving each an equal share
of the Halahala poison;

it is nectar that must be shared that way.
“Nectar for the devas;
poison for the asuras” —

that is an unfitting judgement of the mythic age;
this new form, flowered in revolution,
rejects that judgement utterly!

When all the debate was over
and they were returning,
one friend asked the other:

“Yes — what is that woman's name?”

“She is one who never spoke;
everything that happened was only the language of drama, friend —

for our own wish, let us give her a name.
Because what we saw was a Stone Age drama;
because she drew the men toward her;

and because the Iron Age is what will come next —
let us call her the ‘Magnet Stone’
that draws iron to itself;

first, let us move away from this place,”
said that friend!

(One who lived in the Neanderthal Valley was called Neanderthal man.)

## Item 5 — We Need a Heart of Gold; We Need the Love It Gives!

<!-- scan 25 -->

Today we take up a sheet,
fill the pen with ink,

and paint in marvellous detail
the condition in which early man lived
six thousand years ago —

what he ate, how he slept,
all that he experienced.

Calling a thousand facilities and opportunities to our aid,
we conduct our research;

then announce its conclusions with ease.
But Tolkappiyar, older even than our master Valluvar —

more than two thousand years ago,
did he not compose the *Tolkappiyam*
with a stylus?

That incomparable grammar of life,
created upon palm-leaf,
begins by saying that a one-sensed life-form
knows through the body,

and ends by saying that six-sensed knowledge
is that which can be known through the mind —
explaining every class of life
and how many senses each possesses.

That great epic,
that painted grammar of life,
that poem which begins with tree, plant and creeper
and speaks onward into human history —

in this earth, a mountain;
on that mountain, a peak —
he who gave us that stainless grammar of tradition,

<!-- scan 26 -->

I would say that kneeling before him,
worshipping and paying homage,
is a mighty duty to be performed
in the very midst of drawing human history!

Six thousand years ago,
man had no clothes,
no power to prepare food of the six tastes,

no sense of love, romance, affection or duty,
no ordered bonds among bodies wrapped in bone and skin,

no habit of thinking about refinement or conduct;
what he saw became the sight,
what he seized became his style of life.

He wandered over forest and hill,
and made friendship too with goat and cattle.

Whenever hunger struck,
he learned to pluck whatever he saw along the path
and eat what he had plucked;

so even if he saw the “magnet stone,”
and even if that stone itself came toward him at first sight,

like owl and horned owl,
each would seek out
whatever prey each could find.

After that — who was he? who was she? ........
who would sing *aararo*
for one yet to be born? ........

So it is in one of Rahul Sankrityayan's stories:
when the beautiful enchantress Diva
and her desirous lover Sur

embrace in delight, drink from each other's lips
and lie there forgetting themselves,
she says:
“My beloved, you are my mother's son!
Even so, forgive me
for not having come close to you until now.”

“Enough — from now on,
do not feel shy with me,” he says;
and afterwards they —

<!-- scan 27 -->

became flood mingling with flood,
veena and its resonance —

then moved apart,
and later each ran off
to play a different veena.

A life slipping along with no bounds,
the custom of the age of gesture
before spoken language —

showing by facial sign
“No,” “You must not,”
and “I cannot desire this” —

that mime-language of performance
was the speech-language of that day.
There is a whole history behind
word-language later rising into sound.

When struck, crying “Ah!” “Oo!” —
calling to one far away, “Come!”;

in fear, trembling and screaming “Ayyo!” —
in overwhelming joy, laughing “Aav!” —

if these took second place and became musical sound,
then *iyal*, *isai* and *koothu*
are surely the basic grammar of every language.

In time, the dominion of the tongue
made even the language of gesture
a minister without portfolio,

and, in keeping with our master's command,
“Whatever else you fail to guard, guard the tongue,”
it seems a rapid-action guard was set
for the kingdom of the tongue!

For the tongue that opened the road
to the heights of civilization to begin speaking,
the advance that arose forty thousand years ago
was, they say, the cause.

Man, who ate raw flesh to appease hunger,
wanting to taste his food,
learned to kindle fire and cook it.

<!-- scan 28 -->

Only by hunting and killing animals
could he find a way to manage the hunger of the belly —
and even in that hunt,

competition, jealousy, war — new problems one after another;
these clashes that sprouted in the days
of our grandmother and great-grandmother

have not stopped even in the days
of granddaughter and great-grandchild.....
The counsel of the *Aathichudi*,
“Let anger cool,”

enters no one's ears;
why that is, I do not know.

Rather than the people of this age,
who have grown accustomed to a life of enmity,

the people of that age,
who moved uncivilized through cave-life,
seem in many ways better —

so that such a thought
need not arise,

the heart of gold that the earth possesses
must be possessed in greater measure
by woman and man;

that heart, though rare to obtain,

will create love —
will cultivate peace!

Another story will branch out;
its seed will sprout tomorrow!

## Item 6 — The Knife Belongs to the Enemy; The Blood Is What We Give!

<!-- scan 29 -->

Whether Stone Age man wished
to learn many new things or not,

Nature herself stepped forward,
like a mother feeding milk to her child,
and taught him many things.

He killed animals for food,
ate their flesh,
and wore the flayed skin as clothing —

was that not truly the result
of a command unfolded by his brain?
And for him to make the tools
with which to live by force,

was it not Nature's own kindness
that told him, “Take iron and stone”?

Without lifting even a splinter,
letting the belly grow,
living as income pours in —
that paradise of landed domination;

if today there is a poor working class
rising with a roar to make it weep,
though there was none then,
the reason is surely that here

there sounds the cry of an intellectual revolution
that breaks the condition of slavery!

To the poor and ruined who asked,
“Why? For what? How?”
and were told by a crowd of scoundrels,
“It is all God's command” —

because Bharathidasan,
king above kings among poets,
beat the war-drum of verse,
calling for the worker to be set against
the binding knot called God —

<!-- scan 30 -->

we now see softness and humility
in the mansions of the very wealthy!

Because kindness and culture
could be found at the beginning of the Stone Age,
men made battlefields and fought
in forests, on hills and uplands!

Even if one says the Golden Age was better,
the support of wicked lords and great men
must be searched for only with a magnifying glass;

and to eyes wearing a Vedic lens,
not even drops of sweat
can ever be seen.

Festivals, spectacles,
all kinds of amusements —
a condition that had found unity in difference
changed;

because difference and division
grew swiftly,
hostility put down roots
and sent out shoots as a poisonous tree.

They hacked at one another,
struck one another with stones,
and by the hundreds, according to the count,
fell as victims on both sides;

all the possessions of both sides
lay wasted, unusable.

In the darkness of folly,
the warriors left standing on both sides
lit the lamp of death,
knelt and touched the earth,
and began to pay silent homage
to the martyrs of heroic death!

As I write this,
a passage from Pandit Nehru's
book of world history,
which I have read many times,

shadows itself across my heart
and stirs many memories.

Here — Nehru says:

“If we look closely at history,
we can understand the evolutionary process
by which this world has grown and changed —
‘as grass, as shrub, as worm, as tree,
as many forms of vegetation,
as bird, as snake,

<!-- scan 31 -->

as stone, as human’.”

He calls Manikkavasagar's *Tiruvachakam* itself
as evidence and witness;

and Pandit Nehru — the Light of Asia —
after praising the power
of human reason,
says in that same book:

“If, as we saw in the World War,
what we call progress means
united brothers destroying one another,
then there is some fundamental error
in that progress.”

What medicine can possibly cure
the anxiety of people like him
who say this?

Carrying a bowl of medicine,
a young woman came there —

I can say firmly:
she was not that beautiful enchantress,
the “magnet stone”!

With her eyes opened wide,
she walked toward the wounded,

placing each step so gently it might ache,
her lifted foot almost blistering —
slowly she walked.....very slowly.

She turned over one or two bodies and looked.
Except for the bodies of warriors who had fled,

all the others lay fallen
as honour-filled warriors
welcoming death.

The young woman walked with the medicine bowl —
an upright walk;
if you call it a majestic stride,
a proud stride —

<!-- scan 32 -->

all that tract of land,
registered in the names of many,
had been swallowed by that sinful landlord;

he had taken a heap of money,
distributed portions,
and belched in satisfaction.

To hide the sin he had committed,
he babbled like a madman:
“I shall perform sacrifices and yogas,”

“I shall win; I have no fear.”

What should have become the possession
of those who had the right to it,
men of fox-like nature
made their own property,

leaving many good people
in the middle of the street.
Cursing him — “May he perish!” —

the whole village boiled up;
the Rising Sun too rose.

At the waterside,
as though lying there waiting for her,
was a stone used for washing clothes.

The peahen climbed onto it and said:
“Comrades, lend me your ears;
listen to the ideas I speak!

We are only a few,
people living in the deep forest!

Those who burn with irritation and envy toward us
are, like us, only a few!

If irritation meets irritation,
only more irritation will flare,
and nothing but our ashes will remain.

To provoke both sides
and make them clash
requires not even a drop of intelligence.

<!-- scan 33 -->

The landlord lies asleep
in his bedchamber!
We labouring common folk alone
are travelling toward the graveyard!

From being Stone Age humans,
through the changes of time,

we are gradually becoming refined.
As proof that we have also gained reason,

let all of us unite;
first,
let us take high and low from among ourselves
and throw them into the rubbish bin!

After that,
to turn everything upside down,
let us raise the flag of revolution!
Rise — conduct the uprising!”

Seeing the young woman speak
as though a sluice-gate had suddenly burst open,

wounded and unwounded alike
came and stood there,
mouths agape:

“Ah! What a wonder!
You speak so beautifully —
how can you do it?”

When everyone asked,
she answered:

“I did not learn to speak
only for myself —
I learned to speak
so I could teach everyone to speak.”

Until then,
a warrior who had lain unmoving
under cover of darkness,
with an honour-wound upon his chest,

woke and asked:
“Do you know me, woman?”

<!-- scan 34 -->

“Why would I not know you?
Are you not my beloved?
Why did you join the enemy's camp?

To look upon traitors is itself betrayal,”
she said, and moved away!

“Woman, forgive me.
I came to understand the enemies,
and then returned here itself;
do you not know?

I am dying now
because the enemy's arrow
has pierced my chest;
look, my dear!” he said.

Crying “Ayyo!”
she embraced his body.

At the edge of death he asked:
“My beautiful statue!
When you say ‘revolution,’ when is it?
When you say ‘uprising,’ how is it?”

“My dear, what I spoke of
was not a revolution that breeds riot;
it was a cultural revolution!

Not an uprising that conducts a fight;
a social-justice uprising!
Everything only through the path of righteousness!” she said.

The dying man smiled and said:
“A war without knife, without blood —
that is it, is it not?”

“Yes — the knife is what the enemy brings;
the blood is what we give —
that is the righteous struggle!” she said.

Still embracing her,
his life went out.

## Item 7 — The Form of an Age in History!

<!-- scan 35 -->

The landlord gathered a hired force and waged war,
finished off the lover of that virtuous “maiden,”

and when the lover breathed his last
in his beloved's lap,
he said that the struggle for rights,
when it entered the field,
should be conducted on the path of virtue,

and that it should continue without slackening
until the troubles were gone;
with those words he closed his eyes.
If one is to tell that story —

even before then,
that region had stood as a dwelling-place of uncivilized life;
without the vigour and hunting power
that animals possessed,

humans fell one after another
as food for their bellies —
and that history itself
stands before us and shakes our confidence.

If a few examples must be told:
thinking it was the hard root of a tree,
one frog-brained man,
in the half-light,

sat down upon a python.
When the python writhed,
unable to bear the man's weight,

that mighty hero shrieked “Vee!” and convulsed;
his heart burst then and there,
and family and village alike
wept themselves out —

such a “Bakasura-sized coward!”
Was there only one such case, or two?
It was an everyday serial!

<!-- scan 36 -->

A man went on foot through the forest,
meaning to offer food to Kaliyatha
and sacrifice a goat-kid;

a rutting elephant blocked his way,
trampled him underfoot,
and did not leave him until they were bound for `Kaavur` —
that report
terrified those who thought of entering the forest;

and then there was the great terror
of hornets that would swarm even upon people
lying inside their houses and sting them to death!

What can one do? ........
At the next stage after the Stone Age,

the struggles were between animal-kind and humankind,
producing savage fields of death!

In the stages after that,
even when animals bore no hostility toward humans,

among humans themselves,
again and again,
the struggle came to be arranged
as though it were animal-kind against humankind;

and it went on in such a state
that one could no longer tell:
who is the animal? who is the human?

Among the disasters seen as a result,
I shall show you one scene
that those who carefully open the casket of time
with the key of poetry must see —

## Item 9 — What Truth Does the Conversation Reveal?

<!-- scan 44 -->

I myself have sometimes wondered
whether the previous day's casket of time
was a little too long;
when telling history,
one must travel by the proper road.

Five and three make eight;
even if we add it as three and five,
only the manner of addition differs —
there is no contradiction
in the total!

As an example,
in Sankrityayan's stories
with historical backgrounds,
I fold my hands and invite you:
come see the portrait called “Purudhanan,”

Show me your kindness;

pour out your opinion too!

From the highly regarded book
*From the Volga to the Ganga*,
I shall show you a conversation drawn there;
that is my duty to you!

A conversation between two Aryans —
I give it exactly as it stands,

<!-- scan 45 -->

without adding or subtracting,
without correcting what he has drawn;
value this work of mine in presenting it!

The conversation between the two Aryans in that book.
(Book — pages 101, 118, 119, 120)

“We have done away with the office of Indra.”

“You have done away with it?”

“Yes; for us Aryans of the southern region,
this office had begun to cause fear.”

“Why fear it?”

“Do we not call the commander of the army ‘Indra’?”

“Yes.”

“Yes; Aryans do not regard the commander-in-chief
as master over all their affairs.
In wartime it is true
that we accept his order exactly and act upon it.
But we Aryans consider above everything else
the social assembly in which each has authority
to speak his opinion openly.”

“Yes, yes.”

“But the Asuras hold the opposite principle.
The Indra, or king, of the Asuras
possesses every authority.
He recognizes no social assembly above himself.
Every word that comes from the Asura king's mouth
is law.
Every Asura must accept it exactly.
For one who disobeys,
the punishment is death.”

<!-- scan 46 -->

“Yes, I too have seen it.
Their priests have turned the Asuras into donkeys.”

“Yes — lower even than donkeys,
the Asuras perform linga worship.
The shape of that linga is like
the male organ joined with the female organ.
I accept that these are instruments
for the pleasure of union between man and woman,
and that through that union
our future generations grow.
But is it not sheer foolishness
to make that shape in earth or stone,
set it up and worship it?”

“Yes; what doubt is there in that?”

“That Asura king is a great devotee
of this linga deity.
But I think this too is a disguise.
That Asura king and his priests are not fools.
They are cleverer than we Aryans.
It will take us a long time

Their market streets,
tanks filled with lotus flowers,
the high flights of steps beside them,

we cannot see such arrangements
anywhere in our Aryan land.”

What you are seeing is the casket of time
from roughly four thousand years ago —
from 2000 B.C.!
Rahul's *From the Volga to the Ganga*

worshipped by the Asuras

as utter foolishness!

<!-- scan 47 -->

If the divine form we saw four thousand years ago

Is the union of the Avudaiyar and the linga

Every day we see deities

Even in worshipping the divine,

Cultures were different —

Has that condition now changed?

## Item 10 — The Ancient Tamils' International Connections!

For the growth of world civilization,

<!-- scan 51 -->

The affectionate Aztec people

There is another piece of news too —

A brave-hearted young man,

he gripped tightly and embraced

<!-- scan 53 -->

with Egypt from before 3000 B.C.,

That our people sailed ships
across the deep seas of Arabia, Egypt,
Greece and Rome,
and even beyond them —

and that this was as far back

## Item 16 — As a Flavour United Within the Segment!

“Octavius wore the jewelled crown

The Roman Senate, swelling with delight,
bestowed on Octavius the title ‘Augustus’,
a shortened name drawn from the heroic phrase
‘the majestic great warrior’.

It was in that period, historians have determined,
that victory flourished in our ancient Tamil land
beneath the flags of the three great kingdoms,

the second Karikal Peruvalathan, was ruling.
Tamil scholars place this Karikalan's period
between 60 B.C. and 10 B.C.

Records are also found saying

and that this was in 20 B.C.

If so, who was the Pandyan king
who sent that envoy
to Augustus, king of Rome?

The poet Karikkannanar of Kaveripoompattinam,
who sang a *Puram* poem about Karikala Chola,
joined a Pandyan king with him in that poem.

<!-- scan 84 -->

‘You are the king of Uraiyur where virtue abides;

May your spear, winning and winning,

Engrave tiger and fish,

In the fifty-eighth poem of *Purananuru*,

Chola Karikalan and Pandyan Peruvaluthi.

From this we conclude
that the Pandyan of Karikalan's period
was Peruvaluthi.
Karikalan's period was from 60 B.C. to 10 B.C.

It was in that same span
that Augustus Caesar exercised authority in Rome —
that is, from 39 B.C. to A.D. 14.

By examining this chronology,
we are led to the conclusion
that the Pandyan who maintained friendship with Augustus Caesar
and sent him an envoy
could only have been Peruvaluthi.

Literature bears witness
that Tamil kings called Greeks and Romans ‘Yavanas’

‘the sword-wielding Yavanas,

<!-- scan 85 -->

shows that Yavanas
brought varieties of wine into Tamil Nadu

Among the foodstuffs exported

pepper was extremely important.
When a man named Alaric once conquered Rome,

Near Korkai, the great port of the Pandyan country,

Of the pearl of Korkai,

‘Korkai, guarded by the warlike Pandyas in righteousness,

Pearls, fine cotton cloth,

The Yavanas, it is said,

Sangam literature proudly says

Tamil literature says
that traders and sailors from every land

The single fact that many Yavanas
also served as bodyguards of Tamil kings

the goodwill that existed between the two sides.

lie south of the Vindhyas,
we think and think
of the old relationship between Rome and the South
and let out a great sigh.

That sigh has still not subsided;
you see it escaping even here,
in this Casket of Time.

Those traces of history from that age,
dug up and discovered,

may appear fit only for contempt
to those who black them out —
but they are not merely things to praise and cherish;

they are things that ought to sit enthroned,
with joy,
in the assembly of the world.

Crawling and crawling,
stumbling and tottering,
struggling at the threshold —

<!-- scan 87 -->

only when the mother lifts
this child called Tamil Nadu's history
and places it in her lap

will the account come right;
then a bridge will be formed
between the many national peoples!

Within the sweet fruit called ‘world history’,
one of its inner flavours is Indian history;

and within that flavour,
united as flavour within flavour,
is this history of Tamil Nadu!

## Item 17 — From Where Is World History to Come?

<!-- scan 88 -->

Crores — crores — crores —
how many crores of years ago,
perhaps on one year, one month,
one day, one *naazhigai*, one second —

we knew nothing of what stood before us,
nor who stood before us;
darkness — darkness — darkness —

we know the colour of that darkness now:
black!
But then we did not know it —
because we had seen no colour at all!

Black, red, green, yellow,
blue, white —
for us to give each colour a separate name,
no such colour had yet come
and stood alone before us.

What we saw then,
we now understand,
was only the colour black.
Had we then named it ‘white’,
then even now
the colour of darkness
would have been white — white indeed!

A feeling —
as though someone had brushed past,
as though someone stood nearby!

<!-- scan 89 -->

Who was it?
Adam and Eve?
Or was it we ourselves?

Before an answer could be found,
something exploded!
What was it that scattered?
The heart trembled!

Such a colossal sound —
as though a thousand enormities
had been stacked one upon another,
and an enormity larger than all of them
had fallen —

surely we might have heard it
before our ears went deaf?
But we neither heard nor saw it.

Only the mark left by that sound,
like a line scratched across the surface,
became known —
as I said at the beginning —
crores — crores — crores
of years later!

The noise rising
from a basket packed with a crore chicks
can now be heard —
that same sound is heard from beside us,
and clearly from above us and below us too!

Those baskets floated in the sky then,
and they float there now!
The basket we inhabit does too!

There — another basket;
why does it circle us?
No — no —
we are the ones circling it;
we keep circling!

<!-- scan 90 -->

Ah! We ourselves
are spinning around ourselves;

are all those things
that appear far away from us
and keep circling
also baskets holding crores of chicks,
like ours?

Are all the baskets circling?
How are we to know?
We lie within darkness,
not even knowing the colour of darkness,
in a blind condition —

we revolve around ourselves;
it is a movement unknown to us!
No one obtained our consent for it!

Things that exploded and split apart like us —
do they too revolve,
under the name ‘universe’?
Do they circle something else,
or someone else?

Are we alone the universe?
Or is everything together the universe?

Whatever it is, let it be!
What is our task now?
What command has been laid upon us?

Task or command —
even if we do not come forward to fulfil it,
it is not left lying idle,
without even beginning to be carried out:

it moves! it moves!
it keeps on moving.

For however many crores of years,
for that many crores of years,
that ‘movement’ has continued —

and in between,
through some tremors or other,
who knows what, and what all,

<!-- scan 91 -->

changes and changes again.
Only afterwards
are we able to understand them.

Even if we knew beforehand,
we have not been given
the power to prevent them —

and when there is no one there
to do the giving,
how can it be given?
Who would give it?

I and you included — that is, we —
were, and remain,
chicks within one basket
that exploded and scattered
crores upon crores of years ago;
like our own basket!

Here — I shall give you an image.
On a seashore where a cool soft breeze blows,
children blow bubbles of air
and send them floating;

like balloons,
they fly until they burst!
One of them is this basket of sky
upon which we have climbed —
or have been made to climb.

Wind tosses that basket about;
sea-water strikes and rocks it;
and facing every fierce natural assault —
sea-catastrophe,
storm, cyclone,
violent gale —

we hang on,
reaching out and catching
the fragments and scraps
left standing.

And in that hanging,
what temporarily holds us up,
and what strikes and shatters us,

<!-- scan 92 -->

is nature itself,
as far as we have come to know it.

In its creation,
then and now,
we took form —
and so did our baskets;
yes,
baskets of a crore chicks,

baskets accompanied
by the chorus cry
‘kuk! kuk! kuk!’

Was the first basket Kumari Kandam?
The land of Africa?
Something else?

What was the age discovered there —
Stone Age? Golden Age?

When man — that is, you and I —
had not yet appeared,
when we ourselves had not appeared,
what meaning could Stone Age or Golden Age have?

How did we appear at all?
The chick became a hen!
The hen laid an egg!
From the egg came the chick!
Which came first — egg or hen?

Only the explanation called evolution
can unfold an answer to this!
Are not the seeds of plants,
in one sense, eggs too?

First, something called an egg takes shape —
and not merely the hen:
even the human being forms within an egg.

An egg does not mean only a hen's egg,
nor only the eggs laid by birds.
In every drop of semen
there are lakhs upon lakhs of ‘eggs’ —
and from among them,
using only those that are required,

<!-- scan 93 -->

any embryo takes form;
according to the place in which it forms,
on the basis of evolutionary development,
it takes shape as bird,
animal,
crawling creature,
or human being!

If we say man came from the monkey,
we must understand it
as stage-by-stage development,
as coming through evolution;

can we then ask,
‘Why have the monkeys we see today
not turned into human beings?’

As I have said once before:
we mix mud to raise a wall.
After the wall has been raised
from the mixed mud,
the mud left over
can only remain mud;

must that leftover mud itself
somehow become a wall?

All right —
if an egg becomes a hen,
how is the egg itself formed?

The answer is:
as mud is formed,
so too is the egg formed.

Just as clay — or wall-earth — and water
mix together to form mud,
so too certain mixtures are needed
for an egg to form.

From egg to egg,
the nutrients required,
the substances able to form an egg,
lend their help —
and from that egg comes the hen.

In the same way,
through evolution,
from the monkey grew the human being!

Did he first appear in Kumari Kandam?

<!-- scan 94 -->

Or in Africa?
This is a question needed
not only for human history;
it is needed for world history too!

‘The human who arose in Lemuria,
the great continent embracing India,
South-East Asia, Africa and Australia,
was the first civilized human,’

so, in 1974,
the Soviet scholar Kondra Rao is said to have written
in *The Riddles of Three Oceans*;

but because ‘Lemuria’ had broken apart
and today's continents had already taken
approximately their present form
one crore years ago,

it has been announced as the conclusion of research
that, about 12,000 years ago,
during the Ice Age when sea level was low,
the southern land that existed
south of Kanyakumari before the sea took it —
Kumari Kandam —
is more suitably regarded
as the birthplace of humankind,
or the birthplace of human language and civilization.

Therefore,
against nature itself —
which shows its mighty power
to alter the birthplaces of rivers,
the paths in which they flow,
the sea,
and the city,

through the hand of earthquake,
the speed of storm,
and sea-catastrophe —
against that nature
human beings have stood and lived
for all these ages.

The growth of human civilization
could not have taken shape evenly,
at the same moment,
in every place in the world.
In connection with this,
Mozhi Nayiru Devaneya Pavanar writes:

‘When skill in crafts
and the ability to speak a refined language
grew among the people of Kumari Nadu,
agricultural abundance,

<!-- scan 95 -->

commercial abundance,
and the arts of city-building
rose correspondingly to excellence’;

and also:

‘At this frontier of time,
when language was spoken in refined form
and civilization was developing,
the ancestors of the Scythians
and the ancestors of the Sumerians
may have separated and departed
from Kumari Nadu.’

Thus Kumari Kandam is shown
not merely as the place
where human beings first appeared,
but also as the cradle
of human civilization.

It was on this basis
that scholars such as Vincent Smith
and Professor Sundaranar said
that Indian history
should be written not from the north,
but from the south — from Tamil Nadu.

Now, turning that argument around and extending it,
we must say that the idea grows firm
that not only Indian history,
but world history itself —
the history of humankind
‘from the appearance of man to this day’ —
should be written from the south.

In accordance with that,
I have been opening and showing
the ‘Casket of Time’
with the ‘Key of Poetry’.

## Item 18 — We Seek What Remains After the Rubbing Away!

<!-- scan 96 -->

Because no expert capable
of classifying, compiling,
and presenting history
came the Tamils' way,

have not stone inscriptions,
great epics and literary works,
and literary evidence that has not perished with time,
become the history of the noble Tamils
from that day down to this?

The rising Pandyan king Nediyon,
who stood before all others;

Chola Karikal Peruvalathan,
who strode as far as the summit of Mount Meru;

and Cheran Senguttuvan,
who went to the Himalaya
to bring stone for Kannagi's statue
and defeated the kings Kanaka and Vijaya —

these are men stamped by history
as our ancestors
from three thousand years ago.

They saw many battlefields —
Ganga, Vanga, Kalinga —
and defeated their enemies;

even if our eyes close,
the earth will not close over their names —
heroes of history!

Their descendants —
how many Cheras,
Cholas and Pandyas!

<!-- scan 97 -->

‘Tamil Nadu,
whose kings possessed the strength
to subdue the mountain Himalaya
whose head strikes the sky;
who fought in Kalinga
and destroyed its darkness!’

and

‘Tamil Nadu,
whose people sailed to Lanka,
Putpakam, Chavakam
and many an island,
and there saw
their tiger-banner and fish-banner
stand in dignity!’

and

‘Tamil Nadu,
whose fame spread through China,
Misiram, the land of the Yavanas,
and many other countries;
where knowledge, arts,
warcraft and commerce
grew splendidly!’

What Bharathi sang
are not merely poems;
they are unshakable seeds of evidence
that Tamil valour,
wisdom,
commerce,
and artistic sensibility
crossed the deep seas
and exercised influence beyond them!

In England, France, Spain,
and even in parts of America,
with Elam, Sumer and the Egyptians,
our Dravidians had civilizational contact —
who has ever denied it and prevailed?

Just as here we gladly praise iron
as the ‘black diamond’,
so, among the people who lived in Mexico,
they praise Maya civilization
as Dravidian civilization,
and say that their own firm blackness
is the colour in which beauty shines.

‘Nikito Sindo’, in their language,
is said to mean
‘black alone is the beautiful colour.’

<!-- scan 98 -->

Writing and civilization,
the Egyptians learned,
were gifts bestowed
by the Tamils of Kumari Kandam.

That Mesopotamian civilization rose to greatness,
that the people called Elamites rose in stature —
learned and impartial researchers of history,
men of good knowledge without deceit,
say the cause was the great fragrant rain
of civilization that Kumari Kandam poured forth
before that lost continent was destroyed.

‘Across the Mediterranean,
Kumari Kandam,
the Indus region,
Egypt,
Sumeria — everywhere it spread;
it was Tamil civilization,’

says the worthy Ramachandra Dikshitar,
offering what he presents as fitting evidence
in his book *The Origin and Spread of the Tamils*.

Even when clear evidence exists
that the civilization and culture
of the Dravidian Tamil people
had spread in every direction,
and that their capacities
had grown and flourished,

someone or other grinds down sandalwood,
smears it over the whole body,
and celebrates a festival;

from the sandalwood block used for that grinding,
after all that has been rubbed away,
we seek at least what remains —
we roam searching for it,
to put it to use for our Tamils!

That is this Casket of Time!

## Item 19 — A Historical Event to Grieve Over!

<!-- scan 99 -->

That Senguttuvan was a son born of the Chola line,
and that his mother
was Narchonai, daughter of Karikala Chola —
this is what *Silappathikaram* tells us.

What does the *Pathitrupathu* decade say?
That the maiden's name
was Manakkilli, daughter of the Chola.

Since it is natural enough
for one person to bear two names,
there is no need to magnify this
and become confused.

Greater confusions than this
raise their heads in Tamil history.
There are three —
first, second and third Karikalas;

and the victories won by those three
collide with one another
until we struggle to know
which victory belonged to whom!

As though there were another Kuttuvan,
there is one called Velkezhu Kuttuvan;
yet scholars say he is no other man —
only another name
for Cheran Senguttuvan himself!

In England, from Henry the First
to Henry the Eighth,
however many Henrys may come,
time and history remain exact.

Here, because literature and inscriptions
are what have come down to us as history,
I would say that only as far as possible
have we been able to research
and fix the dates with certainty!

<!-- scan 100 -->

The peerless warrior who went to the Himalaya and won;
the Chera who, whenever the occasion came,
added glory to the Tamils;

the great king who held honour alone
to be the good life,
the valiant Tamil who stood like a great mountain
to guard his people —

“there is none who can defeat him,” people said;
“whoever comes against him will fall,”
the words rang out;

and as Senguttuvan's fame spread everywhere
like a full, resounding note,
some kings who ruled the earth
boiled over with jealousy!

Like embers that refuse to die,
jealousy and unbearable irritation —
seeing a Tamil's greatness,
these arise in another Tamil himself;

and among those kings,
one who sprang up so
was Palaiyan of Mogur.

To finish his enmity
and send the Chera to the burial pit,
Palaiyan made new plans,
wove wicked plots,
prepared for war,
and had the war-drum beaten.

On the frontier of his country
was a chieftain called Arugan;
thinking the time had come,
he leapt upon Palaiyan
in support of the Chera.

Palaiyan's anger against Arugan
was a thousand times fiercer
than even his anger against the Chera;
he rose first to destroy him.

Escaping that assault,
the chieftain Arugan resolved
to bring Palaiyan down
and throw an unforgettable challenge
at his honour;
he joined the Chera army
and broke Palaiyan's fort!

Senguttuvan, who appeared
like a lion-bull at the battle-front,
shattered Mogur and roared —

<!-- scan 101 -->

“Wherever you may be,
I shall not let you go!”
He surged forward
and captured Palaiyan too.

He cut down completely
the neem that stood
as Mogur's guardian tree;

and, ordering the elephants of Mogur,
now bound under his rule,
to drag away the felled tree,
that incomparable Tamil king
returned to his capital.

As soon as he returned,
Arugan moved to fulfil his own desire —
to settle his score with Palaiyan.

To make a hauling-rope
for tying and dragging the guardian tree,
he summoned the warriors
who obeyed his commands;

he ordered the hair of the women
of Palaiyan's palace to be cut,
twisted into a rope,

and told them to bind the guardian tree
with those ropes and drag it.

Hearing this,
the Chera spat fire at Arugan:
a stain had fallen
upon the victory he had won.

“Using my support, Arugan,
you have settled your own enmity.

To show the anger you bore toward Palaiyan,
you sent forces
and took your revenge;
yet —

to bind and drag
the guardian tree of an enemy land,
against all culture,

you ordered the hair
of the women of Palaiyan's family to be cut.
You may call it an act of revenge —

<!-- scan 102 -->

No! No!
A base act!
A base act, I call it!”
said the Chera.

He was deeply troubled;
not only the guardian tree —

he released Palaiyan too
from captivity,
and only then eased his grief.

Among the Tamils of the Dravidian people
there was valour, there was wisdom,
there was ability whose fame scented all eight directions;

there was the force of a storming tiger in war,
and an honour that would never show its back
to an enemy!

There was fearlessness
that never left them for even an instant,
meeting death in battle
with a spear borne in the chest;

there was a summit of devotion to art
that crossed the surging seas;
there was the strength of principle
that would not let its stand collapse —

but,
but,
alas —
among the Tamils,
that one thing alone,
unity of the people,
was absent.

Is that not why
this event of history
must make us grieve so deeply!

## Item 20 — Even in Falling, He Is Victory's Favoured Son!

<!-- scan 103 -->

Think upon the battle of Venni,
which stands as an example
of both valour and honour —

and the nerves of warriors will tremble
like the strings of a plucked veena;

the heart will heave;
both eyes will become waterfalls.

Between the great warrior
Karikala Chola
and the king called Cheralathan,

at Koyil Venni,
a Tamil town near Thanjavur,
was fought a battle

where countless corpses fell;
the sturdy Cheras and Cholas
stood face to face,

both sides bearing spear and sword,
and fought in that field of blood.

The spear flung by Karikalan
flew and, in the blink of an eye,

pierced the Chera's chest,
bathed in the mire of red blood,

and showed its point through his back,
ending the warrior-king's life.

<!-- scan 104 -->

I will not wholeheartedly say,
“The Chera king died.”
His honour was broken, he felt,
and without asking an exception
to Tamil custom,

he decided to give up his life
by *vadakkiruthal* — sitting facing north;
that is why I spoke as I did.

“I shall take spear-shaft and sword
and hasten to sit facing north,” he said.

It was not a wound gained
by turning his back in battle;
even the wound where the spear-tip
had merely emerged through his back,

he imagined as a disgrace
that had befallen him in war;
without consoling himself
that it was a warrior's honour-wound,
without taking pride in it,

the Chera fell into death
through *vadakkiruthal* —
and, poet Kazhathalaiyar sang,
he clothed himself in fame.

What was that poem?

“The war-drum forgot to sound —
the yaazh forgot to sing —
the milk-pots stand dry,
without milk —
no one touches the honey —

no farmer goes to the field —
the open spaces around the town
have all become desolate —

because our Chera king,
to guard his honour,
has gone to sit facing north
and give up his life!”

## Item 21 — Where Culture Is Maimed, They Would Not Even Wish to Look!

<!-- scan 107 -->

While still a sturdy young bull,
he escaped in anguish
with his ankle scorched
in the fire lit by certain wicked enemies;

he became famed for the Kallanai,
and built embankments for the Kaveri;

with Uraiyur and Poompuhar
as two incomparable capitals —

that Chola king was Karikalan.
His daughter,
the beautiful princess Aathimandhiyar,

was herself a queen among poets,
and a great queen of dance
upon the stage.

Nalankilli, Nedunkilli and Mavalathan —
three brothers she had;

though Aathimandhi laboured hard
so that no cloud of civil strife
should form among them after Karikalan,
it was of no use.

Though they took the Tamil way
as their path,
they acted without unity,
walking the way Tamils so often walked.

To make her own path
a path of love,
Aathimandhi, queen of Tamil art,
fell in love with the broad-shouldered Chera,
Aattanathi,
master of the flourishing art of dance.

Because that truth remained locked
inside the palace,
many princes from near and far
came to see her as a prospective bride;

<!-- scan 108 -->

she did not wish even to look
at any one of them.
Beginning with her confidante,
and when Karikalan —
whose fame surpassed the sweep of the sky —
asked whom else she desired,

she recited, as though from memory,
a poem beginning
“Mallaar kuzhiiya,”
whose sense was:

“In festivals where warriors gather and dance,
and in *thunankai* dances
where men embrace courtesans,

I have not seen him.
I too am a woman skilled in the art of dance;
likewise,

the man who captured my heart
so that these bangles loosen upon my arm
is himself a master of that art.”

Hearing this,
Karikalan sent word of the marriage
of Aathimandhi and Aattanathi
to all the kings
and conducted the wedding.

But one or two villains,
who had waited many days,
were stunned as though a cotton pod had burst,
and joined together to devise a plan
to cut their life of love short halfway.

When someone in the house next door,
or the house opposite,
is eating a sweet jackfruit segment,
how can hearts bitter as *etti* fruit
simply stand and watch?

And so,
those whose nature was like a honey-sting
fixed a day
to cut their love apart with a sword;

<!-- scan 109 -->

they chose the day
and did the work of jackals.

After finishing dance practice,
the two lovers danced and rejoiced
until the festival of the Kaveri's fresh flood ended;

later they read together
the midnight bedchamber epic,
and, kissing that thornless rose,

embraced and fell asleep in exhaustion.
Then the plotters bound Aattanathi with rope,
lifted him away;

though Aathimandhi woke and chased them,
they kicked her aside,
and into the Kaveri,
roaring as it ran with an “O!”

they flung him —
like a ball swinging at the end of a rope.

Those nameless, worthless husks
escaped,
leaving the daughter
of the earth-ruling king in torment.

She of the cool, flawless moon-face
ran along the wave-beaten riverbank,

burning for him,
crying “Aththaan! Aththaan!”
until her heart seemed to burst.

He, caught within the rope
in the river's whirl,
thrashed like a splinter
caught in a cyclone.

When the news reached King Karikalan,
he stood bewildered,
not knowing how to console
his precious Aathimandhi.

But she,
calling and calling for her man,
ran along the riverbank —
ran and ran.

She danced a dance of frenzy,
danced and danced;
like a lily-stalk falling into flame,
she withered and withered —

<!-- scan 110 -->

and in that midnight hour,
an old woman who rowed a boat upon the river

hurried in distress
to the maiden Aathimandhi,
who had collapsed upon the bank,
and turned her face to see.

Lightning struck — “paleer! paleer!”
Thunder whirled and cracked — “suleer!” —
like a lash of the whip.

To Aathimandhi,
who opened her eyes only halfway,
the old woman said:

“Are you not, child,
the one who sang to my granddaughter,
your friend Nilavazhagi,
when so many came to see you as a bride:

‘Even in the festival where wrestlers gather,
even in the *thunankai* where women embrace,
nowhere have I seen the man worthy of me;
I too am a woman of the dancing-floor,
and the lord of high worth
who loosened the bright paired bangles on my arm
is himself a man of the dancing-floor.’?”

Hearing the old woman recite
her own poem,
her own pure Tamil song,
without rejecting a word,
Aathimandhi marvelled;

she sobbed,
and the tears poured from her eyes —
one might say they conquered the Kaveri itself.

Filled with compassion,
the old boatwoman said affectionately
that her own name was “Kaveri”;

a smile blossomed
in her toothless, hollow mouth,
and she told them
that she had safely sheltered Aattanathi
in her hut —

<!-- scan 111 -->

news that filled everyone nearby with joy.

She handed Aattanathi,
son-in-law of world-ruling Karikalan,
back to Aathimandhi,
who ruled through Tamil song.

The two danced in joy,
and sang in reverence
to the old boatwoman.

Aattanathi–Aathimandhi
is the very epic that declares
that art and love
were already a settled culture
among the Tamils of that age!

## Item 22 — “Why, Then, the Question? — That Is My Question!”

<!-- scan 112 -->

I, I, I alone —
I keep moving forward.

When the tongue bends and straightens,
saying I, I, I alone,

it is not with arrogance —
but with humility,
with great humility.

How did this “I” come to be?
Where did I come from?

Above all these questions
there is one fundamental question —

that is the question,
“Why did I come?”
Only because I came
can I ask, “Why did I come?”

Only by asking
does an answer arise.
Then did the question come first?

Or did the answer come first?
Ask “Why?” — the answer is “Because of this!”

Ask “How?” — the answer is “Like this!”
Which appeared first — the egg or the hen?

An explanation for that
has already been given;
what stands before us now —
is it the question, or the answer?

<!-- scan 113 -->

Can an answer arise
without a question?
What is this called?

Is this the question?
The universe — this is the answer!

Only because this answer already exists —
or because it existed —

could we look at something
and ask, “What is this?”

When there was nothing,
no question arose.
Since no question arose,
no answer appeared.
And so,

there was a state
in which neither question nor answer
was needed —
and I imagine that I too
was in that very state,

clinging to it like a shadow,
not one year,
not two,
not a thousand,

not a lakh,
but crore upon crore —
when, five hundred crore years ago,

this world is said
to have come into being;
and when, eighty crore years ago,

life is said to have appeared —
among all that arose,
I imagine myself as the first life,

and through those eighty crore years
I have floated as air with air,
mingled as water with water,

been heat with heat,
and cold with cold;

<!-- scan 114 -->

been darkness with darkness,
and light with light;

playing with sky and earth,
crawling, writhing,
going on my knees,
creeping,

walking,
and moving forward.
Who can say
that a full stop
has been placed upon my progress?

Even of eighty crore years ago,
Manikkavasagar,
melt and melted in song
of life's growth:

“becoming grass,
becoming shrub,
becoming worm...”

If the full stop
had been placed even then,
what conversation could there be
between you and me now?

In my journey,
shall I call it a walking journey?

A moving journey?
A crawling journey?
A flying journey?
Since “journey” in general
fits them all,

I say only this:
my journey has gone on
for many crore years —
five hundred crore years —

and that within it
I appeared as the first life
eighty crore years ago:
how, and to whom,
can I prove that?

Who among you was there then?
And who among us now
will be there hereafter?

So is “many crore years”
not itself a compromise agreement?

It lacks an exact reckoning;
but is not “an uncountable span of years”
itself an acceptable reckoning?

<!-- scan 115 -->

In the Bhagavad Gita,
Parthaman piled up examples
saying, “I am everything”;
no one should misunderstand
this “I” in that manner.

Perhaps Manikkavasagar,
to travel safely backward
through the stages of development,
began first by saying
“becoming a worm”!

Across these many crore years,
how many, how many lists
of stagewise development
have I been able to see
upon my journey!

A bridge-like land link,
said to have existed until 12,000 BCE,
joining North America
and East Asia,
was submerged in the Ice Age;

and because East Asia
had been connected with North America
until then,
marks of Maya civilisation
having spread there —
these too I can see
as one part of my journey!

From worm to human,
from monkey to human,
I have been carrying this journey
from the time I was a worm —
crawling, writhing —
so that I may tell you too
the meaning of stagewise development,
leaning not on one or two supports,
but on many!

The universe,
where countless planets
including our world wander,
keeps no family planning;

who knows how many more planets
it will bear and bring forth
as children!
Only as the journey goes on
without stopping
will we come to know.

<!-- scan 116 -->

In this journey
of crore upon crore of years,
I have seen
worms writhing in the soil,

birds spreading wings in the sky,
animals roaming in the forest,
creatures that crawl
and creatures that swim,

and the human race —
younger in age than all these,
yet regarded, within our own measure,
as the complete one —

and the divisions within that race,
and the cultures
each of those divisions honours;

seeing,
rejoicing,
grieving,
being darkened by sorrow,
shedding tears,

I carry many kinds of feeling
and continue the journey —
together with you!

As I travel forward,
I turn for a moment
and look behind.

The learned call this
an *arima nokku* — a lion's backward glance;
this humble servant's glance
may even be an *ariya nokku* —
a glance hard to know.

There is no selfish aim in it;
there is a view toward Tamil history.

So do not take it
as a narrow view;
take it as the view
of my principle!

The reason for that view
stands spread before us
as the answer —
“Why, then, the question?”
That is my question!

## Item 23 — Kundalakesi, Who Speaks the Strength of Feminism!

<!-- scan 117 -->

Kovalan, great lord among merchants;
his beloved companion,
Madhavi, the truthful mistress of dance;

Kannagi, queen among women,
who came to him
as the sacred lamp of life;

and, born in their line,
the woman of the noble clan of language,
of inexhaustible fame —
Manimekalai,

an unattached ascetic
who gave no room in human society
for anyone's reproach —

with these come the sounding pair,
*Silambu* and *Mekalai*;
with them shines as a twin
*Seevaka Chintamani*;

there is the rare Tamil literary work
*Valayapathi*;
and *Kundalakesi*,
about the woman
who brought patriarchy down.

Jainism, Buddhism,
Saivism and Vaishnavism —
when great conflicts arose among them,

though the poets nurtured by the Sangam,
and learned masters
joined to every art,
needed time
to come to honour equality,

before that time arrived,
alas,
Tamil land had lost
two of the Five Great Epics.
What a wonder!

How did *Valayapathi* bend away from us?
How was the form of *Kundalakesi* broken?

Did the termite called time devour them?
Or did the carelessness of the Tamils
stand as the cause?

For more than sixty years
this humble servant searched,
wandering without tiring —

<!-- scan 118 -->

and afterwards,
a torn leaf came into my hands.
In it was a favourite son
of the palace preceptor,

one who had made murder,
robbery, rape and abduction
into the arts by which
he spent his life.

When he was captured,
his hands and feet shackled,
and dragged through the street
to be kept under guard,

she saw him —
the maiden bearing two hills
upon her breast,

the virtuous woman called Kundalakesi,
whose other name was Pathirai.

Her father,
the great minister,
would call her “Amudham” — nectar —
pouring out his love.

That nectar came to desire poison:
she fell and lost herself
in the beauty of the beast
who passed before her in chains.

She bowed at the feet
of her father the minister,
won his favour,
and had the king's punishment
set aside.

Pathirai,
whose heart was stolen
by the glitter of the serpent,
became the maiden of his bedchamber;

together the two
drained deep
the drink of pleasure's nectar.

One day,
in a lovers' quarrel in the bedchamber,
while they held each other close,

“You are a thief, aren't you?” she said —
not as a harsh word,
but as a word of love.

Black anger rose in him,
though he did not show it;
he embraced her
until her full breasts pressed deep,

and said,
“Beloved, come;
shall we go see
the nearby mountain peak?”

When he called,
she clung to him and went;

the two winged birds
reached the summit —

<!-- scan 119 -->

“Shame! Shame!
You mocked me as a thief;
for that I am going
to push you from this peak —
you will be shattered!”
he said.

Within a few moments
that woman,
whose heart was a garland
of cool flowers,
understood the truth.

Deceit and intrigue,
she resolved,
must be defeated
not with a sword,
but by deceit itself.

“Life of my life!
Rare medicine of my being!
Before you take away my life,
let me have a little time
to worship my husband
and rise,” she asked.

The robber —
the man without virtue —
gave it to her.

With great skill
she acted out the sham drama
of a woman bound by devotion,
full of worship toward her husband.

When half her circling was done,
she planned
to take the sinner's life;

while he waited
for the other half of the circuit,
she pushed him
from the mountain rock's summit.

That great sinner died.

Though the literature of *Kundalakesi*,
which declared the strength of feminism
against arrogant patriarchy,
no longer exists,

this Kundalakesi
remains as its grammar —
its living rule.

She went on
to spread Buddhist doctrine,
winning many debates,

and later
brought her life
to fulfilment.

## Item 24 — This Tamil Land, Two Thousand Years Ago!

<!-- scan 120 -->

If we turn and look back
at the history of roughly
two thousand years ago,

we see, as though ceremonial lamps
had been lit upon the sea,
ships of every country
standing there in rows;

both eyes shine at the sight.
In art, civilisation and culture
we may stand victorious —

and I give you news of Tamil Nadu
that cools the ear with delight:
maritime trade,
overseas relations across the sea,
art and literature,

a rule and splendour
that entered many battlefields
and wore the garland of victory —

as these gather like clusters of fruit,
I too savour them together with you!

Kannagi's chastity,
Madhavi's dignity,
and, through the words
of the great poet Karikkannar,

the attachment, affection,
friendship and relations between states
that grew between Pandya and Chola kings —
we have come to know them all
and rejoice.

In defensive fortification,
in the skill of regulation,
there was no contradiction
in Kaveripoompattinam.

And when it comes to wealth of art —
Madhavi, of fame beyond imagination!
The very list of jewels
with which she had adorned herself
is enough to make us dizzy;

let us see at least a few of them
in *Silappathikaram*.
Here they are:

<!-- scan 121 -->

Imagine the moon herself,
adorning herself with jewels,
standing before a full-length mirror —

and hide yourself away
and look!

Upon the feet,
fittingly,
*pariyagam*, *silambu*, *padagam*,

*ariyagam* and the ornament called *sathangai*;
upon her beautiful thighs,
tightly set,
*kurangu-seri*.

A pearl-string
with thirty-two *virisigai*;
at her waist,
a gleaming *mekalai*
showing the beauty before it.

On the shoulder,
a bracelet joined with pure gems,
and the *kandigai*;

earrings of ruby;
a *sudagam* set with diamond.

Upon the neatly ordered forearm,
many kinds of bangles —

diamond bangles,
conch bangles,
gold bangles,
nine-gem bangles,

coral bangles —
so many kinds upon her arm;
and upon her face,
a smile that outshone
all that gold!

A curved ring
like the split mouth of a vaalai fish;
a ruby signet-ring
glowing in rich red;

upon fingers lovely as *kaanthal* flowers,
the radiant emerald-finger ornament,
surrounded by fitting diamonds.

Firm and delicate chains;
on the ornamented arm,
flower-worked *savadi* and *sarappani*;

upon the pearl-garlanded neck,
a chain falling elaborately behind;
a clustered *Chandrabani*
diamond ear ornament;

between indra-neelam stones,
a beautiful conch appearing here and there —

magic or marvel,
these ornaments continuing
as *thoyyagam* and *pullagam*,
beautifying Madhavi's beauty,

are they not truly
emblems that reveal
the refinement of Tamil civilisation?

Is it not like the feeling
of breathing in
a gentle flower-scented breeze
within our hearts?

<!-- scan 122 -->

Is that all?

What was the arrangement
of the stage
upon which that beauty, Madhavi,
danced?

A place was chosen
that did not differ in the least
from the principles
laid down by masters of sculpture.

Bamboo born and grown
on Pothigai hill
was cut to the proper measure,
leaving a *saan* of space
between each node;

it was trimmed precisely,
reckoning four finger-breadths
from the thumb,

and that itself
was used as the measuring rod
for the stage.

Seven rods in width,
eight rods in length,

one rod in height,
with a good interval
of four rods,

with the “standing lamp”
and the “figure curtain” —
all of it arranged
without diminishing artistic beauty —

thus was the dancing stage made,
the chapter declares;
yes,

*Silappathikaram*,
which captures the mind,
declares it!

Ah!
For the mere act of mounting the stage,
there was the dance-master,
the music-master,

the master of *iyal* Tamil,
and then
the flute-master,
the yaazh-master —

how many flawless masters,
how many accomplished experts
in the fullness of art!

<!-- scan 123 -->

That Tamil Nadu
was a land where art flourished;
that it held to honesty
without wavering from its station;

that it upheld the lofty, singular principle
that conduct must be guarded
even more than life —

Madhavi stood
not merely as one example of these.

She also stood as proof
that the field of art itself
is no enemy of chastity;

that after her man
left her and went away,
she embraced renunciation
and stood alone;

that she raised her daughter Manimekalai,
like herself,
as a queen among women
with purity of mind.

This literary history
is not merely an example of all this;

it tells us that
two thousand years ago,

when so many countries
lay immersed in darkness,
Tamil Nadu stood established
as a lighthouse,

casting light
upon the shores of all those lands,
spreading art, literature,
civilisation and culture!

It is as though
tears were sprinkled
upon the face of the sleeping Tamil
to wake him!

## Item 25 — Kannagi's Emphasis on Culture

<!-- scan 124 -->

Kannagi, they say,
was the very form of grief —
one who knew not even
a mustard-seed's measure of joy in life;

weariness, pain, sorrow,
suffering and anguish —
apart from these,
she knew nothing at all
of the happiness of married life,
right to the end.

Kovalan alone, they say,
swam and rejoiced with Madhavi
in a waterfall of pleasure,
ceaselessly tasting
the sweetness of kisses
in which lips clung together;

and Madhavi alone —
waist swaying,
gait swaying,
her twin fruits swaying —

tasted the delight of desire herself
and gave herself like a tender creeper
so that her lover too
might taste until his longing was quenched;

she alone, we men and women alike imagine,
was the woman who performed
such a great *tapas* of pleasure.

This is the stain produced
by the poison mixed into nectar
by those who turned literature into puranic tale:

Kannagi made into a doll
that stays behind and then comes forward,
her two eyes always swollen
from weeping and lamentation;

rising only after worshipping her husband,
drawing the threshold design,
sweeping the house —

nothing but a bonded drudge
inside Kovalan's home;

was there even a single moment left for Kannagi
to be caressed,
to mingle in love,
to come together?

<!-- scan 125 -->

In those days,
all the “Jains” who watched
the *Sampoorna Kovalan* drama
would become distressed;

among them,
we have seen women in particular
watching with great sighs.

Yet *Silappathikaram* itself
stands before us as the proper witness
that all such stories
are wrong from the root!

Did Kannagi not come together
with her bridegroom Kovalan
and rejoice in the bedchamber?

Did those young doves
never engage at all
in the task of seeking medicine
for the disease of desire?

Only after eight years
of married life, it is said,
did Kovalan leave Kannagi and depart the house —

this is no fabricated tale;
Dr. Su. Ve. Subramanian
has pointed it out
in a work of commentary.

Ilango Adigal,
the great one who gave us the epic,
extols the loving pleasure
of Kannagi and Kovalan

as though squeezing sugarcane for its juice,
sprinkling powdered rock-candy upon it,
and adding the blended flavour
of the three fruits until it melts and overflows —

so does he celebrate it
in *Silappathikaram*!

Like a new beauty
in which *puram* song and *akam* song mingle,
Kannagi, a new marvel of beauty,
and Kovalan, drunk with love,

embrace,
rejoice,
and lie forgetting themselves.
See the *venba*
that shows us that scene:

“Like two smoke-dark serpents
joining and steeping into one,
they joined hands
as lovers, husband and wife;

all pleasures whose names do not fade
they drew close and enjoyed upon the earth,
as though they had seen
that nothing here endures.”

<!-- scan 126 -->

Like enraged snakes
clasping tight and immersing together,
like Kama and Rathi,
Kovalan and Kannagi mingled;

thinking upon impermanence —
as though asking whether this world
would even exist tomorrow —

they tasted that very day
all the pleasures there were to experience
and rejoiced,
says this *venba*.

After this,
can anyone still say
that Kannagi knew no joy
of married life?

“O flawless gold!
O pearl of the right-turning conch!
O fragrance without blemish!
O sugarcane! O honey!
O gem not born within a mountain!
O nectar not born amid the waves!”

Kovalan praised Kannagi
with words such as these;
would he not have swept her into his arms,
seated her upon his lap,
and rejoiced in loving play?

Would he have stood far away,
like a poet upon the earth
gazing at the moon in the sky,
and merely showered words at her?

Kannagi too,
leaning upon Kovalan's shoulder,
would surely have listened and savoured
those rich words
that make the heart melt.

After the two had joined
and steeped together like intertwined serpents,
what other act remains to be described?

Kovalan breaks away
from that union
because another companion
comes into his life.

The beautiful Kannagi lies withering — yes:
flawless gold, upon the earth;
a right-spiralled pearl,
upon the wound of marriage.

Hearing of Kannagi's condition —

<!-- scan 127 -->

her friend Devanthi,
an Aryan woman,
wife of one called Sathan,
comes to console her.

“Do not grieve, Kannagi.
Where the Kaveri joins the sea,
in the grove-land of the *neithal* tract,
there are two ponds,
Somakundam and Suryakundam.

If you immerse yourself in them
and worship Manmatha
at the temple there,
your man will return at once,”
she said.

Though the Aryan woman
was indeed her dear friend,
Kannagi of Poompuhar rejected that path
because Tamil culture did not accept it,

and answered sharply:
“That is no distinction!”

Though such details
may not now circulate widely
enough to enter our ears,

they are unshakable evidence
of the emphasis Kannagi placed upon culture
and of her attachment to it!

## Item 26 — Awake; Here Is the Dawn of a Classical Language!

<!-- scan 128 -->

No one has written
the history of Tamil Nadu in full
and left it behind for us.

As a spectacle found
in no other country of the world,
we have had to read our ancient history

in palm-leaf literature,
in an inscription carved by some king,
in a story told by some passer-by,

in an investigation conducted
by gathering bones,
in the skull of a body
placed long ago inside a burial urn...

Only through lines and traces
discovered from such things
could the history of antiquity be read.

From them we could infer
art, civilisation and life;
we could learn the pattern of the age —

but from the Stone Age
through the ripening course of time
to the present,
has not the domination of darkness remained?

how our ancestors lived

the greatness of this sacred land

## Item 27 — It Will Surely Be Opened, to Show the Way!

<!-- scan 132 -->

As our journey through history
continues without faltering in its step,
let us sit here for a while
and speak of this very matter.

I have been opening and showing
the treasures packed inside the Chest of Time
with the Key of Poetry;

and while we remain immersed
in a joy touched with anxiety,
wondering whether scenes seen in dreams
will return again to the screen of memory,

we also carry an expectation:
not merely that they return to memory,
but that they stand firm
as history happening before us!

That is why,
up to this point,
I have fashioned twenty-six poems;

I have spoken of the time
when human beings appeared,
and of the time before that;

of the ways in which all living beings arose,
and of stage-by-stage development;

and through the thousands upon thousands of years
that followed one after another,
I have set before you
the friendship Tamil Nadu held with many lands,
its civilisational contact,
its bonds of art,

its clasp of culture,
its sea-trade,
its opportunities to meet many battlefields —

the fruit-sweetnesses
and the bitter tablets alike
that the treasury yields.

But do not rush to the conclusion
that I, your humble servant,
can offer all this
because of some extraordinary scholarship
or because I have attained
the eight great *siddhis*!

<!-- scan 133 -->

## Item 28 — The Ancient Civilisation That Spread Across the Whole World!

<!-- scan 136 -->

The ancient civilisation
that spread across the whole world!

As sandalwood gives out fragrance
the more it is rubbed —
as a diamond throws out light
the more its facets are polished —

only by speaking again and again
of the antiquity of the Dravidian people
will coconut saplings rise like teak trees!

Banana saplings will grow like *vaagai* trees!
The moringa bud will become jasmine;
on the thorn, a rose will bloom!

Can a screw-pine frond

can be trampled down and pushed aside!

The Bengali poet Tagore,
who gave the national anthem,

“it was the prosperity and strength

who compares and arranges
the Dravidian languages of North India
with the Dravidian languages of the South,
argues forcefully and establishes
that the Tamils spread northward
from the South.

do we know that Tamil itself went there?

“On the contrary linguistic data indicate that the
Dravidian Languages spread from South to North

<!-- scan 137 -->

G. Zograf
Soviet Indologist in a
Survey of Languages of
India, Pakistan, Ceylon and Nepal.

been identified as one of the branch languages
of the Dravidian language family;
this is no empty imagination!

Among the Kurukkar

the folk songs and ancient stories

who had lived in the far South;
no one has drawn a curtain over that testimony!

Professor S. K. Chatterji says
that the sari and the veshti —

Mother-goddess worship

held an “original” right in many lands

Among the world's ancient civilisations,

one share in three

to the Dravidians.

Historians praise
the Egyptians' distinctiveness

the answer offered

not little by little,

through the relationship
with the Tamils of Kumari Kandam!

That continent too,
even then,
became prey
to the vast mouth of the deep sea —

<!-- scan 139 -->

but afterwards as well,
the Dravidian cultural people

From that mighty tree
of a great people,

many worthy peoples
such as the Tamil people,

those kings ruled,

we have begun
with the three crowned kings —
Chera, Chola and Pandya;

without faltering midway!

Tell me!”

Tamil soil was drenched once again

So long as people refuse

“If even one of you is defeated,

## Item 33 — Is “Seruppaazhi-Erindha” an Honorific Title?

<!-- scan 157 -->

Is “Seruppaazhi-Erindha”
an honorific title?

Scholars marvel with delight
at the distinguished Chola empires
that stood upright
in the history of Tamil Nadu —

at a Chola history
that gained a foremost place
in Tamil history;

that was filled with flavour,
and, to the same degree,
filled with sorrow;

that praised and celebrated
the Tamil language,
and later pushed Tamil aside
and accepted another language;

that guarded art and civilisation;
that offered distinction
in justice and administration
and showed the way;

that accepted with victorious joy
the gifts poured out
by kings of the northern lands;

that crossed the sea
and entered battlefields,
and ate the fruit of victory
on every one of those fields;

that established reigns
lasting many years
and made them flourish in fame.

★ ★ ★

With the special appreciations
of Navalar Somasundara Bharathiyar
and the historian Nilakanta Sastri,

<!-- scan 158 -->

I had the opportunity to read
Dr. Ma. Rasa Manickanar's
history of the Cholas.

He says the age
of the earliest Chola kings
belongs in relation
to the age of Tolkappiyar,
more than a thousand years BCE.

Among the first line,
he places the Cholas
before the third century BCE —
Sibi, Musukundan, Kandan and Sembiyan —

and in the third century BCE,
one called
“Ilancetcenni who was Seruppaazhi-Erindha.”

Accepting the second century BCE

and the first Karikalan,

he calls the first-century Chola
the second Karikalan;

then calls the third-century Chola,
a crowned king,
“Ilancetcenni who was Seruppaazhi-Erindha,”

and joins him with Cholas
such as Nalankilli and Nedunkilli,
whose period he places
from CE 1 to CE 150.

Seeing him set it out this way,
only one doubt arose in me!

You ask what doubt that was?
It was nothing very great;

but because the thought
that came to this humble man
should not trouble someone else next,
I shall explain it.

When all the kings
are called with epithets
that overflow with distinction,

<!-- scan 159 -->

what is this?
Why, for Ilancetcenni Chola alone,
has the title
“Seruppaazhi-Erindha”
been deliberately attached
as though it carried
slightly less worth?

It seemed to me
like a lowering,
a “slide” downward —

as though its meaning
somehow belittled Ilancetcenni.

All this caused confusion in my mind.

Later I looked at *Akananuru*
and cleared up my own confusion;
then I came running
to clear up yours
before it could arise.

There is no need
for even the smallest confusion
about “Seruppaazhi-Erindha.”

Do not think of the word
as `seruppu + aazhi` —
“shoe + ocean.”

Instead,
`seru` means “war,” does it not?

Join that with the dictionary meaning
of `paazhi` as “military camp,”

and with the Tamil explanation
that `erindha` means “conquered.”

Put them together:
`seru + paazhi + erindha` —
Ilancetcenni.

The joy I felt
when I understood this —
was it not joy discovered
in our Tamil,
which is dear as life itself!

The history of sacred India —
particularly because it began
from the middle,

and more particularly
because it failed to begin
from Tamil Nadu —

became like the story
of trying to grow a tree
by pouring water
upon the tip of a branch,
without reaching trunk or root!

Or else it became
like an event in which
hot water was poured
and the root itself was boiled!

<!-- scan 160 -->

It was in Ilancetcenni's time
that the Chola army
had to enter the field
against the Mauryas!

The Mauryas dreamed among themselves
that, with Chandragupta's valour
and Chanakya's cleverness
as favourable ground,

Tamil Nadu too
could be brought down.

They even broke through
the mountains that stood
as obstacles before them
and advanced toward Mogur!

Near the Athur pass
of South Arcot district
lay Mogur,
where the powerful Chola army
had its military camp.

The Chola saw that
if the place established as the `paazhi`
were “struck down” and conquered,
the enemy Mauryas
would bow and flee.

When the enemy host
came with its army,
he sprang upon it
like floodwater plunging
into a hollow,

and attacked on many fronts.

The Mauryas yielded;
they hid with their army!

They withdrew
from the Mogur camp;

they fled
with their heels striking
the backs of their heads!

It was on seeing
that victory won by Ilancetcenni
that Tamil poets,
with rising pride,

fashioned the resounding distinction
“Seruppaazhi-Erindha Cenni”
as a glorious honorific,

crowned that lion-like Tamil
with the title,
and rejoiced,
singing a song of delight!

Without strongly drawing attention

the hearts of the Tamils —

their valour,
their sense of duty,

when will we pluck

at the golden feet
of Mother Tamil?

Is that not what

For our dream to triumph,

And further —

## Item 37 — Can He Be Bought with Love?

<!-- scan 179 -->

Can he
be bought with love?

There was a woman poet named Narkannai,

in the Sangam collections
*Agam*, *Puram* and *Natrinai* —

“Here is the cave where the tiger lived!”

the lord among poets,

<!-- scan 180 -->

★ ★ ★

Before you read and relish those days,

Since this too points

★ ★ ★

## Item 38 — He Who Lives in the Hearts of the Grateful!

<!-- scan 186 -->

The “Chola moon”

After Kulamutrattan
and Kopperuncholan,
Chola history rolled through
an age of darkness
for five hundred years;

Let us now meet Killi —
the one who died at Kulamutram —
who remains in literature
as “living history.”

The treasury of *Purananuru*

## Item 39 — Not One Who Came on His Own; One Brought by the Commander!

<!-- scan 191 -->

If we begin writing
the history of the South
from the most ancient times,

lists of the ancient Tamil kings —
Chera, Chola and Pandya —
long lists and short lists alike,
come and stand before us.

From the three crowned kings
who bore three banners
and nurtured Muttamil,

the people who saw
three Sangams — first, middle and last —

when we say Chera,
we think of the great emperor Senguttuvan;

when we say Chola,
of Tirumavalavan,
Karikal Peruvalattan;

when we say Pandya,
of Mudat Tirumaran,
who ruled from Kapatapuram,
the capital taken by the sea,

Up to the mature age

their crowned empires,
with grip unloosened,

In those times,
they fought fierce wars
to extend the lands they ruled
and settle long-standing enmities;

then they embraced,
cast aside bitterness,

and, though ruling
their separate territories,
maintained friendship.

<!-- scan 192 -->

No one has written
the history of any one of them
from its very alphabetic beginning
to a final point and said,
“This alone is the whole of it.”

Nor is that possible!

Later,
before the Later Chola rule
was born, grew
and became a great power,

in the intervening five hundred years,
there were many groups:

those called Kalavar,
those called Kalabhras,
and the Satavahanas,
said to have ruled up to the Ganga.

They formed governments
like small territorial divisions,
built forts for them
and ruled.

Yet among Pallava kings,
Mahendravarman of undying fame
and his son,
the great warrior Narasimhavarman,

and, the source says,
among the best.

They were lion-like heirs
of the Simhavishnu line.

With Kanchi as their capital,
they ruled in monarchy
for three hundred years.

History says
that Pallavas such as Nandivarman
defeated even Pandya and Chola kings
in some battles.

Mahendra,
who once held that Jainism
was the faith he accepted,

later became a Saiva king;
then he mocked Jainism,

wrote the play *Matta Vilasam*,
and used it
as an instrument of religious propaganda.

Seeing this,
Jains looked back on the past
with deep sorrow,
asking:

“Can a mind change this much
the moment one crosses
to another camp?”

<!-- scan 193 -->

Many researchers have even raised doubt
over whether Kanchi itself
was the Pallava capital.

As evidence,

Petta Kanchi in the Andhra region

The Chinese traveller
“Yuan Suwang,”
it is conjectured,
may also have spoken
of that Kanchi;

we shall know the truth!

In the Chinese traveller's
special account
there is also a drop of honey
for our thought:

“Ashoka planted many pillars
in the Dravidian country;
some of those pillars
stand damaged.”

When I think
of all the times,
and of all the kinds of scholars,
by whom the name “Dravida”
has been uttered,

my heart swells —
joy overflows!

The Chalukya Pulikesi
came to war

bend his knee in battle.

Both great armies
leapt into war
like erupting volcanoes.

had a falsehood carved
into an inscription:

Mahendra had run away
and hidden!

The great king Narasimhan,
who rejected that claim,
was also Mamallan —

and because of that name
Mamallapuram itself arose,
the source declares.

As if to prove it,
he challenged:

“Will you come
for a face-to-face battle?”

<!-- scan 194 -->

The moment that challenge rang out,
Pulikesi ran asking,
“Where is the room

did not let that storm-maker go.

in pursuit,

all the way to Vatapi,
Pulikesi's capital.

There,
he exposed to the world
the wound upon Pulikesi's back;

as war-spoil
a beautiful elephant image

music programmes begin

brought that day

is not one who came on his own
to grant boons;

he was brought to Tamil Nadu
from Vatapi
by our commander Paranjothi,
after victory in war!

heaps of treasure,
ornamented elephants
and herds of horses,

## Item 40 — The Tenderness and Compassion Shown by the Soil of Kanchi

The Islamic prophet Muhammad,

## Item 41 — Let Us Protect It: the Pallava Capital!

When I read
*History of the Pallavas*,

a textbook written

prescribed for students,

Kanchipuram was not

there were no Pallavas,

the Pallava capital

The scholar Mayilai Thiru Seeni Venkatasami,
skilled in researching
and writing history,

reminds us that
this is no story:

“Simhavishnu,
father of Mahendravarman,
ruled the Pallava country
with Kanchi as his capital.”

The Kasakudi and Velurpalayam
copper-plate charters,

stand as evidence
explaining these events.

<!-- scan 201 -->

Pullalur,

The rock sculptures,

Mamandur,

Mandagapattu,

Tirukazhukundram

‘Mahabali’puram near Kanchi?

Can we neglect
the research of Dr R. Rajamanickanar
that says so?

Can there be doubt
that a Pallava king
built them?

Mahendravarman,
after changing religion
and turning to Saivism,

wrote the comic play
*Matta Vilasam*,
had it performed,

<!-- scan 202 -->

and mocked Jainism and Buddhism.

In that religious-propaganda play,

across the glorious towers of Kanchi;

how did one of Mahendra's honorifics,

The emblems of Pallava rule

To uproot that
and plant it in Andhra

the fame of Kanchi.

At a time when
true histories of Tamil Nadu
have been screened off
from the eyes of the world,

of this part of Pallava history

## Item 43 — The Tamil Tradition of the Dravidian Race!

<!-- scan 207 -->

Like the rays of daytime sun,
spreading wide and giving light,

were the ancient Tamil Cholas,
the Pandyas, Pallavas and Cheras —

like the plough-point that brings yield,
like the sword-point that fells the foe,

they won fame upon the earth.
Then, like the axle-pin
coming loose from a chariot,

unity across the land
fell out of joint;

they became like the thinning crescents
of a waning moon,

little chieftaincies made into topknots —
scenes like a palmyra fruit
set upon a sparrow's head —

and the “majesties” (?) fashioned
by petty rulers
whose only possession was a sceptre.

Thus, amid many divisions,
Kalabhras and Kallavars mingled,
with the confederacies
of the Three Crowned Kings and Muttaraiyars;

contradictory circumstances
butted and collided,

until at last all broke apart
into enmity, armies, war and destruction.

The condition became one
in which people might ask:

“Those who were here yesterday
are not here today —
where have those who stood with us gone?”

Though there were spear-armies,
sword-armies, elephant-armies
and cavalry,

some handed over
the work those forces had to do
to the warriors —

<!-- scan 208 -->

and ran away to wipe off sweat,
shake off weariness,
rest and enjoy pleasure:
such “noble” leaders!

Leaving them aside,
great victories came
through those who devised strategy
and acted upon it
on the field of war.

They were not kings merely in name —
they shone as emperors.

Those who think
that courage and victory
are goods sold in a shop,

and that even to obtain them
one needs divine grace,

lack firmness in their ideal;
therefore dignity, duty and discipline
fall away.

Without waiting long,
those who showed great ability
to remove all the causes
of such decline

brought, through the six hundred years
called the medieval period,

an age like the pans of a balance,
rising and falling,
up and down in turn.

The renaissance of Tamil Nadu,
which sprouted under Pallava rule
and flourished under Pandya rule,

took on a new form of history
so that the greatness of the Sangam age
might be won again.

Let us begin that history here.

Across the darkened Chola east,
the whiteness of the conch
will spread across the sky;

our history
will climb that sky
and shine!

<!-- scan 209 -->

The rule of the Three Crowned Kings,
which had once spread like a banyan tree,
began losing its fullness around CE 350.

Kalabhra rule in Chola country
continued until CE 575!

as today's preface.

Pandya, Chola, Pallava, Chera —

did Vijayalaya Chola gain the chance

that at first he followed his predecessors,

When the Pandya Varaguna
made war upon the Pallavas,
Vijayalaya stood
on the side of the Pallava Aparajita.

As reward,
he conquered Thanjavur,
then in the hands of the Muttaraiyars,
received it for himself,
and gave victory to the Pallavas.

The distinction of giving birth
to the Later Chola empire
belongs also to Aditya,
the Chola who fashioned a golden age.

Vijayalaya's heroic son,
Aditya Chola,
showed all his ability
in the battle of Tiruppurambiyam,
where heaven and earth seemed to shake;

<!-- scan 210 -->

all eight directions
praised the victory he won.

History then laments
that he made Aparajita turn his back,

defeated him,
took Tondai Mandalam,
and made him bow.

When self-interest
raises its head in politics,

is it not “statecraft” (?)
to leave no room
for love, friendship
or gratitude?

Here, let us see
what the “Alangadu charter” says!

Aditya had stood beside
Pallava Aparajita
against the Pandyas,

To those who speak separately
of Pallava lineage,
Pandya lineage
and Chola lineage,

there is another explanatory piece of news,

when Aditya Chola's wife
is described as
“a maiden who came from the Pallava lineage,”

in the minds of historians
that every lineage
of the Dravidian race

## Item 44 — The Iron Pillar and the Wings of Flies!

<!-- scan 211 -->

The iron pillar
and the wings of flies!

In the age of Aditya Chola,
the one who ascended the throne
as his son
was Parantaka I.

He had twelve wives —
and their sons were:

Rajaditya, Gandaraditya,
Arikulakesari, Uttamasili
and Arinjaya.

Of these,
Rajaditya died
in the battle of Takkolam;

therefore Gandaraditya,
who came after him,
received the crown.

He married Sembiyan Mahadevi,
daughter of Malavaraiyan,

and had Uttama Chola as his son.
That son also had another name:
Madhurantakan.

When Gandaraditya died,
Madhurantakan was still a child;

so his younger brother Arinjaya
took responsibility for rule.

But he too
met an untimely death.

Arinjaya's son,
Parantaka II,
next came to the throne.

He was granted
the distinction *Rajakesari*.
Though still young,
in war —

<!-- scan 212 -->

he faced Veera Pandya
at a place called Sevur,
won,

and, says the Thiruvalangadu copper plate,
sent the Pandya to the land of death;
that charter sings his praise.

Among the wives of Parantaka II,
two are especially mentioned.

One was Vanavan Mahadevi,
daughter of the Malaiyaman.

She was the one
who mounted the funeral pyre
with her husband.

She was not one
who deceived anyone by saying,
“I thought I would mount it.”

We too say
that such a custom
does not accord with rational thought;

then, now, and always,
we have never supported it.

Before Vanavan Mahadevi
and her life-partner Parantaka II
lost their lives,

Rajaraja —
the Chola prince,
pure gold,
who would become a cause
of the Chola empire's rise —

sat upon the lap
of his mother Vanavan Mahadevi
and played by whirling a sword.

Another wife of Parantaka II
was Cheran Mahadevi;

the town named after her
is “Cheranmadevi”!

The jewel-womb of Vanavan Mahadevi,
mother of Rajaraja,
Aditya Karikalan
and Kundavai Nachiyar,

was delighted to see
the Chola tiger-flag
fly across the world.

Knowing that the strength
of her own children
was the cause,

she became the very grammar
of the Kural:

“Greater than at the moment of birth
is the mother's joy
when she hears her son called a worthy man” —

<!-- scan 213 -->

and that mother
stood amazed at herself!

Vanavan Mahadevi's eldest son
was Aditya Karikalan.

Yet while his father,
Parantaka II,
was still alive,

he became the victim
of a criminal conspiracy —

a consequence of the question:
who had the right to rule the land?

Once Aditya Karikalan was killed,
Rajaraja had the right
to come next to the throne
and be crowned according to order.

So a slander arose against Rajaraja:
that he himself,
eager to rule,
had arranged the conspiracy
and committed the killing.

That accusation vanished at once,
like dew before the sun;

but the country,
the source says,
came to understand what had happened.

In truth,
the deceased son of Parantaka II,
Aditya Karikalan,
had been the one entitled to rule.

But Madhurantakan,
who had received only the title
“Uttama Chola,”

regarded taking life
as though it were a game
and, utterly unlike his own name,

completed a violent conspiracy
one day, it is said.

He became an example
of how, for some,
name and deed do not match —

and then joined
the assembly of the “worthy”!

<!-- scan 214 -->

There are also those
who construct another account:

that the charge upon Uttama Chola
was empty blame,

and that his own close relatives
were responsible
for Aditya Chola's murder.

Whatever caused
Aditya Chola's end,

whoever carried out
the conspiracy may remain a mystery;

but no one who argued against it,
the source says,
denied that conspiracy
had indeed taken his life.

Those who had thought,
“Now Rajaraja will be king,”

those who had immersed themselves
in sweet dreams,
reeled in disappointment.

But Rajaraja calmed everyone.
Though he himself suspected
whether Madhurantakan too
had a role in Aditya Karikalan's death,

he let that very paternal uncle
hold the sceptre.

See, then,
how fitting the name
“Arulmozhi Devan”
was for him!

Thus Aditya's grave itself
became the throne
upon which Madhurantakan
sat wearing the crown.

Yes — conspiracy had won!

The innocent people murmured,
“Alas — fate has won!”

and shed floods of tears.

Yet through all this,
not even a sesame-point of disturbance
stirred Rajaraja's heart.

Will an iron pillar
break merely because
the wings of flies strike it?

Rajaraja took responsibility
with this vow:

“I will remain prince
and watch over this government.”

And every step
his paternal uncle Madhurantaka took —

<!-- scan 215 -->

he watched
without allowing his eyes
to tire even for a moment.

Had he not fully understood
his uncle?

Had that uncle not,
it is said,
planned to make the crown his own
and brought Aditya Chola's life
to an end?

Thus, like the saying
“Murugan seated upon the peacock,”

Rajaraja sat
in the responsibility of prince,
while in his mind
Madhurantaka Chola himself
was that peacock beneath him.

When even great families
see contests
for leadership of a household,

could there be no contest
for leadership of a vast empire?

Rajaraja handled precisely that
through intelligence and tact,

through faith in the maxim
“those who endure shall rule the earth,”

and, above all,
through fear that confusion
inside the palace
would open the way
for outsiders to enter.

Even in youth
he understood the proprieties,
conducted himself accordingly —

and later
he ruled the country!

## Item 45 — Father Rajaraja and the Son Who Captivated Hearts!

<!-- scan 216 -->

Father Rajaraja,
and the son who captivated hearts!

After the terrifying history
written in blood —
“the murder of Aditya Karikalan” —

still sounded
from Sundara Chola,
Parantaka II,

broken by love for his child
dying in the “Golden Palace of Kanchi,”

Madhurantakan ascended
the seat of rule

Panchavan,
Brahmadhirajan,
Parameswaran,
Devadasan,
Soman,

as Aiyars.

<!-- scan 217 -->

Since many scholars who researched it
offer differing pieces of information,

from the shock

to the wonder

Rajaraja,

how he built
a golden-age empire.

Arulmozhi Rajaraja

“He made Arulmozhi Varman prince

he set aside his own son
and made Arulmozhi crown prince?

As though his dream itself
had come true,

Uttama Chola obtained
the office he valued as life itself —

Rajaraja and his kin.

<!-- scan 218 -->

It was historian
Nilakanta Sastri,
the source says,
who began the argument
that Brahmins had treacherously
murdered Aditya Karikalan.

Rejecting that,
Sadasiva Pandarathar
cites evidence
that Madhurantakan was innocent.

The evidence both present
consists of inscriptions
and copper plates.

Let us move those witnesses
a little aside,
stand them outside,

and give as our judgment
the release granted
in a climate of doubt.

Then let us follow Rajaraja —

the golden son,
the lion-like Chola,
who established,

a Tamil empire
not merely over the south

We can then investigate

and know it
with boundless delight.

While Rajaraja

Madhurantakan trained him

administration,
political strategy,

how to embrace those who came near,

Perhaps,

this too may have been

“Let us forget enmity,

<!-- scan 219 -->

With such words turned into action,

“Climb, climb!” —
advancing only toward

Rajaraja took the crown

Before coming to the throne,
Rajaraja had already received
sixteen years of training
in administration
as crown prince.

If we calculate his age
from the year
in which he was crowned,

he began direct rule

After that,

Rajendra Chola —
the son who captivated
the minds of the Tamils —

as far as the Krishna River;

that to the west
the waves of the Arabian Sea,

and to the south
the island of Lanka,

bowed before
the rule of these two!

## Item 46 — The Three-Crowned Chola Who Stood as a Model!

<!-- scan 220 -->

The Three-Crowned Chola
who stood as a model!

The age of Rajaraja's rule —
an age that showed the way
and opened eyes

for the growth of joyous Tamil,
for the training of ethnic welfare,
for the rise of ethnic consciousness,

for cultural maturity,
for the celebration of ancient glory,
for the flowering of an egalitarian society,
for the happiness of the farmers.

Yet I hesitate
to inscribe it in history
as a golden age.

And when I make that hesitation clear,
when I say that the reasons for it
are as transparent as crystal,

courage and integrity
stand beside us as allies.

At the same time,
I do not wish to diminish
the distinction of those pioneering kings

under their sway,
showed their strength upon the seas,

formed a vast realm
from Kumari toward the Himalayan peaks,
established the pride of empire,

and laboured to make it flourish
for three hundred years.

Yet in their victories,
in their proud titles,
without seeing even a fault
smaller than a millet-seed,

like the farmer who forgot
to winnow while the wind was blowing,

when the chance came
they failed to secure
the life of the Tamil people —

<!-- scan 221 -->

hearts that did not think of it,
crippled into inability,
without striving,

the Tamil race itself
brought its progress to an end.

That murmuring sorrow
makes it impossible for me
to praise without reservation

the glory of the empire
and the greatness of the Three-Crowned Chola
who built it.

That is the truth —
and even while writing it,
my hand trembles!

The age of the Chola emperors
was a Sangam age
only in the field of politics;

it was an age
that saw many rare victories.

But in the field of life
it did not reach
the dignity of Sangam times,
the cultural dignity of the people.

Not only historians;
people of self-respect
who cherish the truth of Tamil

Into the faultless art,
literature and culture of the Tamils,

spreading far and wide,

Tamil culture became
fruit bored through by the beetle!

One cruelty alone is enough as example:

the custom of a woman

From the queen of Bootha Pandyan

<!-- scan 222 -->

that contagious disease seized them;

Not the funeral pyre alone:

distinctions of high and low

born together,

as the harvest of victory
for those “born from the forehead,”

caste restrictions

could not mingle equally.

When history shows us
how these came and made us suffer,

The period of the Chola emperors

But politically alone

an age of many rare victories.

In the field of life,

Not historians alone;
all those of self-respect
who honour the truth of the Tamil people

at the change

Ancient or modern?
Rational or not?
What accords with our culture?

Before such things
could be examined and judged —

<!-- scan 223 -->

Rajaraja surpassed
the line of those who had come to the throne.

Many flashes of light
called “reward”
seemed to change form,

becoming a moon of cavalry,
then the high noon sun,

until he stood
as the circle of radiance
in Chola history.

He was not old in years;

he was adamantine.

If he stood holding a sword,
his heroic form looked
as though a crescent of the sky

and fixed upon his face as a moustache.

There was such majesty in that face
that even a kiss could not approach it;

as though the forehead of an elephant
had been taken
and fashioned into a chest full of strength,

as though a mountain itself
had appeared as a man —

such was that handsome male lion.

If amid the clamour of work
he gained even half a *naazhigai*
or a full *naazhigai* of rest,

he would hurry

five and six together.

On the way,

“Arulmozhi” or “Arun,”

and turn toward the voice.

Grandmother Sembiyan Mahadevi
would embrace her grandson;

beside her,
Kundavai Pirattiyar,

and discuss the country's friendships —

love and overflowing concern
between grandmother, sister and king.

“Has Devar still not come?”
he would ask Kundavai.

“I have come!”
Vandiyathevan would laugh
as he entered —

<!-- scan 224 -->

his elder sister's husband,
one of the greatest officers of the state,

winking at Kundavai
as though asking,
“What is it?”

Kundavai and Devan
would move a little aside
and speak in private.

Even while that conversation continued,
a group of women,
full of delight,
would arrive.

And when I say “a group of women,”
it was the group

Ulaga Mahadevi,
Chola Mahadevi,
Nakkan Tillai Azhagi,

## Item 48 — The Undying Art of Sculpture and the Beautiful Art of Painting!

<!-- scan 236 -->

The undying art of sculpture,
and the beautiful art of painting!

After Rajaraja's father,
Parantaka II,
died naturally;

after his elder brother Aditya too
fell through conspiracy;

Madhurantakan — rightly or wrongly,

wore the crown

Only after those three

and receive the crown.

So whatever else we may say,
in CE 985
his age must have been
somewhere between forty and fifty!

Only after he too
had ruled for thirty years

assume royal responsibility;

scholar R. Rajamanickanar,
after research,
writes that Rajendra's age then

Even for him,
all that is known
is that the year he came to rule

He was born
on the Tiruvathirai star-day
in the month of Margazhi —

but in that family
of kings among kings,
there is no other detail!

For the father,
Sadhayam;
for the son,
Tiruvathirai —

only those are discovered
as the days on which
they “descended” into this world!

<!-- scan 237 -->

Only after son Rajendra
ascended the throne
did father Rajaraja
die naturally;

but even that day,
when we speak of history,
was forgotten.

The heart cannot help
but become wounded!

        Fortunately,
our ancestors forgot the dates,
but did not forget the news!

Is merely naming
a month and a star enough?

Every month,
days and stars
come and go.

Never mind —
        our old palaces

that forgot birthdays and death-days,

burying them out of sight!

Very well, let it go.
History at least
has reached us as a torn fragment;

let us take comfort
that even that fragment
did not fall into destitution,
and continue!

In Rajaraja's time,

South Arcot, Tiruchi, Thanjavur and Pudukkottai.

The subdivisions of a valanadu

the list runs on:
“Pappa Nadu,”
“Uratha Nadu” —

<!-- scan 238 -->

A group of ministers
assisted the king.

Government officers belonged

“Peruntaram” and “Sirutaram,”

They did not receive
a separate cash salary;

lands were granted to them,

To honour the honesty,
integrity and rare service

lands were sometimes given
tax-free.

Each region called a “nadu”

of a guard-officer.

An order was issued
to measure and account

classify them as forest,
upland and cultivated field,

There is a note,
the source says,

“Ulagalavitta Tiruvadigal Sattan”!

Lakes that appeared like seas

from the Chola kings!

Assemblies were formed
in every village;

four kinds of local councils
were constituted here and there
through elections.

Because high-caste domination
entered among them,
those local bodies were called:

“Chaturvedi Mangala Sabha,”
“Devadana Councils,”
“Brahmadeya Sabha,”

and “village councils

I am not building a story;
I say that the researcher
shows inscriptional evidence!

<!-- scan 239 -->

The Uthiramerur inscription too

root-level local-government system

Committees were formed as well

If there was even a drop of stain
in conduct, character,
integrity or honesty,

a person's name could not even
be written upon the *kudavolai*;

he would immediately
lose eligibility.

Only the fit
would be chosen;

the unfit
would be cast aside.

There were committees
for the lake,
for cultivated fields,
for accounts,
for the sluice —

and only those without blemish

in the committees
through which people performed civic duties.

The source also presents Rajaraja
as the first to group local bodies
into unions
and conduct orderly administration.

Rajaraja lived
with the principle
that every religion was acceptable.

Though he had a special devotion
to Saivism,
he embraced people of every faith
and took delight
in supporting good people
whatever their religion.

His elder sister Kundavai Nachiyar herself,
at a place called Dadapuram,

for Saivites,
Vaishnavites,
and Jains —
three communities —

<!-- scan 240 -->

personally led the building
of three temples
so that each might find fulfilment.

“I shall follow my father's path
without straying
and win a place
in the minds of the Tamil people” —

with that vow,
Rajendra Chola
bowed at the feet of his forebears
and took up the sword.

He became the living form

“The good a father does his son

“The good a son does his father

The mountain-sized support

the work they did
to make the arts of dance and song

and beautiful painting

Those works are
Thanjavur Rajarajeswaram
and, its equal,
Gangaikonda Cholapuram!

He who had been

courageous enough

to Indrajit and Abhimanyu,

that rising storm

Rajendra Chola Devan

“Chalukya Chola”?

In an age

A culture

## Item 53 — Tamil Escaped the Sea-Deluge; It Found the Last Sangam!

<!-- scan 263 -->

Tamil escaped the sea-deluge;
it found the Last Sangam!

The sacred land of India,
it is said,

once stretched seventy *kaadams*
south of today's Kanyakumari point!

With the Kumari mountain-range
standing guard as a boundary-fort,

and beneath it
the Kumari river
and the Pahruli river,

protecting it firmly
like the double bolt
on an Ottakoothar song;

from the Santha Islands
in the southeast,

westward past Africa,

up to Madagascar Island
along the lower coast —

that vast spread of land,
it is said,

became prey
to the monstrous hunger
of the deep sea.

Wearing the attractive name
“the great disaster of the sea-deluge,”

it overturned from the roots
the Tamil people's land,
welfare,
wealth,
and strength.

The research of historians,
the source says,
untied this knot.

Across the far-spread earth
of the ancient Tamils,

Pandyan Nilandaru Thiruvin
was the one who cut
the Pahruli river,

so that hostility itself
might tremble —

a heart-gladdening piece of news!

Upon the bodies
of men and women who died
and lay buried

in thousands,
lakhs,
crores —

<!-- scan 264 -->

we are born,
we grow,
and seek a new life.

We seek our history
as manure
so that the youth of the future
may flourish.

Sun, moon,
Venus,
Rahu, Ketu,
fierce Saturn;

Aquarius,
Capricorn,
zodiac signs —
we studied them all.

We said the cycle of years
was sixty:
Prabhava, Vibhava,
and so on.

We even said
Narada and Krishna
gave birth to them as children!

All right —
all right!

But deceived,
we forgot our own history!

We failed
to tell it,
to write it;

and so today,

are we not driving crowbars
into stone-hard earth
and digging out

the history
of the good,
the able?

★ ★ ★

Along a long street
beside the Pahruli river,

Pandyan Nilandaru Thiruvin
comes at the speed of a storm
in a chariot drawn
by harnessed horses.

A guard riding toward him
dismounts,
bends,
and bows.

The king asks:

“Has everyone gathered
in the hall?”

“Your Majesty,
all the poets
are waiting
in the Athani Mandapam.”

“Has the queen departed?”

“She sits in the palace,
waiting for the king!”

<!-- scan 265 -->

“Is that so?”

After that,
the chariot did not run —
it flew down the street.

Though her face
was not red with anger,

there was no abundance
of smiles upon the queen's face!

The Pandyan understood.

“It has grown late —
shall we leave?”

“No matter;
at least after this much delay!”

The queen spoke calmly,
but the weariness was heavy.

She climbed the chariot,
beauty swaying with her.

“The work on the Pahruli river
was completed;
that delayed me a little.”

“Oh!
So the next task
is the presentation
of *Tolkappiyam*?”

“Both are the same task!”

“You say
publishing a palm-leaf manuscript
and regulating a running river
are the same?”

“Not one;
but not two
in the way you say!”

“Are the tasks
not different?”

“Understand one thing,
Tamil Queen:

what is to be presented
is not merely
a palm-leaf manuscript —

it is a manuscript
for the world!

The task may look like one thing;
its benefits are different!”

<!-- scan 266 -->

“The Pahruli river
creates the means
for people's prosperity;

*Tolkappiyam*
shows the path
for people's very lives.

What came before,
they called
the first sea-deluge.

Even so,
Kumari and Pahruli
still stand as protection.

If another sea-deluge comes,
can the great destruction
of the Pahruli and Kumari rivers
be stopped?

My heart suffers
with that fear!”

The queen answered
with pride:

“However many sea-deluges come,

none will be able
to approach and touch
*Tolkappiyam*,
which is being presented today.

Rivers,
seas,
even countries
may change direction;

but Tamil works
like *Tolkappiyam*
possess a strength
that does not perish.

No one,
nothing,
can destroy them;

forever —
*chiranjeevi*!”

Mudathirumaran,
Pandyan Nilandaru Thiruvin,

filled with boundless joy,
wanted to embrace her tightly
and put his gratitude into action.

But because they were
in the middle street of the city,

he embraced her
only with his eyes.

★ ★ ★

The richly harnessed horses,
as though understanding
the urgency of the royal family,

reached the entrance
of the Athani Mandapam
where the presentation
was to take place.

<!-- scan 267 -->

“One more sea-deluge
may come,”

that sense of fear
was present too
among the poets of the assembly;

one could feel it
from their conversation.

Athankottasan introduced
the assembly-poets
to the royal family.

Everyone called him simply
“Teacher,”
with affection overflowing!

Tolkappiyar,
Agathiyar,
Irungaiyur Karungozhi Mosi,
Vellur Kappiyan,
Sirupandirangan,
Thiraiyanmaran,
Thuvaraik Koman,
Keerandhai —

all these sat there
as poets of the Middle Sangam.

They presented
the incomparable epic
*Tolkappiyam*

in the presence
of Pandyan Nilandaru Thiruvin,

received the blessing
of Athankottasan,

and dispersed happily.

Without knowing
that within only a few months
another sea-deluge
was to come!

Though this day
shone as a golden day,

a wicked day
would soon enter,

and that day
would bring another destruction.

If the Middle Sangam poets
had known it,

would they have sung
and rejoiced
through the musical works
*Kali*, *Kurugu*, *Vendalai*,
*Viyazha Maalai Agaval*,
and many more?

They were people
accustomed to struggling
even with nature;

hearts of steel —

not merely this:
they would face anything!

<!-- scan 268 -->

Those Tamil masters
were warriors too,
experts of courage!

Their joy did not last.

As though issuing
a challenge,

six months later
nature's frenzy began
at Kavatapuram!

Nature,
possessed like a “demon,”

with a gigantic iron hand
struck dark blows
upon the waves
of the great sea.

The whole region
became a forest of darkness —

roaring like thunder,
descending like lightning,

shaking like an earthquake,
entering the sea itself
as a quake beneath the waters;

Kumari river
and Pahruli river
became one,

lost their tracks,
shifted their place,

and vanished
as though they had never existed.

Who can calculate
the result
of nature's fury?

Only after completing
all its work of destruction,

and after the sea-deluge's
“demonic dance” subsided,

did peace show its “head” —

and yes,
in that vast open stretch,

it showed the ruin
that had taken place.

There was no movement
of people.

Where had everyone gone?

Bewilderment — bewilderment!

Wherever one looked,
the laughter of death!

The Athani Mandapam,
where *Tolkappiyam*
had been presented
six or seven months earlier,

alone remained there;

all the other mansions,
upper buildings,
even the towers,

had been buried
inside the earth!

<!-- scan 269 -->

According to Adiyarkku Nallar's
commentary on *Silappathikaram*:

“Between Pahruli,
the northern boundary
of the southern face,

and the Kumari river,
across a distance
of seven hundred *kaavadams*,

because of the abundance
of their waters,

there flourished
seven coconut countries,
seven Madurai countries,
seven front-desert countries,
seven back-desert countries,
seven Kurumpanai countries” —

forty-nine small countries
in all.

The savage sea-deluge,
the source says,
completed them all
and wrote the concluding line!

Mudathirumaran
searched until exhausted
for where his queen,
Tamil Queen,
had gone.

He marked her down
as one among the people
who had disappeared
in the sea-deluge
and shed tears.

Yet he sprang up,
blazing,
with the thought:

“My Tamil Queen
may not have escaped;
my Tamil has escaped!”

He vowed:

“Let us preserve
the Tamil that escaped.”

Joining at once
with the surviving poets
of the Second Tamil Sangam,
the Middle Sangam,

he set himself
to establish
the Third Tamil Sangam —
the Last Sangam!

Thus the distinction
of having lived
at the end of the Middle Sangam,
under nature's devastation,

and at the beginning
of the Last Sangam,

belonged to Mudathirumaran,
Pandyan Nilandaru Thiruvin.

We can understand it;
we can rejoice in it too.

become known and spread;

I thought it over too.

Because my name
was common to people
of every religion,

and was not the name
of any particular deity,

I decided
not to interrupt matters now
with a change.

So “Karunanidhi”
remained unchanged;

and I too remained
unchanged in the Kazhagam.

In thought as well,
strong and clear,

having acquired
in the Erode and Kanchi camps
the temper to struggle,

I became
a “permanent fighter.”

Even when those
who grew the party with us
later stood apart,

I have wished them
life with dignity and distinction.

So too was the friendship
of Nedunchezhiyan,
who captivated my thought.

When I heard
that he had died,

I left the capital Delhi
that very night,

and with a heart broken apart
hurried toward his home.

A crowd without principle
surrounded the doorway,

mocked me,
raised shouts:

“Have you come now?”

Little men
with hateful hearts
like Kutilan,

barked like curs
and tried to leap forward
and bite.

<!-- scan 273 -->

Without knowing
the depth of our friendship,

they shouted thanklessly
and babbled.

Even there,
I showed patience,
paid my final respects,
and returned.

Though Navalar
had gone over
to the enemy camp,

my heart held
an incomparable affection

for the individual friend
Nedunchezhiyan,

and for that Tamil name.

So whenever the names
Nedunchezhiyan,
Karikalan,
Senguttuvan

appear in the pages
of Tamil Nadu's history,

I want their honoured names
to find a place
in my classical-Tamil verse.

To fulfil the request
of the younger brothers —

instead of a veena
plucked with the cry
“Let them return again” —

here are the extended lines
of verse,

in two parts
inside the Chest of Time!

★ ★ ★

Chera,
Chola,
Ezhini,
Thithiyan,
Irungo Venman — five;

with Porunan
and Erumaiyuran joining them,

seven armies
stood on the battlefront.

Young-bull Nedunchezhiyan
set out to meet them,

saw the battlefield,
won it,

seized the enemy drums,
fulfilled his vow,

and became
king of kings —

so sings the rare poet Nakkirar,
with delight,
in *Akananuru*!

“As though sun and moon together
had fallen upon the earth,

the great Chera and Chola kings
fell upon the battlefield” —

through a *Puram* poem,
poet Kalladanar
praises the learned young Pandyan!

<!-- scan 274 -->

“For one man to fight another,
and for one of the two to fall,
is natural!

But that one man
fought without fault
to bend and subdue seven,

that all seven lost,
and he alone won —

I call this
a wonder in this world!”

So poet Idaikkunrur Kizhar
shaped in writing
the victory of the young Pandyan.

Unable to bear the grief
of Tamil kings
colliding among themselves,

poet Kudapulaviyanar called it:

“Talaiyalanganam,
where Tamil leadership
was thrown into confusion.”

The broad-shouldered Pandyan
won in the end;

so the poet immediately
longed to embrace him.

His heroic chest,
spread wide
like a hunter's tiger-trap —

“I wish to rush forward
and embrace it,”

cried that poet
in expansive Tamil,

and embraced
the pearl-garlanded chest!

See how that Tamil poet
describes
the battlefield of Talaiyalanganam!

Arrows flew
against fierce foes;

suddenly
the enemy elephants fell.

When warriors
cut with swords
the trunks of fallen elephants
along with their mouths,

those severed pieces
lay scattered across the field,

looking,
it is said,
like ploughs
along cultivated land!

Many commanders
died and fell.

Beside them,
their sons too
lay dead.

Old women,
with wrinkled brows,

stood around,
wept and lamented;

<!-- scan 275 -->

and yet took pride
that they had borne
such warrior-sons:

“What victory
could be greater than this?”

Mangudi Maruthanar,
great poet
of Nedunchezhiyan Pandyan's court,

marvelled at the skill
of the guardian-king
who came crowned
with battlefield victory.

He rejoiced —

but when he saw
the king surrounded
by an unending desire
for battle,

he grew weary of it!

To reduce
the great Pandyan warrior's
thirst for war,

Mangudi Maruthanar
composed the work
called *Maduraikanchi*.

In a *Puram* poem
that Maruthanar sang:

“O Pandyan,
whose elephant-army
cuts through the field
like a wooden ship
cutting through the sea!

Behind it,
your spear-bearing warriors
cut off the heads
of crowned kings
and make them the hearth;

in a furnace
where blood-water boils,

they cook the flesh
of those killed in war,

stirring the food
with shoulders as paddles,

and conduct
a battlefield sacrifice!”

After praising him
in many such ways,

Maruthanar praises
his enemies as well:

“Though they received
the name ‘foes’
and were defeated by you,

because they fell
bearing honour-wounds,

they live forever
in the world of warriors!”

Thus he raised up
even those who opposed the king
and scattered pearls of verse.

“Let the king now
leave war behind
and live while sword-warriors
praise him!” he said.

<!-- scan 276 -->

“Stand as a generous giver
and give life
to those who have nothing!”
he said.

“Drink the clear liquor
given by bangle-wearing women;

let all life hold
pleasure and peace;

shine so that the world
may praise you!” he said.

“Know this:
those who guard good conduct
are the ones who live exalted;

those without it
are not to be counted
among those who live
in the world!” he said.

As the gathering
of Tamil poets praised him,

as every Tamil letter
honoured him,

the man of Talaiyalanganam lived!

Receiving *Maduraikanchi*,
which Maruthanar composed
and gave him,

the great king lived!

And when Nakkirar
sang and gave him
*Nedunalvadai*,

the young Nedunchezhiyan
lived long
and planted enduring fame
upon Tamil soil!

## Item 55 — Nedunchezhiyan and Nedunalvadai!

<!-- scan 277 -->

Nedunchezhiyan
and *Nedunalvadai*!

“The Pandyas saw three Tamil Sangams
and rendered good service to Tamil” —

when, eager to read many works
about the history of those praised Pandyas,

I was troubled by the question,
“What was the capital of the Pandya country?”

I would say it was scholar
“Ayyamperumal” Konar
who cleared that doubt.

In the book he wrote,
he says that in the age of the First Sangam,

many *kaadams* south
of today's Madurai,
ancient Thenmadurai
stood as the capital
of the Pandya country.

After Thenmadurai
was destroyed by the first sea-deluge,

the capital that blossomed
was Kavatapuram —

and there, he says,
stood the Middle Sangam.

When that too
was destroyed by a sea-deluge,

the king Mudathirumaran,
who had not drowned and died in it,

founded the capital
that is today's great city of Madurai!

Now let us see
the spectacle and splendour
of the Pandyan kings
who ascended the throne
and ruled from that capital!

★ ★ ★

<!-- scan 278 -->

The race that,
from that ancient day,
beautifully divided life
into *agam* and *puram*

and has lived by that order —

our precious Tamil race alone,
our thousand-year crop —

is witnessed by the literature
created by the wise!

★ ★ ★

There are many examples,

but Nakkirar strung one
like a garland:

among the *Pattuppattu*,
a song shining like a pearl —

the Tamil nectar
called *Nedunalvadai*!

It speaks of the courage
of Pandyan Nedunchezhiyan;

it speaks too of the heart
of the slender-bangled Kopperundevi,

burning like fire
as she longs through his long absence;

and it becomes a work
that tells how the life called
*agam* and *puram*

stood in Tamil Nadu of that day
as a counter to the doctrine
of this world and the other world!

★ ★ ★

At the battlefield of Talaiyalanganam,
seven kings miscalculated,

thinking a lone young man
had become trapped;

and they lost,
their broad shoulders ruined!

After that,
Nedunchezhiyan,

wishing to live
like inseparable *anril* birds,

<!-- scan 279 -->

accepted as his queen
a beautiful jewel-like dove....

Though the honour-wounds
struck in his youth
by spear and sword
from seven armies

had healed
and turned to scars,

the lovely queen
thought they shone
upon the Pandyan's chest
like golden medals —

and without even thinking,
she kissed them,
every day!

After joining hearts
upon the bridal bed,

time did not grant him
space to hold,
all through the night,

his heart-stealing Anjugam
without letting her leave
the corner of his eye....

The summons
of battlefields
was the great trouble!

Even after
the battle of Talaiyalanganam,

under his leadership
he conquered
Mizhalai Kottam
of the king “Vel Evvi”!

He seized
the crown belonging
to the sword-warrior Muthuvelir,

and captured
their Muthu Kootram as well!

He destroyed the army
of the great warrior-king
“Cheraman Yanaikatchei Irumporai”

and shut him away
in prison!

Thus —

<!-- scan 280 -->

because he heaped
mountain upon mountain
of victory-garlands,

and set out roaring
with army after army
like wave after wave,

Pandimadevi trembled,

like a bird
with clusters upon clusters
of ripe fruit before it,
yet unable to eat,

losing its balance in anguish....

Unable to move
day and night forward,
she shed tears!

The *vaadai* is a cold wind,
is it not?

Even without wrapping cloth,
a woman may wrap herself
in a man's body,

and a man
in a woman's body,

and so defeat
that cold wind!

But what was the condition here?

The Pandyan king,
surging with force,
had gone to the battlefront!

In the heat
that grows courage,

the sky-threatening cold wind
could not prevail
against the king!

But the queen's condition —
alas! pitiable!

Poet Nakkirar sings:

see the suffering
of household fowl
unable to bear
the cold of the *vaadai*!

The hen and cock,
which once wandered
the wide outdoors,

searched and ate food
with delight,
and passed their days in pleasure,

now, unable to tell
night from day,

confined inside
wooden cages,

shift their aching feet
one after another,
it is said!

In such a killing cold,
with love leaping in her heart,

<!-- scan 281 -->

the spotted peahen
rolls upon her bed!

Taking up Tamil
that none can defeat,

Nakkirar fashions
*Nedunalvadai*!

A tall tower
like a mountain-peak —

its flags displaying colours
like rainbows!

Upon stout pillars
of blue-gem hue,

has silver itself
been spread as white plaster?

And the rampart wall —

as though cast from copper,
built with indescribable strength!

Thus Nakkirar,
through classical Tamil,
paints the palace
of Sezhiyan.

And see how,
through song,
he shows the bed
upon which lies

that hibiscus-lipped Pandyan queen,
her whole mind fainting
from thoughts of him!

A fierce war-elephant,
with legs like drums,

after reaching
a full forty years of age,

dies while engaged
in the work of war.

Its two sharp tusks
are taken,
shaped with beauty
and made into a bed.

At its four sides,
beautiful rounded finials,

like the breasts
of pregnant women
swollen with milk!

<!-- scan 282 -->

Hanging strings
of pearls and gems!

Paintings
of mullai and jasmine flowers!

A cover of rare artistry
showing a lion-hunt!

A five-fold bed
with support-cushion,
pillow and reclining cushion,

all fashioned
from swan feathers!

But there is no *avan* —
no “he” —
for her to say *anai*,
“embrace”!

And so the flood
of the queen's grief
breaks through its *anai*,
its dam,
and swells!

Nurses stroked her feet,
saying,
“Mother, sleep!”

“Get away, girls —
have you no other work?”

cried the queen,
furious!

In the painting
of the sky nearby,

she saw the moon
and Rohini
standing close,
never apart.

“Why have I
not received such fortune?

Why do I suffer
uselessly like this?”

A drop of water
peeped out
upon her eyelash;

she flicked it away
with her finger —

but could not
erase and rewrite
her grief;

she suffered on!

Thus,
in the palace at Madurai,

the queen languishes
like a flowering vine
holding nectar,
waiting for its bee.

What, then,
is the condition
of Pandyan Nedunchezhiyan?

<!-- scan 283 -->

Come —
let us see it
in *Nedunalvadai*!

Love did not become
an obstacle
to the Pandyan's sense of duty!

Because of thoughts
of Kopperundevi,

he did not stray
from the goal
he had taken up!

The *vaadai* wind
came to his war-camp too!

Yet he did not forget
the sword-bearing face of war
and run in search
of a bangle-bearing arm!

Here —

at midnight
the Pandyan set out.

For what?

To see the chaste wife
writhing upon the five-fold bed?

No!
No!

In the cold season
that makes even hills tremble,

with the chill wind
blowing from the north,

even though torch-flames
leaned toward the south,

the king,
inside a northern war-camp,

did not turn south
for the sake
of his yearning Pandimadevi!

To perform
the duty of the day,

with sword-bearing warriors
carrying lights
around him,

his upper cloth slipping
from his left shoulder,

he lifted and held it
with his left hand;

and upon the shoulder
of a strong sword-warrior,

he placed his right hand
in comradeship.

At midnight,
with raindrops falling,

he went through
all the places
where many great warriors
lay wounded —

<!-- scan 284 -->

asked after them
with affection,

thanked them,
and embraced them!

All the suffering
they had undergone
through the daytime battle —

dust upon their feet,
hands and bodies
as they drove back
the enemy army —

was forgotten,
it is said,

because the caring gaze
of their leader
fell upon them!

The great queen,
distraught,
searches for her lover;

he,
his legs aching,
walks around the war-camp
fulfilling duty!

If evidence is needed
that the race
which has lived from that day onward

beautifully dividing life
into *agam* and *puram*

is our precious Tamil race —

ah!
is not this one *Nedunalvadai*
enough?

★ ★ ★

(Based upon the literary work *Nedunalvadai*, composed by the Sangam poet Nakkirar in praise of Nedunchezhiyan, victor of the battle of Talaiyalanganam, this has been written in a simple style.)

## Item 56 — When Attachment Goes Beyond Its Bounds, It Burns as Frenzy!

<!-- scan 285 -->

When attachment
goes beyond its bounds,
it burns as frenzy!

The Pandyan kings
are said to have conducted
the First Tamil Sangam,
which the sea swallowed,
at Madurai.

From Kaichina Vazhuthi
to Kadungon,

research commentators say
there were eighty-nine of them!

Because the First Sangam
and Thenmadurai,
where the Sangam had stood,
were submerged,

the Second Sangam —
the Middle Sangam —

was begun and conducted
at Kavatapuram
by fifty-nine,

from Vendher Chezhiyan
to Mudathirumaran.

Then, as a continuing shock,
the sea-deluge
swallowed Kavatapuram too.

As though born again
from that vast destruction,

Pandyan Mudathirumaran
survived!

Because the fierce blow
of the sea-deluge
had crippled the king
and left him without the use
of his leg,

Sangam scholars,
the learned,

and history itself,
with affection and much courtesy,

began calling him
Mudathirumaran!

Was not that title
the gift of land and sea
to the land-giving Pandyan?

Among the Sangam-age Pandyas,
after Mudathirumaran
came Mudukudumi Peruvazhuthi,

who conducted
many sacrificial rites.

<!-- scan 286 -->

He received the epithet
“Palyagasalai” —

and stands, the source argues,
as evidence
that Aryan civilisation
had already invaded
many centuries earlier!

With his long kudumi tied,
in sacrificial halls
he performed *yagas*
and the Rajasuya sacrifice.

History says
that three poets —
Karikizhar,
Nettimaiyar,
and Nedumpalliyathanar —

sang blessings
upon that king.

Karikizhar,
the source says,
was from the place Kari
in Tondai Nadu,

now called “Ramagiri”!

The poet called Nettimaiyar
received that name
because of his long eyelids;

Nedumpalliyathanar too
is said to be
a descriptive name,

because he had learned
many arts
and was skilled
in sounding music.

These poets,
addressing Mudukudumi Peruvazhuthi,
blessed him:

“May your fame rise
beyond the snow-covered Himalaya
in the north;

beyond the Kumari river
in the south;

beyond the sea dug by Sagara
in the east;

and westward,
beyond the ancient ocean!

May your rule grow!”

They also praise him,
the source says,
as one possessing
a naval force.

Seeing the blessings
uttered by the court poets,

and the words embedded in them —

“your crown belongs
to the four Vedas” —

Ayyamperumal Konar
states firmly:

“It is understood
that Vedic religion
had spread
in the age of that king!”

<!-- scan 287 -->

After Mudukudumi Peruvazhuthi
finished a war
and gained victory,

how did he,
in frenzy,
devastate the enemy country?

Let us hear
Nettimaiyar sing it.

“O Kudumi,
who rides an elephant
beneath the shade
of the royal umbrella!

After taking an army
and seizing another's land,

is it just
to tear that land
from them

and use it
so that the bards
of your country
may wear golden lotuses,

and so that chariots
with elephants harnessed to them
may be driven there?”

Thus he continues
the song of questioning!

“In streets
rutted by chariots,

you yoked donkeys
to ploughs
and ploughed them!

You left
the enemies' beautiful
jewel-like mansions
ruined beyond use!

In their cultivated fields,
where birds once cried,

you made the hoofbeats
of horses sound!

And their elephants,
with broad feet
and terrifying tusks,

you drove
from battlefield
to pond!”

On one side,
historical notes may draw
both the poets who sang
and the kings who were sung

as people devoted
to the Saiva faith;

but if we think
of the various cruelties
they used
in taking revenge
upon enemies —

<!-- scan 288 -->

then, crossing
attachment to land
and attachment to religion,

they became people
of land-frenzy
and religious frenzy,

people without humaneness,
who did not desire
religious harmony.

That truth,
one can understand,
shines like a lamp
set upon a mountain!

★ ★ ★

We also gain a chance,
through the introductory verse
of Adiyarkku Nallar's commentary
on *Silappathikaram*,

to know that another Pandyan,
named Madivanan,

held deep attachment
to dramatic Tamil!

Madivanan
was one of the Pandyan kings;

he stood in the line
of those who founded
the Last Sangam.

He climbed
the poetry-stage
so the Tamil world
might praise him —

and through nectar-Tamil
*venpas* and sutra-verses,

Madivanan's dramatic Tamil
shone and gained fame,
the source says!

Pallava Mahendran
wrote a drama
in the northern language;

but long before that,
Pandyan Madivanan
wrote an exquisite Tamil work
called *Nadaga Tamil*

and placed a signet-ring
upon Mother Tamil.

Until the time
of Adiyarkku Nallar,

that signet-ring
had not slipped
from Mother Tamil's finger;

nor had anyone
removed it.

Yes —
literary history says
that until that age,
that dramatic Tamil
was still in use!

## Item 57 — What Prize Is Fitting for the Beauty of a Simile!

<!-- scan 289 -->

What prize is fitting
for the beauty of a simile!

When a king sets out
with armies for war,

besieging the enemy's
capital fortress
is called *uzhinjai*.

Holding fast to one's own fort
and resisting the siege
is called *nochchi*.

When two armies
face one another
and wage battle,
that act is called *thumbai*.

Whoever wins,
the victory is called *vaagai*!

These, we may say,
are the names
the poets gave

to each stage
of the warfare
of the Three Crowned Kings.

There was a king
named Pandyan Ukkira Peruvazhuthi.

Let us see,
in this part
of Pandyan history,

the event in which
he went with a sky-rising army

to wage siege-war
against the fortress
of “Kaanapper Koyil,”

and how that affair
was brought to its full stop.

Was it the enemy's wonder?
Or the poet's wonder?

Read —
and we shall know!

★ ★ ★

Chinna Marudu and Periya Marudu
ruled the Sivaganga country —

two lion-hearted great warriors
who bloomed as red-blood flowers
in the history of red soil.

At Kalaiyar Koyil,
where statues stand for both,

in ancient Tamil Nadu
there was another name:

<!-- scan 290 -->

“Kaanapper.”

Even now,
it is a land of *Maravars*
that launches wars of honour!

There,
a minor Vel chieftain
named Vengai Marban

built a fortress
of towering fame

and issued a challenge:
who could conquer it?

One might pierce an atom
and enter inside it —

but to enter
that fortress
was impossible,

and warrior after warrior
stood bewildered!

At that time,
the king ruling Pandya country
was Ukkira Peruvazhuthi!

A lion
upon the battlefield!

Strong enough
to strike elephants
and toss them about!

For enemies
to escape his sword
and remain alive
was rare — rare indeed!

With overflowing strength
and a learned artistic heart,

he gained fame,
the source says,
by compiling
the poems of *Akananuru*.

The presentation-festival
of the good verses
of the undying lamp,
*Tirukkural*,

is also said
to have taken place
in his presence!

One day,
Avvai saw him seated
with Cheraman Marivenko,

strung and placed upon them
a garland of refined Tamil verse,

and blessed there
the unity
of the Three Crowned Kings.

The fish-bannered king
Ukkira Peruvazhuthi

was lover
to a fish-eyed maiden.

One day,
while reading
a bedchamber epic
with that beloved,

she hung a question mark
from the corner of her eye
and said:

“My lord of my eyes!
Clear one doubt for me.”

<!-- scan 291 -->

“What is it, my dear?
What doubt do you have?

Are you asking me
how you came to possess
such softness,
like the feather
of that swimming swan?

Or do you wish to know
how honey came
to your lips,

though bees never
brought and poured it there,

yet I scoop it up
and drink it?

Was your face made
by looking at a red lotus?

Or, my radiant one,
was the red lotus itself
created only after looking
at your face —

is that your doubt?

Unable to bear
the two round globes,

will your waist,
writhing like the worm
upon a hook
cast for a kendai fish
in a pond,

continue to stand?

Or will your shining
ivory thighs
strike it
and hold it up?

These are all
the doubts growing
in your mind,
are they not?”

So asked Vazhuthi.

Before him,
she bowed her head
with modesty,
like a curved ray,

and said:

“I did not raise a question
so that you might write poetry
upon me and win a prize!

Tell me:
in delightful Tamil,
what does *eyil* mean?”

“Ah, foolish peahen!
Do you not know even this?

To stop an enemy army,
we build a fort
around the capital —

that is called *eyil*,”
said the king!

“I know that too.
But there is one thing!

There is said to be
an *eyil*
that no one can break;

and a king,
bubbling with pride,

is said to boast
that even ‘this man’
cannot come near it.
Do you know of that?”

<!-- scan 292 -->

When she said
“even this man,”

she pointed at Vazhuthi
and held him tight!

“That news
too has reached my ears.

The fort is said
to have been built
at a city called Kaanapper.

Our court poet
Aiyur Moolankizhar
has made a rare description
of it!

I shall tell it to you.
Listen:

in the order of years,
it deserves first place!

A long moat
deeper than the depths
of the earth;

high walls
rising to touch
the blue sky;

upon them,
missile-platforms
working swiftly
like the stars
crowding that sky;

and a guard-forest
so dense
that even sunlight
finds no place to enter;

surrounded as well
by countless little
supporting forts —

such is the Kaanapper *eyil*,
which will not move
even if a thousand elephants
butt against it!

Many times
I have rejoiced
hearing our poet
describe it
in poetic Tamil,”
said the king!

If, after laying siege,
one goes down
into the moat
surrounding the fort,

defying the resistance,

stones from slings
and rain-showers of arrows
fall from the walls above.

If one gets past those,

boiling oil
and molten lead
come rattling downward!

<!-- scan 293 -->

If one overcomes all that
and begins climbing
toward the top
of the fort,

the ladders are smashed.

Even after that,
metal-bird machines,
with beaks,

stab at the crown
of one's head.

Escaping all of those,
one must still descend
inside the fortress
on the other side!

“The Pandyas can see
enemies far away;

you bring foes
like tigers inside cages.

Yet nearby,
in the city called Kaanapper,

Vengai Marban
has built a fortress,
raised his sword,

and cries so loudly
the town shakes,

saying no one,
with any army,
can come near him!

After he has indirectly
mocked our courage,

would it not be shameful
for us to remain saying,
‘What is that to us?’

The doubt
that had lain for many days
at the bottom
of my heart's depth

I have brought
to its full stop today!

For a king
who can confront
even a rutting elephant
and split its forehead,

what is the wall
Vengai Marban has built —

the Vindhya mountain range?

Even if the moat
he has dug
is a deep ocean,

for my Pandyan
it is merely
a distance to cross!

Then why,
seeing the arrogance
of Vengai Marban
who issues the challenge,

should he lie quiet?”

<!-- scan 294 -->

“Let the king
clear this doubt.
After that,
we may issue the decree,”

she said,
sulking!

The Pandyan
caught the woman
who tried to move away,

held her tight,
interlaced their legs,

and said:

“Let morning blossom!

I shall order
our infantry,
elephants,
horses and chariot-force

to set out
toward the Kaanapper *eyil*.

Leave your worry!

Now,
take away this hand
that is trembling.”

She took it away!

Within the blink
of an eye,
the sulking was forgotten —

and union continued
until the rays
of morning spread!

The next day,
just as he had promised,

the ranks of the army
set out
so that Pandimadevi's wish
might be fulfilled!

They struck
the heavily guarded fortress
called Kaanapper *eyil*,

waged a fierce war,

and brought the siege
to its end.

Under the determined attack
of Ukkira Peruvazhuthi,

the defences
of Vengai Marban's fort
were shattered!

The Pandyan king
returned to tell
the victory-news

to the queen
who had placed
the victory mark
upon his forehead
and sent him forth.

Upon the wounded chest
of the king,

she gave pressing comfort
again and again
with the mound
of her own body!

Every honour-wound
from which blood still seeped

received her kisses
and healed completely!

★ ★ ★

Some days later,
Aiyur Moolankizhar
came to the Pandyan court.

<!-- scan 295 -->

“King!
What greatness —
that enemies who did not respect you
now respect and praise you!

O king
who knows nothing
but victory
when you enter the field!

From today,
let the epics sing you
as:

‘Ukkira Peruvazhuthi
who crossed the Kaanapper *eyil*!’

Let the poetry-stage
praise you!

As long as the sun exists,
let that name endure!”

So spoke the poet.

“Thank you, poet!”
said the king with humility,

and asked him:

“Vengai Marban,
who has been diminished
in this war —

will he,
spitting sparks of anger,

make another battlefield
to recover the Kaanapper *eyil*?”

The answer
Aiyur Moolankizhar gave

presented Mother Tamil
with a rare simile!

What was it?

If one drop of water
falls upon iron
heated by a smith
in the furnace-fire,

can that water
ever be recovered?

(In the same way),

the Kaanapper *eyil*
that Ukkira Peruvazhuthi
has conquered

has become
like a drop of water
absorbed by forge-heated iron!

“Vengai Marban
stands grieving in his heart,
speaking of your greatness,”
said the poet.

What prize
could the Pandyan give
worthy of the beauty
of that simile
bestowed by the golden poet?

I say
he must have struggled
not knowing!

Is there an error
in my thought?

You who know
the taste of Tamil —
tell me!

## Item 58 — Beside the Enemy Sword's Edge; Let Us Labour All Our Days!

<!-- scan 296 -->

Beside the enemy sword's edge;
let us labour all our days!

Kulothunga I,
who ruled with radiant fame
and unmatched distinction,

won in history
the stamp of such a king.

He maintained friendship
with China,

and, ruling the land,
gained distinction
by sending in CE 1077

a mission of seventy-two people
to the Chinese empire,
the source says.

Karikalan,
Senguttuvan,
Nedunchezhiyan —

the Three Crowned Kings
who had been great rulers
and great warriors —

in intelligence,
skill and courage,
Kulothunga conducted
an energetic rule
the source sets fully beside theirs.

More than that,
he displayed administrative ability
one step beyond,

shown, it says,
by his interest
in public-welfare measures
such as the land-survey system
and the order abolishing customs duty.

Kulothunga,
a hero among heroes,
made the Chola empire prosper.

After him,
however,
a weak government took shape.

Rajaraja II,
Rajadhiraja II,
Rajendra III —

and, in all,
seven Chola kings
ruled after him.

<!-- scan 297 -->

Among those seven,
Rajaraja III himself
became the weak one in rule,

and that history concludes
that he became a cause
of the fall of the Chola empire!

Already during
the reign of Kulothunga II,

the Pandyan king
Maravarman Srivallabhan,
who came to the throne in 1132,

encircled the source's
“Tiruvaranga Rayam”

and sowed the seed
for the Pandyan empire.

The end of that Pandyan empire too,
the source says,
arrived within
the fourteenth century.

Since it has already been noted
that Rajaraja III
was the weak one
among the Cholas,

the great warrior
Manavarman Sundara Pandyan,

as early as 1219,
leapt toward Thanjavur
with an army.

Terrified by the Pandyan attack,
Rajaraja III,

the source says,
left Thanjavur and Uraiyur
burned and levelled to the ground,

and ran into hiding,
thinking escape alone
was enough!

The Pandyan
held a victory celebration
in the coronation hall
at Thanjavur!

Then came the news
that King Sundara Pandyan
was resting
in the fine city
now called Ponnamaravathi.

On hearing it,
the Chola did not rage forth
to oppose him in battle.

Instead,
with both hands folded,
he went to beg
that the responsibility
of ruling the earth
be given back to him.

He ran like a storm
all the way to Ponnamaravathi.

“What news?
Why have you come?”
asked the Pandyan.

The Chola
who had ruled the earth
bent low,
bowed,
fell in supplication
and said —

<!-- scan 298 -->

“Pandyan king!
Leader praised by the world!

I have one request;
fulfil my wish.

My wife has come here
with me.

The two of us
fall at your feet
and ask:

show mercy,
thinking of the future
of our children.

It is enough
if you give back
our country
that you captured!

It would be noble
to return
the Chola land
you seized.

Moreover,
there need be
no disgrace in this
for us —

nor need there be
any for you.”

Seeing a king
who came in the line
of Rajaraja —

a Tamil who would not
bow his head —

now bend his head,
stoop and worship,

Sundara Pandyan said:

“Mother Tamil!
Alas —
forgive this Chola king
who has lost self-respect.”

And within his own heart,
Sundara Pandyan
placed a burning mark
upon himself.

It was he
who worried
about self-respect
and Tamil honour!

This condition
continued again and again:

among elder and younger brothers,
among kin,

the Three Crowned Kings
collided with one another,

made room
for other peoples,

and brought retreat
upon the Tamil race.

Thus the lands
of the Three Crowned Kings,
once great empires,

became minor kingdoms,
then smaller and smaller states,

and at last
ant-sized kingdoms —

<!-- scan 299 -->

For me,
who rejoiced
writing and writing
this once-rising history
of Tamil Nadu,

and for you,
who gained feeling
reading and reading it,

it is better now
not to continue it further.

Let us think
about ourselves.

Let unity
and relationship
among us grow stronger.

Let us labour
all our days —

and let us heap up victory
until every enemy sword
has its point blunted!
