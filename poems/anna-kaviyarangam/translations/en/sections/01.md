# Anna Kaviyarangam

## Source context

At the World Tamil Conference in Chennai,
Public Works Minister Kalaignar Karunanidhi
presided over the “Anna Kaviyarangam”
held on 7-1-68.

This is the poem sung by Kalaignar Karunanidhi
about Anna’s qualities, welcoming the poets
who took part in the kaviyarangam
and praising the excellence of their poetry.

## Poem

<!-- scan_page: 119 / printed_page: 19 -->

Kaveri, blooming with flowers—
Kaveri too,
leaping to the dark tresses,
“Place a hand! Vaigai!”
Leaping, placing a hand in the dark tresses,
Kaveri blooms with flowers;
a hand placed in the dark tresses
of the newly painted maiden, Tamil Woman—
(Pennaiyar)

O glorious southern land
that makes her a mother and drinks the milk-river, Palar—
great mountain of battle-fervour!
Charged with ruling the waters,
taking on the charge of ruling the clouds—today,
I, who stand apart from the art I love so deeply,
offer my greetings; receive them...
O my life—Anna; mothers; fathers;
golden-radiant brothers; famed poets;
guardians of our precious Tamil,
all born of Tamilagam ringed by the roaring sea—
on this sacred day, receive my greeting.

At the summit of life on earth,
we shall engrave the new—
so rose, thrilled and surging,
the freedom-warriors Tamilagam gave us.
That they may be born again
in fragrant, sweet-scented verse,
we have gathered the World Tamil Conference,
proclaiming,
“Every place is our town; everyone our kin”—

<!-- scan_page: 120 / printed_page: null -->

By the invitation given
by the committee that fosters bonds beyond our shores,
the leader of the poets—myself—
has come to the city of Poompuhar...
For a few moments setting aside the minister’s thoughts,
opening the heart that longs for noble art,
to hear the resounding drum of freedom—
my thanks to all who granted leave.

We have seen many men of worth
who gave themselves for the freedom of the homeland;
and as the commander under Periyar,
war-leader of that homeland’s freedom,
for the freedom of the Tamil language,
for the freedom of Tamil art,
for the freedom of Tamil culture,
labouring without weariness,
Anna speaks like a beating drum.
Eight poets here are singing of him—
can I alone leave him unsung?

Softer than the softness of flower-petals,
a sacred heart—a loving heart—
a mother’s heart that gathers all in embrace—
his flower-petals speak Tamil;
even the three fruits—mango, jackfruit, banana—would be defeated.
The flowers of his eyes become spears—become swords—
if any harm should come to Mother Tamil.
Though the flowers of his feet may wither,
his arduous journey will not stop!
The flowers of his hands clasp together
the brothers, the movement!
Those very flowers forgive enemies
and make their heads bow like ripened paddy—
so that the flower of democracy may sway,
the flower of equality may give fragrance,
and Mother-tongue Tamil itself may become the garden of life.
O breeze that dances,
O flower-crown that comes seeking us! O crown of fame!
In the heaps of greetings that seek you—each day
I shall fly as the bee that comes singing;
to protect you, I shall give myself up.

Why—
here is the great leader
of the song sung by ten million Tamil youth;
Mother Tamil, who two thousand years ago
was young as she is today,
gave birth to poets who made a thousand books
so that she might live for hundreds of thousands of crores of years;
yet our mother, goddess of art,
bore Valluvar as her first-born son.

If one says flower—it is the lotus;
if one says king—it is Karikalan;
if one says book—it is the Tirukkural:
so revered is the scripture of virtue
he gave us—and all humanity rejoiced.

Along the mountains that touch the sky—
like a poison-tree rising, seen and unseen by our eyes—
how did wounded doctrines flare up
in the cultured life of Tamils?
In the Tamil land where the Kural sounded without seeing caste,
how did crores of castes arrive halfway along the road!
Temples where the fragrance of knowledge spread disappeared,
and people found shrines where a thousand gods dwell.
People ruled by the palate, with no feeling for language—
whether Tamil perished or lived, what did they care?
On the great land where righteousness had been the goal,
they sowed alien ways—and heaped up error.
Like a field without rain, the Tamil mind changed.
Some Tamils went about as though no task could be done
without the four—envy, desire, anger, hurtful speech.
“Without wealth there is no world,” they said;
and in the land where wealth had once been amassed and plenty flourished,
darkness spread and misery planted its flag.
The just sceptre of the classical Tamil people,
a sceptre without evil—
it changed—
into a tortoise-paced staff,
into a truthless rod that crushes the voiceless.
Once Mother Tamil withered—she sang a song of grief;
Mother Tamil danced in burning fire;
she ran, she ran—
no path could be found.
Leaving the world of earth and living in the world of fame,
the great king of the city of poetry,
the master of the Kural—

<!-- scan_page: 121 / printed_page: null -->

she went to him and said,
“My precious son,
I have one request.”
“What is it, Mother?” asked the Kural-poet.
“In Tamilagam, where gold comes
wherever one digs,
you must be born again,” she said.
“To mine gold?” he asked.
“To turn the minds and lives of Tamils
into gold,” she said.
“What has happened now?” he asked.
“Danger has come to this mountain-like language,” she said.
“For the poor there is no hut to reach;
not knowing how to raise the rod sternly
and bring it down gently,
a rod that kills has risen,” she said;
and she told him too of the idle tales
spoken by calves in wisdom.

Wiping her tearful eyes,
Valluvar of nectar-speech asked,
“Mother, where shall I be born?”
She kissed the crown of the son who bowed,
and with arms like the hanging roots of a banyan embraced him;
like a farmer rejoicing that from now on
a ploughed field will not dry for want of seedlings,
her whole heart leapt with fullness.
“To pour gruel into the dried belly—
O sugar-candy! O honey-syrup! O Tirukkural!
be born in Kanchi,” she said.

He was born as our Anna;
as a king of knowledge—
as the breeze of the Pothigai hills,
as the fresh fragrance of Tamil feeling flowering into bloom,
as the meaning of a hymn,
as an ancient Tamil puram-song.
Anna arose—like the sun rising below the sky—
like a riddle to those who cannot understand!

To sing his fame,
we, the brothers he nurtured,
have taken the Tamil he gave us
and come here—this is the truth.
Like a stone released from a sling,
a poet who aims and hurls his words,
whose flavour pours like the flood of the Porunai river—
the great poet Anandam, rich in compassion,
who has sung many battle-paranis,
who has made a place of his own
in the realm of Tamil poetry,
a man of merit with a cool heart—
my own close friend, a fruit-sweet friend—
under the theme “Anna the Leader”
he sings an enduring poem.
Close to Anna,
he gives us an Anna-song;
we shall call it appam,
we shall call it aval,
we shall call it honey,
we shall call it millet-flour,
a great feast that never cloys—
(not for him)
for all of us!
Mmm! Serve it, sir!

### Poet Anandam sings “Leader Anna”

**M. K.**

All the world knows
that Anna is a leader,
a great leader—yet he has given us
similes we never imagined,
turning them into a new melody.
He lives at Mayiladuthurai,
on the Kaveri where cuckoos sing;
we rejoiced—well done!

Like the sea that sings in waves,
like Mamallapuram that sings through art,
Ponni Valavan, who sings through poetry, now comes.
Some people live by abuse—even now
Anna speaks of that with a deep sigh.
By his speech no one has been wounded;
many have been refined.

“One skilled in speech, tireless, unafraid—
it is hard for any to defeat him in debate,”
and with speech of such a nature
that binds those who hear
and makes even those who will not listen desire it—

<!-- scan_page: 122 / printed_page: null -->

speaking openly and sweetly,
he made the world hurry to heed his work.
All these qualities,
as “Anna the Speaker”—
Ponni Valavan, who went to prison
to guard Tamil, the breath of life,
now gives us.
Let us drink deeply of the fresh nectar!

### Ponni Valavan sings “Anna the Speaker”

**M. K.**

The immense power of the king of the tongue—his power of speech—
cannot be reached even by the songs of kings of verse;
yet this poet went as far as the boundary could be reached
and gave us a poem thick as set curd.
This much from him;
let it stop at that.

Now comes the pearl-like Tamil writer,
fragrant as a bunch of coriander greens,
the poet Subbu who composed a ballad for noble Gandhi;
a fine poet with a heart spread like a peacock’s fan,
with the song of a cuckoo—with the speech of a lovely parrot......
He has come to set a ruby
upon the crown Anna already wears
as “Anna the Writer.” Come—live long!

“Hey, brother, take up the pen!”—
thus he began to give garlands of writing,
ever-fresh flowers.
Like a farmer seeing grain
in fields that had lain like wilderness—
to the literary world lying like an oil-press bull,
he said, “Dance, peacock!”
Anna made it dance;
he established its fame.

Knowing that the single aim
of living by selling songs
was not enough to enrich the land,
this poet was among those who came with a Tamil page
to end the suffering that torments us.
He will speak with grace
of our Anna’s fame;
let us listen too.

### Kothamangalam Subbu sings “Anna the Writer”

**M. K.**

Today Anna rules the Tamil state—
he rules writing—
he rules speech, said Professor Kalki,
and went away bestowing the title “Bernard Shaw.”
To say how fitting that is,
with petals like flowers,
with petals of verse,
he has given us a melody.
Let us praise him
and go where the madman is.

Not a madman
whose head spins—whose world spins—
this man is mad for Tamil...
Scholar Anna, the crescent-wearing “madman”
(for having forged an alliance
with the Muslim party),
even in greying age,
is a great youth who stands against
the philosophy that says life is a foam-flecked water-bubble.
Let us receive today
the learned doctrine of that philosopher.
Tamilpithan, sing it for us—
well!

### Tamilpithan sings “Anna the Philosopher”

**M. K.**

Long live the one who gave a noble poem
so that the philosopher’s philosophy
may spread across the world.
When the rule of the stick lived,
Tamil rule did not flourish;
when the new government came,
giving the measure of rice,
Hindi came wagging its tail
to bring down the government of the Tamils.
If that is called
the greatness of democracy,
hearing it, Anna declares:
“the banner-bearing Tamil government will not sleep.”
To sing that Anna’s fame,
poet Mudiyarasan has arrived—
let us ask him to sing.

### Mudiyarasan sings “Anna, Guardian of the Mother Tongue”

**M. K.**

Honey mixed with milk,
ripe fruit’s sweet taste mixed in,
flesh mixed with life—
a poem that never cloys,
leaping with the speed of a deer,
he has poured down
like summer rain from the sky—

Mudiyarasu;
from former days to this day,
our poet-king.
Next,
why Vezhavendan is going to sing
under the theme “Anna as Mother”
I do not know.

Feeding me tender Tamil,
dressing me in every grace,
sending me off to school—
and rejoicing even when I donned battle-array,
saying,
“Do not fear, my son—live like a fighting bull!”—
when my mother, Mother Anjugam, departed,
she drew Anna near and said,
“From now on, you are mother to my son.”
If the poet-warrior comes to sing of that mother,
will he not cast me into a sea of tears?
Even so, as “Anna, the Mother,”
from afar he will sustain—he will console.
With that courage I ask to hear
the song of the victorious king—
Vezhavendan!

### Vezhavendan sings “Mother Anna”

**M. K.**

Squeezing the three fruits separately,
straining and blending them into one,
mixing in sugar and powdered candy in abundance,
placing mother-love beneath Anna’s title,
my friend Vezhavendan has given it to us—
very good!

In *Or Iravu*,
the world-famed *Velaikkari* entered *Kumari Kottam*,
rejoiced at *Chandrodayam*—and there
fell in love with a *Chandra Mohan*;
and thus *Needhi Devan Mayakkam*—
all these stories we saw Anna draw.

Was writing the plays enough?
Asking that question,
Muthulingam, who writes page after page
in *Murasoli*, conducted by my younger brother Maran,
has come here
to write and show, sweet as jaggery,
every sight the art world witnessed
of Anna’s power as an actor.
Sing, brother, as honey;
we are here as the bees.

### Muthulingam sings “Anna the Actor”

**M. K.**

Mixing fragrant ghee,
lowering it from a gentle heat—
sweeter than the sweet confection thus made,
clear nectar itself,
Anna acted.
In one of his plays,
I played the hero;
on the conference stage
splendidly assembled at Mettupalayam!
In that social portrait called *Chandrodayam*—

<!-- scan_page: 124 / printed_page: null -->

he took the role of Durairaj,
whirling like a battle-storm
to bring down Sazhakkarai—
and in the final scene,
something amusing happened because of me.

Saying there was no more work for him in this world,
Durairaj—Anna in the role—
drinks poison and dies.
His friend Sambasivam
(that was me)
must fall upon him and wail,
crying for five or six minutes.
That day the play was new to me;
forgetting that only the role had died,
thinking instead that Anna himself had passed away
(a mistaken thought),
emotion rushed through me.
I rolled and rose—recited the written prose—
and then, instead of getting up and ending the play,
I spoke my own dialogue for half an hour;
falling again and again upon Anna—
ah!—at last—the curtain too came down—
and I finished.

“Ayyo!” Anna cried as he rose,
holding his shoulder.
“What happened?” we all asked.
“This fellow cried and cried
and pressed down on my shoulder—look!
It is swollen; the pain is killing me,” he said—
laughing!
Even now, when I remember it,
I laugh to myself.

Enough of the joke; let it go.
Let us enter politics rich in novelty—
writing and speaking that
“a heart that can bear anything” is needed,
embracing those who came after hearing it;
saying, “He who has a younger brother fears no army”;
looking upon opponents with dignity;
asking, “If this army is defeated, what army can win?”—
this is the man who proved it:
the Buddha of Sarnath;
the Gandhi of nonviolence.

The whole world agrees;
only a few corpses here
let their bellies swell
with irritation!
Let them be lost to us.

Anna—
the victory he found in politics,
the victory the whole world praises,
the victory of becoming a minister—and even then
the victory of showing restraint,
the victory of preserving good peace—
and, fitting them all, that broad forehead—
how did all these victories come to flower?
So that the wondering Tamil world
may receive an explanation,
Rahman comes to sing with head held high—
like a bull freed from its tether,
he comes to dance with Tamil words—
to rule the stage with Anna.
Like a lion declaring
that chaff and rubbish can do nothing,
he has arrived;
a poet of valiant fame—
to give Tamil like the juice of fruit—
he is about to mount this stage—
my friend Rahman—yes,
Abdul Rahman—

### Abdul Rahman sings “Anna the Chief Minister”.

**M. K.**

They called him Leader; they called him Philosopher;
they called him Actor; they called him King of Drama;
they said he possessed writing-power
rich with the savour of eloquence;
they called him a Man; they called him a Gem;
they called him a Minister of the State—
they called him Mother; Guardian of the precious tongue;
they called him a Politician.
Those who have no time
to say all these one by one,
let them, from the love in their hearts,
call him by a single word—Anna.
His mother too gave him that name—
Long live Anna! Long live, and well!
