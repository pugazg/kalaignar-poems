## Section 1

<!-- scan_page: 6 -->

### One-Sided Love

Nakkannai, a woman poet who shaped verse in rock-candy-sweet Tamil,
did not content herself with songs merely of this world and the beyond;
in the Sangam collections called Akam, Puram and Natrinai,
six poems of hers were set like golden gems —
the very juice pressed from fruits of love!

Along with those six poems,

there is the martial mother called Kavarpentu,
who, when a maiden asked about the son who had gone to battle,
swelled with pride and said, “Here is the cave where the tiger dwelt,”
and made a puram poem ring out as a song of war;
that very mother, who raised the Chola prince
Perunarkilli like a foster-mother of his own;
Nakkannai, entranced by Killi’s beauty and prowess,
floating through dream-worlds as though drinking him in by the handful;
and the great-hearted valour of Perunarkilli,
which the master poet Sattanthaiyar fashioned
into a stately, striding puram song
and crowned with a garland of praise —
all these I studied a little more deeply,

<!-- scan_page: 7 -->

and from that study a fancy sprang in me,
like an aerial root from a banyan!
The love that leapt in Nakkannai toward Narkilli,
overflowing like milk from a golden bowl
and spilling upon the ground —

that tale I now shape as a little serial picture
called *One-Sided Love*,
a brief epic of love.
Read it — and savour it!

♦

In Uraiyur, the ancient capital
of the prosperous Chola land,
where crops in the fields swayed
like a young peacock in green silk and girdle dancing Bharatanatyam,
there sat Veera Venman Veliyan Tittan
beneath the white royal victory umbrella,
pouring away the state’s great wealth
into sacrifices and yajnas performed by ascetics!
“This does not befit Tamil tradition,” said his son,
standing in the way and continuing to oppose it.

But the Chola king Tittan brushed aside
every argument put forward by his son Perunarkilli.
Like an ignorant child who, not knowing the worth
of bitter medicine that cures disease,
refuses to drink it and knocks it away,
he rejected every sound counsel Narkilli gave
and went on pouring out the wealth of the realm.

<!-- scan_page: 8 -->

<!-- full-page illustration; no source caption -->

<!-- scan_page: 9 -->

“Filling the royal treasury is the people’s labour!
Is a king’s task to turn their lives into wasteland
and pierce their hearts?
The glory of rulers is to embrace their people and govern;
to set fire to a noble purpose
is the deed of those without principle!

Pot after pot of milk and honey that could be shared and enjoyed
are poured into the fire;
goats, cattle and horses are cut down as sacrifice;
sacrificial fires are fed and tended
so that an oblation-share may be received —
is this not an act that shakes the very foundation
of the doctrine that says, ‘Love itself is God’?”

So Killi questioned and tried to stop it.

“I am the king who rules this land!
And you, my own son, stand against me,
your tongue callused from speaking atheism?
Before I cut out that tongue
and throw it to dogs and jackals,
walk out of this country!”
Tittan commanded.

“I leave today the Uraiyur that gave me birth;
but, O king,
you call it atheism when I say
that God is one and asks for no living sacrifice.
Yet you —
to behold a heaven you say hangs above the sky —
turn this world into hell
and torment living beings!
Even a madman might be brought to reason;
but with the decision that my father Tittan
can no longer be corrected,
I go.”

<!-- scan_page: 10 -->

Thus the golden image Perunarkilli left Uraiyur
and went to Amur in Mukkaval Nadu.
At that very moment,
the sovereign of words, the poet Sattanthai,
refused to remain before the Chola king
and followed Killi.

When the young prince had abandoned a throne
for the sake of the convictions he held,
the office of court poet seemed trivial;
Sattanthai cast it aside
and stayed beside Killi,
vowing to guard without decline
the honour, valour, culture and tradition of the Tamil people.

♦

One day in Amur of Mukkaval Nadu,
a brutal wrestler came running in, slapping his shoulders,
and roared:
“Who is there in this town who can defeat me?”
He issued his challenge and declared that,
if the men of the town could not stand before him,
all of Mukkaval Nadu would become his possession!

Hearing this, the ruler of the land
strode forward in a warrior’s gait
and tried to bring down that human wild-buffalo in wrestling —
but was defeated!

“Aha! Amur is mine now!”
the victorious wrestler shouted.
Before him,
with the speed of a star falling from the sky,
a warrior leapt down with a thunderous stamp upon the earth —

<!-- scan_page: 11 -->

“Wrestler! Stand where you are!”
he cried, beating his chest,
and the hero of the warrior clan
sprang upon that human beast!

“Who is it? Who is it?”
Amur trembled with the cry.
“It is Perunarkilli, son of Tittan of Uraiyur!”
shouted one crowd, clapping their hands.
“What will happen?”
said another crowd, standing with tear-filled eyes.

“The ruler of Amur himself could not win;
can victory come to a youth so young?”
The people of Mukkaval Nadu
stood astonished,
fingers pressed to their noses.

“One puff and you will fly away like a straw!
And you carry this much arrogance?”
The brutal wrestler charged at Killi.

Let us watch from a little distance
the wrestling that whirled like a violent cyclone,
through the window that is Sattanthaiyar’s poem.

Narkilli sprang upon the wrestler,
folded one leg across the man’s chest,
blocked the opponent’s attack with the other,
and locked himself hard against the brute’s back.
Like a hungry elephant trying to eat bamboo,
bending it, breaking it and trampling it,
he battered and broke the wrestler’s head and legs
and cast him dead upon the earthen arena floor.

<!-- scan_page: 12 -->

The poet hailed the Chola prince
with the praise-title “Poravaik Ko-Peru-Narkilli,”
then grieved deeply:
“Did Tittan, the tiger who rules Uraiyur,
not come to see his son’s valour?”

How swiftly Narkilli fought —
the great Tamil poet Sattanthai
lets honey drip from his words
and explains it through an image.
Here it is:

★

> “As though a festival had begun,
> as though a woman had reached the hour of childbirth,
> as evening rain fell when the sun was sinking,
> the strap-threading needle in the hand
> of the lowly man binding a cot flew in haste —
> faster still, it seemed, was the battle
> of the tall, garlanded hero
> with the warrior who came to seize the town!”

(*Purananuru*, poem 82  
Poet: Sattanthaiyar)

I shall give its fitting sense
in the Tamil I have learnt.
You will then feel the sweetness
of this finely wrought comparison!

“A labourer of low station weaving a cot
must hurry if he is to sell it and make his living
before the festival begins.
At that very time comes news,
piercing his ear,
that the wife he married
is writhing in the pains of childbirth.
He must finish his work and leave —
and then,

<!-- scan_page: 13 -->

in the roadway of the sky,
the sun sinks westward
and signs of heavy rain appear.
How fiercely, then,
would the needle in the cot-weaver’s hand
race as it drives the leather strap through!
Faster even than that needle,
Killi finished the battle
and flung his enemy away like dust!”

Thus,
when Narkilli bowed his head in thanks
both to the garland of verse
from the master of threefold Tamil
and to the flower garlands
from the people of Mukkaval Nadu,
a woman of artistic beauty stood there,
her heart surging in waves of love,
and asked her friend with her eyes:
Could he be won with love?

The maiden who could not hold herself steady,
who poured down like heavy rain
rushing from a mountain peak,
was the young woman called Nakkannai!

### Source explanation

- `சாறு` — festival.
- `தலைக்கொண்டென` — as though it had begun.
- `ஈற்று உற்றென` — as though the time of childbirth had arrived.
- `பட்டமாரி` — rain.
- `ஞான்ற ஞாயிறு` — the time when the sun’s rays fade / sunset.
- `நினக்கும்` — binding.
- `போழ்தூண்டு ஊசி` — the needle used to thread a leather strap.

## Section 2

<!-- scan_page: 14 -->

Nakkannai was the Tamil creeper
born to the great merchant Perungozhinaykan.

In the Chola land,
where the Kaveri does not fail even when the sky does,
its waters spreading abundance and making gold flourish,
commerce had grown across the seas.
At the harbour of Kaveripoompattinam were heaped
swift horses brought by water,
sacks of black pepper brought by land,
clusters of gems born in the northern mountains
and glittering gold,
fragrant sandalwood from the western hills
and smoke-sweet akil,
pearls from the southern sea,
coral from the eastern sea,
the produce of the Ganges,
the bounty of the Kaveri,
food from Eelam,
wares from Kadaram,
and countless other things,
piled high as imports and exports
and raising the distinction of the Tamil south.

Seeing such prosperity,
the poet Kadiyalur Uruthirankannanar
set down the wealth of Karikala Chola’s age
in the ancient Tamil work *Pattinappalai*,
singing it into lasting praise.
Descended through generations
from that renowned merchant tradition,
Perungozhinaykan flourished in Amur in those days.

<!-- scan_page: 15 -->

A diamond statue cast by the jewel among merchants,
Nakkannai was a beauty of form,
her speech the prattle of a parrot.
The whole world would become slave
to those light-radiating eyes!
In hip and gait she had
the joyous motion of a dancing peacock.

When the light of intelligence joins beauty,
is it not nectar, nectar, nectar —
like a thousand jars of honey
breaking and mingling in a waterfall?
So did Nakkannai give fragrance to the world,
a poet of Tamil nectar.

She took a palm-leaf in her flower-soft hand,
held the stylus,
and wrote as a poem
the valour of the handsome Narkilli,
who had defeated and felled the wrestler of Amur
as though mounting a bull and breaking its horn.

What she wrote,
she lifted and read again and again.
For some reason she herself grew shy;
then she lay down without picking it up,
could not sleep,
sat up again,
and pressed what she had written against her breast.

“Is there sugarcane that turns sweet without being bitten?
Yet there is love that can be tasted without even seeing!
And as proof that comes without seeking,
here I am today,
rolling about with eyes that will not close.”

So the tender creeper lamented to herself
and suffered,
when a voice came:

“Why do you tremble
like a poor man who has lost his gold?

<!-- scan_page: 16 -->

<!-- full-page illustration; no source caption -->

<!-- scan_page: 17 -->

I am asking you, peahen!”
Her dearest friend Porkodi had come near.

“You still do not understand why I tremble?
Listen to what I have written!”
Nakkannai picked up the palm-leaf.
Kissing every letter with both her eyes,
she explained to her friend
the echo of her heart:

★

> “Though this is not my lord’s town,
> though this is not my lord’s country,
> one group says, ‘He will win, he will win!’
> another says, ‘He will not win!’
> How good are these two kinds of words
> spoken by so many!
> With my fine anklets ringing, I ran,
> and at our house,
> stood close against the thick-trunked palmyra —
> I saw with my own eyes
> that victory became his.”

(*Purananuru*, poem 85  
Poet: Nakkannaiyar  
Hero praised: Chola Poravaikko Perunarkilli)

“With the fire of scorching love
I have cooked this dish of delight
and composed this poem in weighty Tamil.
My dear one, my friend Porkodi,
listen — I shall give its meaning in a simple manner.

The town is not the town
of the lord who has captured my heart and life;
nor is this his country.
So one group said
the parrot of victory would perch upon his shoulder,
while another said victory would belong to the wrestler
and not to this warrior.

<!-- scan_page: 18 -->

Though they spoke two different views,
my heart found happiness in the fact
that both groups were thinking only of him.
Certain, certain that my beloved would win,
I ran with my anklets ringing,
my heart thrilling as I waited for the battle’s end.
At my house I stood hidden
behind the tall palmyra that had grown there,
all my thoughts leaping toward him,
and at last I saw the one who had stolen me
stand victorious.”

Thus she gave both the poem
and the meaning of the poem,
then performed a penance before her friend,
begging for a boon:
“Tell me a way
for this tormenting love to be fulfilled.”

Her friend took Nakkannai by the hand and said:

“Red-lotus flower!
Do not imagine that the red sun you seek
stands far away in the crimson sky!
The young warrior who defeated the brutal wrestler
the day before yesterday
is staying with the venerable poet Sattanthai
only three houses away from here.
Come with me — I will show you.”

“If the sky itself comes down toward the pond,
then the sun is indeed within reach of the lotus —
and yet,

<!-- scan_page: 19 -->

*achcham, naanam, madam, payirppu* —
fear, modesty, reserve and decorum,
the qualities prescribed for a woman —
warn me and hold back my hand!

If I go to him by force,
fall into his net,
and the whole town learns of it,
what if he laughs aloud
and tells me, ‘Go back the way you came’?
Would not my flower of love,
before it had borne fruit or even ripened,
become a blossom that opened in the morning
only to wither at once?

My eyes have seen the sun and the moon,
the sea and sky,
mountain and cloud,
peacock, dove, parrot and cuckoo,
lion, tiger, leopard and deer,
the beauty of nature, gardens of delight,
man-made statues and the subtle art of sculpture.
Yet whenever they saw all these,
my eyes returned in an instant
to their place upon my face!

Do you know my condition today?
The eyes that saw that sweet man
have still not returned to me!
To you alone I have told this truth.”

Hearing Nakkannai speak,
Porkodi decided that no medicine
of any country could cure the illness that had seized her friend.
She resolved to break the news openly
to Perungozhinaykan.

At that moment,
“Daughter! Nakkannai!”
came a voice calling,

<!-- scan_page: 20 -->

and the merchant-chief entered the house.
“Listen, child — I have joyful news!
I have invited Perunarkilli,
who defeated the wrestler,
to our house for the midday meal tomorrow!
I sought the consent of the poet Sattanthai;
he too has agreed.”

A tingle began at the tiny toes
of her jewel-flower feet;
the two mangoes of her breast
rose fuller and fuller;
the young woman’s whole body thrilled and bristled;
her cheeks turned the red of henna.
With a leap that would shame a fawn,
Nakkannai — without even fainting —
fell into her friend’s lap!

- `என்னைக்கு` — to my lord / leader.
- `அன்மையானும்` — even though it is not / even if absent.
- `ஆடு` — victory.
- `போந்தை` — palmyra tree.
- `குடமலை` — western mountain.
- `குணகடல்` — eastern sea.

## Section 3

<!-- scan_page: 21 -->

Nakkannai had thought she could send her friend Porkodi as a messenger
and begin the continuing tale of her love.
Now good news came as though the treasure she had gone seeking
had itself come looking for her —
yet another piece of news her father told her
threw her mind into great confusion!

A king from an enemy country, intending to seize Amur,
was first coming with troops to drive away its herds of cattle;
to repel that enemy, the ruler of Amur,
planning to send the mighty Killi as commander,
had asked for his help;
the field-battle fought against an enemy that steals cattle,
called the Karantai battle,
would begin when Killi set out the next evening to the beat of the war-drum;
and before that,
he would come to Nakkannai’s house to dine.

Only after Perungozhinaykan had finished the whole account
did she push out a great sigh and find room to breathe again.
Without moving her tongue,
the doll-like Nakkannai blessed her father within her heart!

♦

<!-- scan_page: 22 -->

<!-- full-page illustration; no source caption -->

<!-- scan_page: 23 -->

Night and day went about their own work,
ceaselessly carrying out upon the earth
the duty nature had assigned them.
Yet is it not the way of lovers,
according to their own state,
to curse both night and day,
and then to raise them up in praise?

When two bodies have become one
and the very world is forgotten,
some leap up in furious anger:
“Why must this night already race toward its end?”

Others, who spend all day tortured by longing,
vow, “Tonight in bed we shall settle accounts with it!”
When evening comes their hearts grow cool;
rejoicing that the monkey-like day has gone,
they bless the night that comes
suited to a lovers’ meeting!

But here our heroine Nakkannai was in another state.
Her long eyes, like tender palmyra fruit, stayed open;

all night in bed, without sleep,
sitting upright, leaning over, turning, rising,
her every thought fixed upon Killi,
she lost all steadiness!

Those lying within an embrace may startle and ask,
“Has the night ended already?”
But she fretted:
“Why does only this ‘Amur night’
move like a tortoise?”

<!-- scan_page: 24 -->

“If the dawn-sun does not rise quickly,
how shall I gain the chance
to welcome my Killi, dear as life itself?
What is wrong with this town’s rooster —
a cough? a cold? a fever?
Or has something blocked its throat?
Why has our rooster still not called the dawn?
If these lazy creatures refuse to crow,
will daybreak itself refuse to wake?
Where has my friend gone?
If she were here,
I could make her crow like a rooster
and wake the sun!”

The lightning-vine maiden Nakkannai grieved deeply;
she loosened her braided hair, then tied it again.
She ran about asking,
“What has happened to this flower of the day?
Why has it still not opened?”

“O limpid nectar! O Lakshmi! O honey-soaked painting!
Why do you alone languish without sleep at midnight?”

Her guileless-hearted mother came near
and asked, pouring out love like a flood.

“Mother, if a thorn pricks the foot,
it can easily be pulled out.
But if that very thorn has pierced our heart,
is removing it such an easy thing?”

“You are keeping one thing inside
and speaking another outside, daughter!
Before I flare up in anger,
tell me the truth,” she said.

<!-- scan_page: 25 -->

“Mother, listen! I am planning
to write a rare poem
and give it as a gift to Killi,
who is coming to our feast tomorrow,
that auspicious day.
That is why I am flying, beating my wings,
through the sky of imagination!”

With this carefully ripened lie
she sent her mother away,
then lay down, hugging the pillow to her breast.

Though she escaped by telling her mother
that she could not sleep and was suffering,
trying to write poetry because no imagination would conceive,
Nakkannai nevertheless wrote upon a palm-leaf
a poem like a golden gem,
hinting at her true condition.
She hid it inside the cloth across her breast
and waited ready to give it to Killi
when he came the next day.

Knowing none of this,
her mother believed her daughter’s words
and rested her head in the hall.
Poor innocent mother,
unaware of her daughter’s state!

♦

Night opened the casket of its eyelids;
white, wakeful day slowly peeped in.

At the appointed hour the town rooster crowed without fail!
In the eastern quarter the rising sun appeared!

♦

<!-- scan_page: 26 -->

In the kitchen of the seafaring merchant’s house,
five or six servants gathered
and ran about in a great commotion.

“Let every serving leaf be a full plantain leaf,” said the mother.
“Let bunch upon bunch of fruits be heaped up,” said the father.
“Fry the minced meat till ghee drips from it,” said the friend.
“Make the pepper gravy for the chicken joint
so fragrant that the tongue itself is perfumed,” said Nakkannai.
“Before the seeraga-samba rice goes into the pot,
mix water and milk in equal measure,” said the mother.
“When you lift the firm curd by hand,
it must sit like a ball of butter,” said the father.
“Soak the appam in goat-leg bone broth
and make its taste richer,” said Nakkannai.

“Only do not forget the salt!
Food without salt and one-sided love —
both belong in the rubbish!” said her friend.

Nakkannai started!
Her state of floating like a raft
upon the sacred pool of delight vanished;
she wilted on the spot
at that inauspicious utterance.
“Wrong! Wrong, girl!” cried the friend,
striking herself under the chin,
but Nakkannai’s anger would not subside.

<!-- scan_page: 27 -->

When all the invited guests had arrived,
Perungozhinaykan welcomed them lovingly and seated them.
With a smile flowering on his face,
he introduced his wife Perundevi Nachchi,
his daughter Nakkannai,
and her close friend Porkodi
to the ruler of Amur,
the bull-like warrior Narkilli,
and the great poet Sattanthai who had come with them.

Then Perunarkilli pointed with swelling pride
to a noble lady:
“Do you know who this is?
When I lost my mother in youth,
she made good that loss,
guarded my precious life
and raised me to be what I am today.
This is Kavarpentu,
virgin queen of Tamil poetry.”

They joined their palms and greeted one another,
exchanged ripened affection,
and finished the feast.
Then, her eyelids fluttering,
Nakkannai hurried toward Killi,
who stood like a vengai tree.

“You have come here
and filled both our house and our hearts with delight.
I have turned my heartfelt thanks
into a poem on a palm-leaf.
Read it whenever you have leisure — that is enough,” she said.

Afraid he might read it aloud
in front of parents and all the others,

<!-- scan_page: 28 -->

Nakkannai stood there almost wholly forgetting herself.

“My lord! Fierce enemies are coming toward Amur
with cavalry!”
A sword-bearing guard came running like the wind.

“I go to the camp to array the army.
Give me leave,” said Narkilli with a bow.
Walking with the stride of a tiger,
he sprang upon his horse like a storm.
The moment he reached the Amur camp,
he ordered the troops to move out rank by rank.
In the midst of that urgency,
he also tried to read quickly
the poem Nakkannai had given him.

This was the palm-leaf:

★

> “My lord, though he eats humble fare,
> is broad of shoulder indeed;
> I, though I dwell close by,
> have become golden-pale.
> When my lord enters the battlefield to meet war,
> in the roaring great town at its festival ground,
> those boastful warriors who exhaust their own strength
> become like salt-merchants frightened by a dangerous ford!”

(*Purananuru*, poem 84  
Poet: Nakkannaiyar  
Praised hero: Chola Poravaikko Perunarkilli)

<!-- scan_page: 29 -->

Narkilli read the poem;
then he recast its meaning
into lines of verse in a simpler manner.

Here it is:

“My noble lord, who has lost his land of Uraiyur,
may live eating simple food,
but he has mighty shoulders
from which enemies flee in fear!

What use is it that my home
stands close to where he lives,
when there is no way
for his body to touch my tender shoulders?
Waiting for that day,
my body has withered greatly
and the golden pallor of lovesickness has spread over me!

And when my matchless lord goes to war,
some warriors strut proudly through the battle-festival
of the great town,
bragging as though they could frighten salt merchants into flight;
yet before my lord
they lose the very strength they boast of.

As he makes the opposing warriors suffer,
is it right that my lord
should make me suffer too?”

Thus,
in the poem Nakkannai had written and given him,
Narkilli saw more than poetic grace.
Afraid that if he were bewitched by her words
he would be caught fast,
he frightened his own heart into obedience and restrained it.

“I love the war-sword —
not the flower-petal!
No! No! Certainly not!”

<!-- scan_page: 30 -->

He lifted the sword high as he said it,
then climbed a hillock
and called the warriors to him — that bull of a man!

## Section 4

<!-- scan_page: 31 -->

“People of Amur! Rise roaring to war!
Let the fortress drums of Mukkaval Nadu thunder!

The enemy plants its foot on our soil
beating its proclamation drum;
we shall beat the war-drum
and turn that enemy into a mound of dust!

A person takes form
inside the womb of a woman;
then takes the form of a grave
inside the womb of this earth!

A fulfilled life does not mean
living for a hundred years!
Even if one dies young,
if it is for a purpose —
for people of honour
there is no life more fulfilled than that!

A jewelled crown, a ruby throne,
a couch, caresses,
hundreds to serve,
all and sundry standing with folded hands and pleading —

all these are easy to obtain in this world!
By smiling obsequiously, bending low,
raising one man up and praising another,

calling one who knows no propriety
an incomparable lord;
calling one who has never learnt gracious speech
a teacher of the good path;

calling one who rejects culture
a thinker famed throughout the world;
calling one bent on revenge
a broad-hearted worthy;

<!-- scan_page: 32 -->

<!-- full-page illustration; no source caption -->

<!-- scan_page: 33 -->

calling one who values others lower than dogs
better even than a mother;

calling an unlettered fool
a great learned elder;
calling a wicked scoundrel
a good man for the whole earth;

by writing and speaking of virtues that do not exist,
everything can easily be obtained in this world!
But —

to hold that honour alone is the good life,
to embrace death and still smile on the battlefield,

to fall bearing the warrior’s wound upon the chest
and attain great fame —
that is a greatness hard indeed to win!

More than the plundered delight
of embracing and sporting with a maiden of one’s clan,
there is this:
to give our lives in battle
so that our maiden Earth does not become slave to the enemy,
and then to take a long sleep
upon her broad lap —
that is the enduring delight,
the path to something that lasts!

So that the history of the Tamil people
does not stagger,
rising and falling like waves of the sounding sea;
so that it spreads across the earth
like fragrance from a flowering grove,
there is one life-giving principle — honour!

Those who pawn that principle away somewhere
merely to preserve jewels and palaces —

<!-- scan_page: 34 -->

we shall call them *takkai men*;
we will not accept them as Tamils!

Therefore —

the other, mango-gold,
the magical body of the maiden.
Two vessels of nectar pressed deep against his chest,
and her water-lily eyes quivered —
pleasure! pleasure!

The fall of dream-frost was scattered;
the sun-ray of waking memory rose.
Killi opened his eyes,
his face spilling delight,
and threw one leg up over the pillow.

As he lay there on the soft bed in ecstasy,
a rolled palm-leaf message flew in and fell upon him.
He picked it up and read.
Killi sprang up, trembling.

“Tonight Princess Aiyai
will be carried away captive from here.”

Seeing those words on the leaf,
Killi laughed aloud.
It seemed that Katti and Panan, who had fled and hidden,
still had not cured the itch in their bodies!
“To protect my own sister Aiyai,

<!-- scan_page: 92 -->

this sword at my side is enough!” he roared,
and set out like the fire of cosmic destruction
toward the palace where his sister slept.

## Section 11

<!-- scan_page: 93 -->

On a splendid bed shaped like a blue peacock
with its tail spread wide,
good maiden Aiyai lay deep in sleep,
her anicham-flower body covered
by a garment soft and winding like a serpent,
knowing nothing of her brother’s approach.

There had been no exaggeration
when Katti and Panan, before Kavarpentu,
had praised Aiyai’s beauty in imagination!

Paranar once wrote a poem about a maiden
as though stripping away her garment;
the Chola princess now stood as its fitting example —
cast in gold,
endowed with milk-pots that could bewitch bulls!

The breeze slipped mischievously through the lattice
and shifted her upper cloth.
The stars spread across every quarter of the sky
blinked in startled wonder:
how could a white moon rise between two mountains
and pour its coolness upon a palace bed?
Eager, in abundance, to know the mystery,
it was as though they held a council of secret deliberation!

That night,
through the curtain stirred and teased by the wind,
a figure wrapped in a black cloak entered,
a dagger in hand.
It came to Aiyai’s bed,
looked all around,
and fixed its gaze on that maiden
of purest ten-and-a-half-touch gold.
At that very moment,
Killi too arrived with sword in hand
to protect his sister.

Could Aiyai simply be carried away like a temple statue?
Was she mute? Deaf? A lifeless image?
The dark-cloaked figure seemed to decide
that only after drugging her unconscious
with a poisonous herb
could she be turned statue-still and taken captive.
It set the dagger down
and began moving a packet of herbs
toward Aiyai’s nose.

“Fool! Brute! Stop there!”
Like a hunter springing upon a beast,
warrior-tiger Narkilli appeared
and raised his sword,
setting his aim for the throw.

Thinking that any further delay
would ruin the intended purpose,
the dark figure snatched up the dagger.
Aiyai’s hand tried to stop it — but the figure

<!-- scan_page: 96 -->

shoved her back onto the bed
and raised the dagger straight toward her chest.
Then iron-hearted Narkilli hurled his sword
at the black-cloaked shape.
The lamp beside the bed fell,
and thick darkness flooded everything!

A cry of agony rose — “Ah!” — and died away.
Realizing that it was the voice of a beautiful woman,
Killi screamed:
“Aiyai! Aiyai! My beloved sister!
Have I myself come as Yama to take your life?”

He quickly lit a lamp
and rushed forward trembling,
rain pouring from both eyes.

What a sight awaited him there!
Aiyai was unharmed.
But Nakkannai herself
had taken into her chest
the sword hurled by the prince of Uraiyur
and was bidding farewell to life.
The black cloak that had hidden her form
lay cast aside in a corner.

Aiyai lifted her and held her close.
“Brother! What injustice is this?” she cried.

“Princess! Do not blame him for anything!
This is the sweet ending I sought for myself!
Did I not make my vow before you?
Do you not remember?
I have now fulfilled it before your very eyes!

<!-- scan_page: 97 -->

‘If I live, I shall live as his wife;
if I fall, I shall fall upon the point of his sword!’
When I said that,
Narkilli flung back that neither would happen.
Now look — look whose sword has felled me!

I changed the words and wrote a palm-leaf message
saying that you, Princess, would be carried away captive,
and your brother was deceived by it!
Thinking me an enemy,
without even knowing what he was doing,
he has somehow fulfilled my vow for me!
For that, it is you I must thank again and again!
Only because I pretended as though I were killing you
did my body gain the fortune
of kissing your brother’s sword!

Though I could not finish building
the fortress I raised inside my heart,
I have not strayed by even an atom
from my resolve
to give my life, just as I intended,
to the steadfast son of Tittan.
I have achieved a wondrous victory!”

Not even a shadow of pain spread across her face.
Like a warrior accepting death on the field,
Nakkannai continued to speak.

<!-- scan_page: 98 -->

“Forgive me, Nakkannai!” Killi sobbed.
He knelt,
placed her head upon his lap,
and wept.
Nakkannai wiped the tears from his cheek
with her soft kanthal-flower fingers.

“Your brother loves the war-sword!
Not the flower-petal! No! No! Never!”

Even as she gave her life to the sharpened sword,
the straight-gazing maiden spoke those words directly.
Rootless as a tree, Narkilli swayed without balance.

“After I had yielded,
I was waiting to rush here with mother
and receive you as the lamp of Uraiyur palace.
Before that could happen,
your life has ended
because of the darkness of my ignorance!” he cried.

“Has mother Kavarpentu returned?”
she asked with sudden joy,
then let her head sink.
Bathed in a waterfall of blood pouring in torrents,
the queen of poetry spoke once more:

“My beloved! Today my love has borne fruit!
Do you know how the mother who bore me
kept watch over me?”

1. When rain falls even in pitch darkness,
bringing life to all who labour on the earth,
so that the stalks of the lotus spread
and the opened flower-petals fall,
and when wind-tossed waves
scatter droplets beyond the little banks

<!-- scan_page: 99 -->

of the golden-lotus tank,
a watchman guards that water without rest.
So too the mother who bore me
kept watch over me without closing her eyes!

But to reach you,
I broke past even her guard
and ran here,
like a drop of water that strikes the little bank of a tank
and leaps beyond it!

Now you shed tears.
I rejoice, thinking of them
as drops of rosewater
sprinkled at our wedding of love!
My lord who loves only the war-sword —
will your gracious heart consent,
just once,
to bestow mercy upon this flower-petal too?”

His budding moustache touched her lips,
and he gave her a hundred kisses,
sweet as sugarcane.
She received only one —
for at that very moment her life flew away.
The man of Uraiyur fainted and fell.
