# One-Sided Love — 8

<!-- Reviewed English translation from the Tamil final-cleared canonical section. -->

<!-- scan_page: 64 -->

Like a hand-spear hurled in battle,
Nakkannai shot forward in fury.
And like that spear after entering an elephant’s body,
she fell exhausted,
withering down upon the earth.

When she opened her eyes,
she realized that her head lay at the poet’s feet.
“I thought you would give medicine
for the wound in my heart.
Though you knew that Killi would not even think
of retrieving a vessel once it had sunk
in the cool, vast sea,
why did you shatter our honour
in the middle of the assembly
like a pot made of clay?”
She asked through tears,
her words breathing fire.

“Mother Nakkannai! Do not kill me with your tears!
I did act in haste in the assembly,
but you do not know what truly happened.
To learn the heart of the son of Uraiyur,
I spoke openly with him one night,
for your sake,
in the ceaseless war-camp at the battlefront.

‘Let the war end; we can speak afterward.
Let tomorrow dawn with news of victory,’
Narkilli said,
letting a smile play upon his face before he went away.

<!-- scan_page: 65 -->

I believed him.
I thought he was slipping away out of shyness!

Is there any grove that can forbid the breeze?
What mountain refuses the embrace of a honeyed waterfall?
So I believed that this southerner too
would marry you, maiden.
I fixed a wedding day with your father,
announced it in the hall,
and in my mind had already brought the marriage to fulfilment.
When Killi refused,
I died!

What speaks to you now, mother,
is only a lifeless body!
This Sattanthai is only bagasse with all its juice pressed out!
A top without its string cannot spin;
how can your poetry-filled heart understand my state, mother?”

As rivers of tears ran from his eyes
and the silver hairs of his beard
held drops like white pearls,
the poet trembled as though disgrace itself had come upon him.
Nakkannai mixed calm into her words and began to speak:

“No, poet — do not grieve.
Give an answer to pain instead.
Sattanthai — a deathless father to Tamil!
Heaps of gold and hills of diamond, cat’s-eye and pearl,
lofty mansions and halls,
forts and towers,
a realm beneath a victory umbrella
before which men bow and praise,
and spectacles that steal proud hearts through hymns of glory —

<!-- scan_page: 66 -->

<!-- full-page illustration; no source caption -->

<!-- scan_page: 67 -->

all these may exist today
and be gone tomorrow!
But is not the labour done for Tamil
a labour that endures forever?

Therefore,
as long as Tamil exists, you will live;
and as long as this earth exists,
Tamil too will live!” said Nakkannai.

“Good, mother.
For all the years I have left,
I shall spend myself ensuring
that Tamil does not slowly die!
O poet-maiden who fashions victorious words —
but must you alone go after Killi?”

Ashamed to answer the poet’s question directly,
she changed the subject at once:
“What business has taken Killi to Uraiyur?”

“Between Tittan and Killi
there arose a war of ideas.
It created estrangement between father and son,
and that in turn gave their enemies an opening, mother!

1. When the white conch sounds,
when the victory drum is beaten,
when rows of lamps lit by spear-eyed women begin to glow,
and evening comes swiftly —
that is the evening of Veera Venman Veliyan Tittan,
said the poet Mudukootranar,
praising Tittan with flowers of verse dripping Tamil wine!

<!-- scan_page: 68 -->

Was that all?

2. Uraiyur —
where Tittan’s power, tireless in war,
flourished amid a land rich in dignity, distinction and prosperity,
with great elephant forces of small-eyed beasts —
so wrote the stately poet Nakkirar
in his palm-leaf record of Tittan!

He has a daughter named Aiyai —
your beloved Killi’s dear younger sister!
The great poet Paranar,
placing that Aiyai before him,
wrote songs of praise naming Tittan as her father.
If so,
is it easy to speak the full greatness of that maiden?

Hear that too!

3. Anklets filled with little pellets rolled and rang at her feet;
an ambal-flower garland lay upon her;
her beautiful forearms shone
with gold bangles cut and finished by the file;
adorned with pearl, coral and jewels,
her shoulders swelled like bamboo.
A maiden radiant with the treasure called chastity —
beautiful Aiyai!
Her father was the king Maaven Tittan,
whose generosity poured like rain!

<!-- scan_page: 69 -->

Many kings dreamed by daylight
of marrying the maiden whom Paranar praised.
Hearing that Narkilli and his father
were not on good terms,
and deciding that this was the right moment
and must not be allowed to slip away,
a Chera chieftain ruling the Bhavani river region,
a man called Palver Katti,
sent an envoy with a sealed palm-leaf letter
seeking to marry Tittan’s daughter — that sweet mango-fruit.

4. The letter said in writing:
‘After I give you many incomparable, radiant nine-gem jewels,
worthy of Uraiyur itself,
I shall take your daughter.’

Seeing this, Tittan replied:
‘Whatever you give, I will not take it.
I will not push my daughter upon a king called Katti.
This is my final word.
Go today itself and tell your lord!’

Hearing the Chola king’s words,
King Katti’s anger rose to his head.

<!-- scan_page: 70 -->

Together with the powerful warrior named Panan,
he is marching with an army upon Uraiyur!

The moment this news reached his ear,
young Narkilli vowed that,
as though all the tigers of the forest had taken one form,
Katti and Panan would be kicked back
before either could trample across the frontier.
With the war-drum sounding,
he has gone toward Uraiyur,” Sattanthai said.

“I too shall go in search of my god!”
Nakkannai cried,
and vanished from there
like the feather of a swan blown away in the monsoon wind.

Crossing Amur on the northern bank of the Kaveri,
when she entered the flower-filled suburban groves of Uraiyur,
her two eyes danced with wonder.

5. At the front of a little house
she saw the queen of poetry, Kavarpentu —
the nurse of classical Tamil — standing there.
Overflowing with longing,
Nakkannai ran to her,
clutched a pillar nearby,
and asked,
“Mother! Where is your son?”

Kavarpentu knew that this golden-moon Nakkannai
loved not only Tamil but her son as well.
She replied:
“I do not know where my son has gone!
But wherever he is,
he will surely have gone toward some battlefront!

<!-- scan_page: 71 -->

Though the exact place to which the tiger has gone is unknown,
this is the womb that bore the son
and sent him to the field as a foremost warrior —
like the mountain cave in which a tiger once lived, mother!”

Hearing Kavarpentu’s answer,
Nakkannai prepared to set out
toward the red battlefield.
But the mother stopped her
and ordered her to stay in her home.
Nakkannai did not refuse.

“Eat, rest and sleep.
Tomorrow after sunrise
I shall give you a palm-leaf message
and send you to Aiyai!
Even if Killi forgets the words of his own sister,
Nakkannai — I take responsibility
for making the marriage between Nakkannai and Narkilli happen!
Killi too may come to Aiyai’s palace.
Until then,
do not let your arms grow still thinner from longing
and make your bangles slip away!”

Embarrassed by Kavarpentu’s teasing,
Nakkannai pushed her bangles
all the way up toward her elbows!

### Source explanation

- `வெண்கோடு` — white / silver conch.
- `செல்லாநல்லிசை` — good fame that does not perish.
- `அரிபெய்` — filled with rattling pellets.
- `தொடலை` — a woven / strung garland.
- `அரம்போழ்` — cut and finished with a file.
- `பணை` — bamboo.
- `மாவண்` — possessing great generosity.
- `வெண்ணெல்வேலி` — having paddy fields growing white rice as a fence.
- `நன்கலம்` — ornament / jewel.
- `கல் அளை` — mountain cave.
