# One-Sided Love — 4

<!-- Reviewed English translation from the Tamil final-cleared canonical section. -->

<!-- scan_page: 31 -->

“People of Amur! Rise roaring to war!
Let the fortress drums of Mukkaval Nadu thunder!

The enemy plants its foot on our soil
beating its proclamation drum;
we shall beat the war-drum
and turn that enemy into a mound of dust!

A person takes form
inside the womb of a woman;
then takes the form of a grave
inside the womb of this earth!

A fulfilled life does not mean
living for a hundred years!
Even if one dies young,
if it is for a purpose —
for people of honour
there is no life more fulfilled than that!

A jewelled crown, a ruby throne,
a couch, caresses,
hundreds to serve,
all and sundry standing with folded hands and pleading —

all these are easy to obtain in this world!
By smiling obsequiously, bending low,
raising one man up and praising another,

calling one who knows no propriety
an incomparable lord;
calling one who has never learnt gracious speech
a teacher of the good path;

calling one who rejects culture
a thinker famed throughout the world;
calling one bent on revenge
a broad-hearted worthy;

<!-- scan_page: 32 -->

<!-- full-page illustration; no source caption -->

<!-- scan_page: 33 -->

calling one who values others lower than dogs
better even than a mother;

calling an unlettered fool
a great learned elder;
calling a wicked scoundrel
a good man for the whole earth;

by writing and speaking of virtues that do not exist,
everything can easily be obtained in this world!
But —

to hold that honour alone is the good life,
to embrace death and still smile on the battlefield,

to fall bearing the warrior’s wound upon the chest
and attain great fame —
that is a greatness hard indeed to win!

More than the plundered delight
of embracing and sporting with a maiden of one’s clan,
there is this:
to give our lives in battle
so that our maiden Earth does not become slave to the enemy,
and then to take a long sleep
upon her broad lap —
that is the enduring delight,
the path to something that lasts!

So that the history of the Tamil people
does not stagger,
rising and falling like waves of the sounding sea;
so that it spreads across the earth
like fragrance from a flowering grove,
there is one life-giving principle — honour!

Those who pawn that principle away somewhere
merely to preserve jewels and palaces —

<!-- scan_page: 34 -->

we shall call them *takkai men*;
we will not accept them as Tamils!

Therefore —

even if arrows come and pierce us
like the fine drizzle of the monsoon;
even if spears come leaping at us
like kendai fish in the sluices of the fields;

even if the brutal tusks
of enraged elephants,
faces armoured for war,
stab and tear us open;

cry out that death’s embrace
surpasses even the lips of a maiden!
Take up the conch of war and sound it!
Come forward and perform your share upon the field!”

Thus,
turning even banana trees
into diamond-hard teak,

and giving a backbone even to cowards,
Chola Narkilli finished his speech.
What a sight it was —
even worms seemed to become tigers
and march out in arms
to cleanse away the filth of the enemy!

Through the streets of Amur,
at the centre of the marching ranks,
Narkilli led like a crown-jewel.

<!-- scan_page: 35 -->

Beside his chariot,
Sattanthai sat in another.
Seeing the army gather,
fearing not even Yama,
Nakkannai buried her face
against Porkodi’s shoulder.
Joy at seeing the sacred form
of the man who had entered her heart,
and anguish that he was entering battle,
rose together within her and tossed her about.
Trying to keep brightness upon her face,
she stood with tears in her eyes and blessed them:
“May the armies of our country return in health!”

“Look there — Sattanthai!”
Porkodi cried, springing up
and pointing to a nearby chariot.
That was all it took.
In the blink of an eye,
Nakkannai flew forward faster than the wind
and stood before the poet’s chariot.

Understanding the maiden’s heart,
the great poet climbed down and said:
“Poet-maiden! You need have no worry.
Narkilli will oppose the foe,
wear the victory-vagai,
and return laughing aloud!”

“Do you truly say victory is certain?
I am sending my very life with you,” she said.

“In that one sentence
you have shown me the sculpture
you have shaped inside your heart,” said the poet.

Unable to raise the head she had lowered in modesty,
Nakkannai said:
“Our country’s victory needs your blessing.”

<!-- scan_page: 36 -->

“Not only the victory of the country;
I shall also labour
for the victory of the poem-palm-leaf
you gave Narkilli,” he said.

She placed her flower-hand
at his flower-feet in worship
and cried:
“May the Karantai battle be victorious!”

“What doubt can there be?” said Sattanthai,
and at once he fashioned a poem:

★

> “Greater than the roaring sea is his rising battle-noise;
> the cry of his elephant never ceases,
> like the thunder of monsoon rain.
> Who indeed are those pitiable ones —
> those who fall into the hands
> of the generous warrior
> wearing the densely fibre-strung athi garland?”

(*Purananuru*, poem 81  
Poet: Sattanthaiyar  
Praised hero: Chola Poravaikko Perunarkilli)

Hearing the great poet’s poem of certainty,
the flowering vine of a maiden
let a smile creep across her face.

Just then the young Killi came,
a slight smile held upon his face.
“What is this, poet — songs at the front of an army?” he asked.

“This mullai vine is troubled,
fearing that war may bring you harm,” said the poet.

“Perhaps trouble may come —
so is your advice that I should run away?” Killi asked.

<!-- scan_page: 37 -->

At Narkilli’s teasing anger,
Nakkannai fluttered in alarm,
yet answered with grace:

“In Tamil land,
a garland is placed upon the man
who embraces the bull and wins;
no woman will accept a man
who slips away and flees from battle!”

Killi rushed to snap back a sharp reply.
Before he could,
the poet stopped him:
“Enough of this play!”

“Would I not know Narkilli’s valour?
Here — listen to the meaning
of the poem I recited to Nakkannai!”

“The Chola prince wearing the athi garland
leads his army in ordered ranks;
the roar it raises as it moves
is said to surpass even the seven seas!

And if you ask about
the sound of his elephants upon the field —
it is the thunder of the monsoon!
If enemies fall into his hands in such a moment?
Ah! Who are those poor, pitiable ones?”

Not liking the poet’s praise,
he bowed his head.
Nakkannai, swollen with delighted pride,
lifted hers!

<!-- scan_page: 38 -->

Then came a voice:
“What is this, daughter?
Where have you come — here?
What business has a fly
in a place where iron is being forged?”

Like a chariot made of golden jewels,
mother Perundevi appeared;
scolding Nakkannai until her smile vanished,
she dragged her back into the house.

### Source explanation

- `கார்ப்பெயல் உருமின்` — the thunder-clap of the rainy season.
- `நார்ச் செறியத் தொடுத்த ஆர்கண்ணி` — the athi garland tightly strung with fibre.
- `அளியர்` — those worthy of pity.
- `கவிகை மள்ளன்` — the generous warrior.
