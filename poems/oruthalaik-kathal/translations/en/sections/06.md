# One-Sided Love — 6

<!-- Reviewed English translation from the Tamil final-cleared canonical section. -->

<!-- scan_page: 46 -->

Cutting clean through the long tail of a formidable enemy,
heaped with victory from a battle fought for two days,
with the earth of Mukkaval Nadu thundering to the drum
and trumpet-calls sounding through all eight directions,
“Tittan’s son is coming!” cried the people of Amur,
and poured out to welcome him.

There was no sand in the streets — only flowers!
There were no clouds in the sky — only banners!
Everyone who had remained inside their homes
ran out to see the man who had won victory for the land,
like rainwater rushing through a forest.

Could Nakkannai alone remain a parrot shut inside its cage?
She came racing out, asking, “Who has won?”
“Who else could it be?” cried Porkodi,
throwing her arms around her and spinning in circles.
The lovely girl staggered,
unable to bear the very weight of joy!

Keeping proud stride to the beat of the great drum,
upon a white horse that advanced with head held high,
Perunarkilli sat like a heroic painting
no brush could ever create.

<!-- scan_page: 47 -->

Because he had entered battle and fought without rest,
he seemed not to have had even time to shave;
so a little dark stubble had sprung upon his face,
giving him the look of a short beard —

and even that poured a new intoxication into Nakkannai’s eyes
and made every vein and nerve in her body play the music of love!
Along both sides of the streets of Amur,
at every doorway,
men and women stood with eyes spread wide,
waiting to see who among the sons of their homes
who had girded themselves and gone to battle
would return — what remnant would be left to come back.

A warrior came holding his sword aloft;
he bowed at the feet of his mother and father
and embraced the mate who was his very life.

For another warrior who had vanished on the field,
a garland was placed upon his sword
and the sword was given to his household,
quietly telling them what had happened.
Whether they were parents who had lost a son
or a wife whose auspicious married life had been shattered,
they mastered the storm within their hearts
and said, “The fame of our soil has risen.”
Only their eyes showed grief —
and Killi’s heart was wounded by the sight.

At one place,
children leapt with delight
at the tusks of the elephant their father had felled for them.

<!-- scan_page: 48 -->

<!-- full-page illustration; no source caption -->

<!-- scan_page: 49 -->

Elsewhere,
“Mother, why has father not come?”
cried little children with tender steps.

Still seated upon his horse,
Killi looked upon all this with a heart that groaned:
“Is a war that turns a country into a killing-ground
truly necessary?”
he asked the conscience that pricked him with guilt.

“But so long as the enemy calls the principle of peace
cowardice,
can the war-spirit of warriors rich in honour be changed?
When the foe raises a war-sword,
if we alone stand plucking flowers,
would not the blazing fire called valour
become nothing but damp charcoal?”

Questions such as these sprang at once
inside Killi’s heart, softened by pain.

The argument, “Let us stop the war,”
flew away in a single moment before it could stand.

“I love the war-sword —
I love the Tamil land that gave me birth!
If an enemy’s keen sword tears across my chest,
the blood it spills will be sandalwood giving fragrance!
I am no useless man who sends warriors into the field
and watches the spectacle from afar!
This battlefield of cruel slaughter

<!-- scan_page: 50 -->

I have made into a flower-garden in which I myself play!
I am one limb of the army
that roars like a lion at the battlefront!
I am one among them!
I shall bow to the warriors who fell and died in battle —
and,
saying that the path laid down by those men of duty
is my path too,
I shall plough the enemy’s bodies with my sword!”

With this vow taken in his heart,
he waved to the flood-like crowds whose cheers rose around him
and entered the pearl pavilion
where Mukkaval Nadu celebrated its victory.

The ruler of Amur embraced him warmly and said,
“Fearless-hearted one! You will create a golden age
in the Chola land!”

The poet Sattanthai came near, kissed the crown of his head,
and said,
“You will shine as diamond letters in history!”
Then the poet looked all around and asked,
“Where is Nakkannai? I still do not see her.”

Among the thousand gathered there,
Nakkannai sat at one end of the hall with her friend.

“Why are you searching for her, respected poet?
Tell me that first,”
said Perundevi,
the mother given to the lotus-eyed Nakkannai.

<!-- scan_page: 51 -->

“If you ask perversely why the poet would call her,
you will only make me ashamed and confuse everything,”
Perungozhinaykan cut in,
settling Perundevi Nachiyar down and silencing her.

“Because Nakkannai is a fine queen among Tamil poets,
I thought that, with a heart full of affection,
she would offer a marvellous poem
praising the victory Narkilli has won.
That is why I called her,” said the poet.

“Why still be shy now, girl?
Before that young bull who bears a shield,
O fish-eyed one, if you stand and recite a song of praise,
it will become a puram poem that outlives time!
Go into the middle of the assembly without hesitation.
Your heart’s captor will not fail to be moved
when he hears your song!”

So Porkodi gave her courage.
The words that Nakkannai, peacock-like, then poured into her friend’s ear
truly became, just as Porkodi had said,
a puram poem.

1) “Seeing the young bull
with a dark short beard like kohl
and warrior anklets upon his feet,
I fear that my bangles will slip from my wrists of their own accord
and betray me to the mother who bore me as a maiden!

<!-- scan_page: 52 -->

I ache to embrace the shoulders of Killi,
who conquers fierce enemies,
like a parrot longing for a fruit-laden tree.
Yet when I think of the learned gathered in this assembly,
shyness comes and stops this Nakkannai, my friend!”

Her friend interrupted:
“All this wisdom should have been there at the beginning!
What use is fear now, after you have climbed half the palmyra?
Only by climbing the rest can you pluck the fruit and taste it!
Do not climb down on some pretext —
and then go wandering about making a show of it afterward!”

Hearing her friend’s words,
Nakkannai rose with courage,
came forward and stood saluting the assembly.

She drew forth Tamil like a pearl from the deep sea,
offered greetings to the ruler of Amur,

bowed at the feet of the learned elder Sattanthai
and received his blessing,

then looked with delight through fish-like dancing eyes
upon the prince of Uraiyur who had defeated the enemy.

Her song was at once
an example of his fearless heart
and an akam poem of her own inward longing for him.

As those gathered for the victory celebration marvelled and praised,

<!-- scan_page: 53 -->

only her mother watched with knitted brow
and eyes wide open while she sang!

2) “There are beasts called Pani
that hunt and conquer even the striped tiger,
whose prey, struck by its mighty paw,
knows only how to fall to the right and never to the left;
those beasts kill mountain-like elephants
and break their tusks.
Through ranges thick with cool, fragrant groves,
my war-leader will come alone
with only one keen spear for company —
and he does not fear!
But I, separated from him,
suffer from the sickness called separation!
With my eyes shedding drops like dew,
how am I now to console myself and endure, my friend?”

When Nakkannai finished singing,
she trembled and looked toward her mother.

“Why fear when I am here, maiden?”
said the resplendent poet,
turning to the assembly.

“This chaste lamp of her lineage —
her father, the prosperous Perungozhinaykan,
has come forward to offer her before you
as the reward for Prince Narkilli’s victory!
Whether the girl herself desires it, I cannot say!
Her father has agreed without a word of refusal.
Only the mother wonders whether such a thing can happen.
Therefore,

<!-- scan_page: 54 -->

there is no one to stand in the way
of placing this Tamil flower
upon Killi’s broad shoulders!”

“There is an obstacle!”
Narkilli thundered, rising in shock,
the warrior who had returned victorious with his army.

“I love the war-sword —
not the flower-petal!
No! No — never, never!”

And he walked out of the assembly.

Before the scream her mother raised could pierce her ears,
the world had spun twice around Nakkannai;
her heart shattered,
and she collapsed upon the floor.

### Source explanation

1)
- `மை அணல்` — a beard dark like kohl.
- `தொடி` — bangle.
- `கழித்திடுதல்` — slipping from the hand and falling away.
- `யாய்` — mother.
- `முயங்கல்` — embracing.

2)
- `வலம்படு` — powerful.
- `வாள்வரி` — tiger.
- `புகல்வந்து ஆளி` — the beast called yali that comes desiring victory.
- `ஒற்றி` — striking and killing.
