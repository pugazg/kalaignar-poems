# One-Sided Love — 10

<!-- Reviewed English translation from the Tamil final-cleared canonical section. -->

<!-- scan_page: 83 -->

Kavarpentu, the foster-mother who had raised him
with a tenderness greater even than that of the mother
who carried and bore him for ten months,
was now beating her wings like a bird
inside the enemy camp’s cage.
Knowing nothing of the danger to that loving mother,
Narkilli entered Aiyai’s gold-roofed palace to see his sister.
The moment he saw Nakkannai there,
he stopped in shock.

“Brother, welcome!” cried good Aiyai.
“Look at the queen of poetry
who regards you as her very eye and life!”

“A king named Katti, lord of a realm,
has laid siege in order to take you as his bride!
The people of Uraiyur, their hearts wounded,
are all setting out for war.
Tell Nakkannai to write a song by tomorrow morning,
a battle-tune for the soldiers!” Killi said.

Nakkannai fell at his feet and wept:
“Have you decided that my life may turn to dust?
Was it for this that I was born a woman?”

<!-- scan_page: 85 -->

“I came to see my sister.
Must I suffer here too,
hearing the tale of your lovesickness?
Aiyai, I am leaving.
At daybreak tomorrow I go to the battlefront.
Nakkannai is a fine Tamil poet-queen.
Give her a purse of gold as a gift
and send her on her way yourself!”

As he moved away after saying this,
Nakkannai stepped before him:

“I am the daughter born to a wealthy merchant!
I did not come here to make a living by writing Tamil
because you would give me a gift!
Son of a crowned king,
I remind you once more of the vow
I declared before:
If I live, I shall live as your wife;
if I fall, I shall fall upon the point of your sword.
Know this to be certain!”

“I declare once again with certainty:
neither will happen!” Killi replied,
flinging those bitter words at her as he left.

Have you ever seen a creeper that does not sway in a storm?
No falsehood here — behold two creepers
that have gone utterly still in the gale!

1. By Tittan’s command,
the drum of the day-court thundered,
and its commotion spread to every direction.
Panan and Katti trembled in astonishment.

<!-- scan_page: 86 -->

The enemies who had come leaping in,
expecting to conquer Uraiyur while Killi was away,
now decided it was enough merely to pull their cloth free of the thorn.

“Who wants that dark monkey-girl Aiyai,
that kalli-cactus?” they said.
Without even coming to battle,
they shut up shop and ran,
like the fox that grimaces and declares,
“This fruit is sour!”

If they entered the field,
they would have to show their backs;
afterward they would have to shrink in shame.
“Let only Kavarpentu remain in our custody.
Let infantry, cavalry, elephants and chariots —
all four divisions — turn back,”
ordered Katti and Panan.
Thinking, “The pot is burnt — let it go!”
the fourfold army hurried home,
like young crabs racing back toward their holes.

When this good news reached the palace at Uraiyur,
good Aiyai embraced Nakkannai and said:
“The two venomous snakes that came with an army
could not stand before our Killi,
a mountain rushing upon them,
and have wriggled away!
Turn that event into a poem!”

“I would rather die this very hour by your brother’s hand!
I am waiting for the foster-mother Kavarpentu to return.

<!-- scan_page: 87 -->

She said, ‘I myself will stand in front
and conduct the wedding.’
Trusting that,
I am still alive!
If my love is fulfilled sweetly as I have imagined
and I gain the fortune of becoming your sister-in-law,
I shall heap up countless poems —
moonlight, breeze, sweet syrup, honey-rain and more!”

Just then,
a companion came running in, gasping for breath,
and told news that stole the very breath
from Perungozhinaykan’s daughter:

“Those two villains Katti and Panan
have dragged our Kavarpentu away in a prison-cage.
Tomorrow Narkilli goes to the battlefront
to rescue the foster-mother!”

Because this sorrowful news followed the joyful news,
both women forgot their evening meal
and went to separate rooms to sleep.

♦ ♦ ♦

“I shall besiege the cities of Panan and Katti in the morning
and rescue mother Kavarpentu,” Narkilli had vowed.
Unable to sleep at midnight,
he tossed and turned.
When he heard a knock at the door,
he muttered,
“I cannot bear this Nakkannai’s trouble,”

<!-- scan_page: 88 -->

rose reluctantly,
and opened the outer door of his room.

“Has mother come back?” he cried in astonishment.
Yes — Kavarpentu stood before him!

“My son Narkilli!
The two poets Mazhuvanar and Kalladanar
went to meet King Katti and his companion Panan and said:

‘Even on a battlefield where enemies’ lives are taken,
there are rules that guard civilized conduct!
You went to seize the princess of Uraiyur;
you fled when you heard that Narkilli had arrived!
Realizing that the enemy’s strength had grown,
your decision was wise.

But what justice is there
in arresting one of our Tamil poets?
Release Kavarpentu without even a moment’s delay!
If you do not issue such an order at once,
then put a full stop here and now:
from this day, Kalladanar and Mazhuvanar
have no bond with you!’

When the two poets spoke in such anger,
they released me without a moment’s hesitation.
My noble son! I ran straight here to see you.
Nakkannai, who came here to become my daughter-in-law —
where is she?”

“Mother, forgive this servant of yours.
If I marry her,

<!-- scan_page: 89 -->

Tamil may lose many literary ornaments
that ought to have been created!
For Tamil honour and the broad-shouldered valour
of the Tamil people to rise still higher,
she must compose nectar-like poems.
If instead I shut that singing bird
inside the prison of the inner palace,
what benefit — even an atom’s worth —
would Tamil receive?” he asked.

“Who said household life
would obstruct the forging of literature in Tamil?
Would marriage itself refuse her
from heaping up unfading Tamil poems
like fruit crowding a mango grove?
Does pomegranate juice turn bitter
because mountain honey is mixed with it?

Son, listen.
Do not refuse my request — accept it!” she said.

“Do not think I am refusing you, mother!
I do not hate Nakkannai. But —
I love the war-sword.
Not the flower-petal! No! No! Never!” he said.

“The difference is clear, son.
Had I been the true mother who bore you,
as the townspeople say,
you would have been a son who never rejected his mother’s word!

<!-- scan_page: 90 -->

Kavarpentu is only the foster-mother
who raised this child.
That is why you disregard me.
Dear prince, I am leaving,” she said and moved away.

“Mother, you have hurled a spear into my heart!
I will no longer cling, monitor-lizard-like,
to the stubborn hold I have taken.
You are the mother who bore me.
To prove it,
tomorrow morning I myself shall go to Nakkannai
and announce a good decision.”

Kavarpentu kissed the crown of Killi’s head and blessed him.
“It is midnight now, so I shall return tomorrow.
Together we shall see Nakkannai
and tell her the happy news,” she said,
wiping the tears of joy welling in her eyes,
and returned home.

♦ ♦ ♦

Thinking of the victory of Nakkannai’s pure love,
Narkilli closed his eyes and sank into delight.
After a little while,
the whole sea-girt world became her.
He travelled through a dream-world of imagination
and flew beside his love-bird as her equal!

<!-- scan_page: 91 -->

Fear, modesty, innocence and reserve
kept her swaying just beyond his reach.
With an “ich!” of a kiss he altered her senses,
pulled her close and laid her upon the soft bed.
Two bodies — sacred bodies —
one dark body, his;
the other, mango-gold,
the magical body of the maiden.
Two vessels of nectar pressed deep against his chest,
and her water-lily eyes quivered —
pleasure! pleasure!

The fall of dream-frost was scattered;
the sun-ray of waking memory rose.
Killi opened his eyes,
his face spilling delight,
and threw one leg up over the pillow.

As he lay there on the soft bed in ecstasy,
a rolled palm-leaf message flew in and fell upon him.
He picked it up and read.
Killi sprang up, trembling.

“Tonight Princess Aiyai
will be carried away captive from here.”

Seeing those words on the leaf,
Killi laughed aloud.
It seemed that Katti and Panan, who had fled and hidden,
still had not cured the itch in their bodies!
“To protect my own sister Aiyai,

<!-- scan_page: 92 -->

this sword at my side is enough!” he roared,
and set out like the fire of cosmic destruction
toward the palace where his sister slept.

> in the day-court of Tittan Veliyan’s Uranthai;
> unable to fight,
> he fled from the clash
> with mighty Panan...”

(*Akananuru*, poem 226  
Poet: Paranar)

### Source explanation

- `வலிமிகும் முன்பின் பாணன்` — Panan, possessing great strength and fighting power.
- `தார்மலி தித்தன்` — Tittan, richly adorned with garlands of victory.
- `பாடுஇன் தெண்கிணைப் பாடு` — the sound of the skin-instrument raising a joyful resonance.
- `நாளவை` — the king’s Attani council hall / day-court.
- `போரடு தானைக் கட்டி` — King Katti, possessing powerful forces able to defeat enemies in war.
- `பொராஅது` — without fighting.
