# One-Sided Love — 7

<!-- Reviewed English translation from the Tamil final-cleared canonical section. -->

<!-- scan_page: 56 -->

As her eyelids slowly opened
and the dark pupils of her eyes blossomed,
Nakkannai realized that she was lying
on the bed in her own home.
Like sunlight hidden inside the tresses of a cloud,
she sent the light of her eyes
out from the depths of sorrow,
looked closely at Porkodi bending beside her,
and asked,
“Where are my mother and father?”

“Your mother sprang about with a great *tham-thoom*,
as though her head had split,
scolding your father for having put a price upon our honour.
She declared that not even another moment
could be spent in this town,
and, boiling with anger,
set out for Poompuhar!

Your father, crushed by the humiliation here
and saying it had made him bow his head,
followed her —
to piece together the heart
of the woman who had rushed away broken.”

Hearing Porkodi’s news,
Nakkannai raised her bow-like brows and said,
“Why should I alone go on living in vain?”

“To keep alive the Tamil language,
dearer than life itself,
people like you must live for a thousand years!”

<!-- scan_page: 57 -->

At the sound of that voice,
the slender maiden started in surprise.
When she saw that the owner of the voice was Narkilli,
her beautiful eyelids fluttered,
as though asking whether the sight before her could truly be real.
“What world am I in?” she wondered,
unable even to trust her own eyes.
Thinking, “What work is left for the two of them
in this world now?”
Porkodi quietly moved away and left them alone.

And Nakkannai’s state?
In all four directions, everything became a hall of music!
“Has compassion been born — what is this?”
her eyes rose and asked.
“Has tenderness welled toward me?”
her lips throbbed and trembled to ask.

“Son of a king who will wear a crown upon his head!
I stand here carrying two crowns upon my breasts —
why have you forgotten even the thought of a crown?”
The slender-waisted maiden did not ask it aloud.
It was a silent question hidden in her smile.

Though he saw the fine-bangled woman
standing there unsteady,
without trying to calm the turbulence of that wave-filled sea,
he asked,
“Do you understand why I have come?”

She said:

“Honey is sweet; a scorpion’s poison burns!
Tell me — which of those two
have you come to give me?

<!-- scan_page: 59 -->

For the farmer who looks to the sky in longing,
will the reward be only a dry wind?
Or will it be the nectar-drink of rain that brings abundance?
Will the bent crescent grow into a full moon?
Or is pitch darkness the fate written for my life?”

He said:

“I may gladly ask you, maiden,
for Tamil that is like mango juice.
I have no desire for a body like a mango!
I do have a heart that honours
your mastery of great Tamil.”

She said:

“Thank you greatly for praising that talent.
But is it right to set fire
to the love of the woman who bears that talent?”

He said:

“The exchange of hearts from both sides
is the grammar of love!
Can that same grammar apply
to desire that comes from only one side?”

She said:

“Is it not a sin
to show no tenderness to the mullai vine before you,
but instead to trouble it and cast it aside?”

He said:

“King Pari’s chariot was useful
for a mullai creeper to spread upon —

<!-- scan_page: 60 -->

but did that wooden chariot ever breathe in
and delight in the fragrance of the flowers it bore?
If saying you are a maiden means
that you are a vine that must spread,
what use is it if I am merely Pari’s wooden chariot?”

She said:

“You are the son of a king who rules a country —
not some great sage who rules the forest!”

He said:

“Even great sages have fallen into women’s laps,
and secretly learnt the arts of Madan!
I am a king’s son — that is true.
I have desired to bend the land to my will;
I have never desired to bend a woman’s waist!”

She said:

“My lord, who says he sleeps only with his sword in its sheath!
Hear the vow I make:
If I live, I shall live as your wife!
If I fall, I shall fall by the point of your sword!”

He said:

“O poet-maiden who thunders like the monsoon sky —
neither of the two things you have said will happen!
I love the war-sword,
not the flower-petal! No! This too is certain!

<!-- scan_page: 61 -->

And just as surely,
I will not take your life with that sword.
I am leaving.
Forgive me,
and send me on my way!”

Having said this,
without waiting for the beautiful-eyed Nakkannai to answer,
he hurried outside,
climbed into the chariot standing beside the house,
and sat down.

The slender-waisted maiden followed,
grasped one of the chariot posts,
and cried:
“Must this young vine,
leaping to twine itself around you and embrace you,
be left to wither?”

“I must go urgently to Uraiyur today!
I shall return here in a week or two!
There is no time to speak with you now.
Then we can open our hearts
and speak fully,” he said.

Realizing that he was brushing her aside,
Nakkannai wept against the chariot and said,
“My lord! Hear the song
that surges and sounds within this heart
which performs its penance for you.”
And she sang:

★

“Screw-pines crowded with thorn-pierced leaves,
like long horns upon a shark’s face,
rough as the body of a prawn;
their buds ripened like the tusks of old elephants,
their heads drooping like the females among deer,
spreading fragrance and delight
across festival grounds by the sea —
O chieftain of the warrior clan
who plants the flag of victory even there!

Let your charioteer swiftly drive
your long chariot whose bells dance and ring;
yet you tell me,
‘I shall return after a few days!’
Know this:
if you leave me,
this woman cannot live even a single day!”

Through Nakkannai’s sorrowful music,
the chariot beside the house began to move.
At Narkilli’s command,
the horses broke into a four-footed gallop,
flew down the middle of the street,
and vanished in a moment!

Ignoring the dust thrown up by the horses,
she ran behind it.
Porkodi came and tried to stop her — in vain.
She kept running,
her anklets sounding out her lament!

★

> “Broad-based screw-pines,
> rough like the back of a prawn,
> their thorn-leaves like a shark’s horn,
> their buds ripened like the tusk of a great elephant,
> appearing apart like the fine female deer,
> fragrance filling festival grounds by the mighty sea —
> O lord of that shore!
> If your charioteer sets your long jewelled chariot in motion
> and you go away,
> then know this well:
> though you say you will return in a few days,
> she cannot live those days.”

(*Natrinai*, poem 19  
Poet: Nakkannaiyar)

### Source explanation

- `இறவு` — prawn.
- `சுறவு` — shark.
- `பிணர்` — roughness / coarse texture.
- `மருப்பு` — elephant tusk.
- `நன்மான் உழை` — fine female deer.
- `உரவுநீர்` — sea.
- `செலீஇய சேறிஆயின்` — if you go after having the charioteer drive the chariot.
