# Three Letters — Thoughts Three Times Three

<!-- scan 346 -->

### Three Letters —
### Thoughts Three Times Three

At the Kalaivanar Arangam in Madras,
as part of the Five Great Celebrations,
a poetry assembly was held under the theme
“Three Letters, Thoughts Three Times Three.”
This is the presiding poem sung there
by Chief Minister Kalaignar.

★

A great celebration — a sacred celebration;
for us, one celebration is the Three Great Celebration!
This year, two more celebrations
have joined it,
and it has become a Five Great Celebration!

Ayya,
Anna,
the birthday celebration of the Kazhagam;
the centenaries of
Ambedkar
and Paavendhar;
the Diamond Jubilee
marking the rise of the Dravidian movement;
and a magnificent victory celebration
because the Union government
has accepted the Mandal Commission recommendation!

<!-- scan 347 -->

A sacred celebration too,
to offer our heartfelt thanks
to the humanitarian V. P. Singh!
My greetings to all
who have come to see the festival.

To the lion of the knowledge movement,
our Professor of ethnic self-respect;
to my life-dear younger brother Nanjilar;
to dear friend Anbil;
to Mannai and Union Minister Maran;
to the honourable ministers;
to our Assembly Speaker Tamilkudimagan;
to the new poet T. R. Baalu;
and to all the old poets —
my greetings!

“Three letters,
thoughts three times three” —
thus they have arranged the poetry assembly.
And as the head sits
above the neck,
they have made me sit
at its head.

Can a head alone function,
without broad shoulders, arms, legs, trunk,
eyes, ears, nose, mouth, stomach,
heart or lungs?
It cannot.
Therefore none of these limbs
becomes arrogant.
They work with grace,
knowing the old saying
that for the eight-span body
the head is principal.

<!-- scan 348 -->

My limbs!
My beloved treasures!
My siblings dearer to me than life!
Is what I say not true?
Like a god who incarnates declaring,
“I alone am the chief organ,”
the head has never grown arrogant.
This head has never grown arrogant,
nor shifted from its position.
Therefore I see the Kazhagam
in the sight of neck to foot
bearing the head,
doing duty with dignity
and guarding discipline;
I rejoice,
bow my head,
and salute you.

The same blood that flows to the head
flows to the feet.
Yet if *varna-ashrama dharma* declares
that those born from Brahma’s head
are the high caste,
those born from his thighs are Vaishyas,
and we who came from his feet
are the lowest caste —
why is there such disorder
only in Brahma’s blood circulation?

What is Brahma — male or female?
Even if we suppose Brahma female,
did she stitch wombs
all over her body
in order to give birth?

<!-- scan 349 -->

Even lies need a limit.
We must know
that wicked Puranic tales are absurd.

If we say all this,
they warn us:
God will grow angry,
curse you,
and give you only a short life!
Yet Ayya Periyar,
who exposed this matter
far more sharply than we do,
lived to ninety-five!
Until he went to the grave
he roared:
“Let blind custom
be buried in the earth!”
This is Periyar’s birthday!

He gave rationalist thought
like solid refined gold.
Anna did not lock it in a box;
he made it into ornaments
and adorned the Tamils with it.
Periyar prepared
Self-Respect medicine —
a bitter medicine,
but one that saves life.
Anna mixed it into fruit,
dipped it in Tamil honey,
and gave it gently
to cure the disease.

Broad-browed king of knowledge Anna,
when the Kazhagam held
its first State Conference in Madras
in the year fifty-two,
when I spoke to second his selection
as conference president —

<!-- scan 350 -->

it was then that the magic
inside three letters
shone like the full moon
breaking through clouds!

On Tamil land encircled by three seas,
we are descendants of the three crowned kings
who beat three drums, raised three flags,
and nurtured the threefold Tamil,
the three fruits of our language.
Our language is `தமிழ்` — three letters!
The distinct `பண்பு` of Tamils — three letters!
From character rises `அன்பு` — three letters!
From love grows `காதல்` — three letters!
What lovers desire, `வீரம்` — three letters!
The `களம்` where warriors bear honourable wounds — three letters!
The `வெற்றி` seen on the field — three letters!
The seed of victory, `அறிவு` — three letters!
The treasury of that knowledge,
`அண்ணா` — three letters!
And the Kazhagam founded by that Anna,
`தி.மு.க.` — three letters!

Only after I spoke in this way that day
did the song come:
“In three letters there is a breath.”
Today, three letters
and thoughts three times three
have come into this poetry arena!

From ancient days,
as slaves, as servants,
as riding horses
for those who held domination —

<!-- scan 351 -->

the oppressed suffered,
wax thrown into flame,
writhing in agony.
To change that condition,
one rose like a blazing sun —
that worthy scholar
was named Ambedkar!

“What is caste? What is religion?
O humans who preach caste and religion!
If a thousand differences
in God’s creation
are truly his doing,
come — let us gouge out
that God’s eyes!”
Thus he protected people
who were dying like worms,
raised a battle flag
so they might rise again,
and declared:
“You no longer have the right
to suppress and rule us!
Only a separate electorate
can change our condition.”
He stormed like a cyclone,
shook like an earthquake,
and made revolution —
a new Buddha!

Combining the thoughts
of Ambedkar, Periyar and Anna,
Bharathidasan gave poems
as the smile of beauty
and the heat of flame;
a poet who dipped his spear
in volcanic lava
and forged poems of ideals!
His song makes us understand
the true meaning of the word “revolution.”

<!-- scan 352 -->

His similes ride the breeze in procession;
his truths spread wide
and pour a flood of light.
He is a son of Tamil —
he blossomed as Tamil itself!
Verses equal to nectar,
every one a flower rich with pollen!

(Here, in *The Smile of Beauty*,
the Revolutionary Poet sings of pigeons.)

> “Like a lotus that has opened
> and spread its petals,
> pigeons gather in a circle
> and eat their food.
> In their life there is no cutting,
> no stabbing;
> though each is distinct,
> there is no bondage,
> no blind custom of high and low.”

(Paavendhar speaks of the pigeons’ conduct.)

> “She is alone, without an equal;
> he commits a wrong
> that causes her pain.”
> Such an unworthy act,
> a few wayward pigeons
> among those where love dwells
> may perhaps have learned
> from sorrow-laden humans.

Who other than this poet
could call even darkness beautiful?
Tell me after you look!

(Wherever darkness exists, he says,
beauty would not be complete without it.)

<!-- scan 353 -->

> “At either side
> of the lofty beautiful nose you dwell;
> at the edge of a maiden’s fish-like eyes you are;
> in the middle of the ear
> and around it you spread;
> on a woman’s face
you make the marks stand out —
> O darkness, painters know your excellence.”

(He compares darkness with ignorance,
and argues: ignorance can produce knowledge,
but can knowledge produce ignorance?)

> “Knowledge is light — yes, yes!
> Ignorance is darkness — yes, yes!
> Ignorance can make knowledge;
> can ignorance be born from knowledge?”

(And an incident to explain it.)

> “A scorpion stung a boy;
> in midnight darkness a lamp was needed.
> They searched for a match
> and could not find one.
> Someone said it was in a box;
> there was no key.
> By then eight people
> had been stung by the scorpion
> and writhed in pain.
> ‘Cleanliness is compulsory’ —
> such knowledge you gave us,
> good darkness,
> how great is your glory!”

<!-- scan 354 -->

(Paavendhar sang of pigeons and darkness
with graceful simile and humour;
now look — as the Revolutionary Poet
he blows the conch of battle.)

> “Having scorched Tamil life in the north,
> the enemy came south
> intending harm.
> From that very day,
> O Tamil that guards justice,
> you said, ‘Subdue him!’
> Though there are stumbling stones here,
> you laughed: ‘We have broad shoulders!’”

To prove that Paavendhar’s song
has not gone false,
we have shown the whole nation
that in the far southern corner
the illusion and trickery
against the Mandal Commission
did not work.
We shall announce it
to the whole world too!

At a wedding house,
a banana tree,
not knowing it has been cut and tied there,
welcomes those who arrive.
So too many backward people in the north
remain unaware of their own condition.
That is why opposition to Mandal
is greater in those states;
but here the enslaved crowd
that stretches out its hand for *sundal*
and gathers around is very small!

<!-- scan 355 -->

Why is that?
Here is the explanation.

Seventy-five years ago
an organisation arose —
the great Dravidian movement
founded by the elder Sir Pitti Thyagaraya!
If a field is ploughed and prepared,
planted properly and guarded,
there is no need to weep at harvest.
From that day until this,
this is soil that has been seasoned,
land that has ripened —
wealth accumulated by the Dravidian movement.
If paddy is sown here,
grass will not accidentally grow instead!
In education and employment,
reservation on a social basis
is the noble demand of our movement.
That is why all the cries
from the citadels of domination
subside like a snake
that sees a stick.
If little foxes raise their tails
and call, “Come, let us fight,”
will lion-bulls run away
and hide in cave-cracks?
If mice dance here,
will tigers beat the rhythm for them?

<!-- scan 356 -->

The clamour of *alis*
will go only to others like themselves —
how can it defeat those men?

Golden Tamil given by the Sangam,
the Bay of Bengal swelling,
the young lion’s roar rising everywhere —
an people who lived saying,
“One is the clan”;
a people who from ancient days
gave and received;
then came a crowd to eat and grow fat,
tied up the tuft,
and ruined the order.
One caste — the Tamil caste —
became many castes;
therefore it became weak!

Intrigue won,
fraud succeeded;
“There will be no fall for us now,”
sang the worthless,
and danced in frenzy.
In learning,
in education,
in government employment,
a section of people hid away places,
claimed all as their own,
and tormented others,
seizing their lives.
Because we hesitated to fight them,
we once lay inert
like sculpted statues.

<!-- scan 357 -->

Then a day came
like daylight pushing away walking darkness;
the evil planet moved away.
“Only reservation
can set aside a share
for those who have been pushed aside” —
thus, swimming against the current,
the Dravidian movement rose!
It set out to cure
the South’s stupor.

Like clear nectar impossible to find,
like sweet musical song,
like honey syrup mixed with millet flour,
a feast whose taste never cloys,
like moonlight showing direction
to people who had none —
the good fortune that came unasked,
a great jewel:
communal proportional representation —
reservation —
a boon for the life of the backward!

<!-- scan 358 -->

Shall we lose what has come?
Shall we fall into a treacherous net?
People who eat what is cooked
and babble whatever comes to their mouths
write and speak of “merit” and “ability,”
subtly mixing poison,
confusing the minds of the people.
Paavendhar asked,
“If the knot is loosened beautifully,
will anyone simply let it go?”
I ask the same!

If all are equal in God’s creation,
did he make a brain of gold
only for the high caste
and place it in their skulls?
What sits inside the heads
of all other castes?
Clay? Lime?

Valmiki and Vyasa,
who gave poetry in the northern tongue
like the Ganges —
were they not children
born from Brahma’s feet?
Who is Valluvar? Who is Kamban?
Who are Ilango Adigal and Ottakoothar?
Were they born from Brahma’s head?
No!
Yet they are Himalayas of the Tamil language!

<!-- scan 359 -->

In the forty-three years
of independence we have had,
what exactly has been torn apart
by the opportunities given
to “merit” and “ability”?
What has been stitched?
What is this great thing
that has been torn and stitched — what, what, what?
We have never asked
why ability is needed
or whether ability itself is unnecessary!

But no scripture permitted Ekalavya
to learn the art.
Because he might defeat Vijaya — Arjuna —
with the bow,
what justice was there
in demanding his thumb as an offering?
Shambuka, the Sudra, performed penance;
“what qualification has he?” they raged,
and cut off his head —
is that not a story in the history
of Lord Rama?
If anyone today asks
for a thumb or a head
as such an offering,
his hide will be stripped;
his corpse-log will burn in the cremation ground!

Do you feel like asking:
“What is this unprecedented heat
inside a presiding poem?”
What else should there be?
When the very principal itself is in danger,
is it right to lie like a rabbit or tortoise?
When a man who has fallen
a thousand feet into a pit —

<!-- scan 360 -->

is being lifted by the hand toward safety,
if worthless men try to roll
a heavy boulder onto his head,
we must kick them!
We must chase them until they run!
Death may come at six or at a hundred;
let whatever happens happen —
even if this government itself must go!

Are we people who cannot live without a crown?
Were we born as infants
already seated on a throne,
as Sita was said to be born with a bow?
Royal umbrella or principle —
if you ask which we want,
we shall say:
“If you want someone to sell principle to survive,
find another person.”
Government comes and goes;
it is not permanent.
The principle laid down by Ayya and Anna
will not go — it lives as permanence.
Therefore, whether in office or out,
we shall value self-respect as life
and always guard Tamil ethnic dignity.
When he saw creatures abusing
the goal they had embraced,
Periyar would say in anger,
“Get lost, onion!”
Paavendhar would say:
“In prosperous great Tamil Nadu,
if non-Tamils stretch out their tails,
what they will receive is a kick.”

<!-- scan 361 -->

If he saw poisonous speakers
and obscene writers,
our Anna would mock them:
“beauties of an unshaking tongue,
beauties of a dung-like prose style!”
Unable to contain his anger,
Ambedkar himself once thundered
that if necessary
even the Constitution should be burned!

No generation will again
see leaders like these.
So that the thoughts of those leaders
may triumph,
let us take our oath!
Let us lift our radiant faces!
However many hardships come,
let us stand against them!
Let us see victory!

## Translator notes

- The contents witness encloses the Tamil title in quotation marks; the canonical verified opening does not. The English displayed title follows the canonical witness.
- The “three letters” sequence depends on Tamil grapheme/word forms (`தமிழ்`, `பண்பு`, `அன்பு`, `காதல்`, `வீரம்`, `களம்`, `வெற்றி`, `அறிவு`, `அண்ணா`, `தி.மு.க.`). Those Tamil tokens are retained because English letter counts would fabricate a different device.
- Embedded Bharathidasan passages are translated from this anthology witness only; no outside poetic edition is imported.
- The Mandal Commission, communal proportional representation/reservation, Dravidian-movement history, Ekalavya and Shambuka passages are translated as Kalaignar’s source-framed political argument, not as a separate historical adjudication.
- `அலி` is a source-visible gendered polemical insult. The transliterated plural *alis* is retained rather than replacing it with a modern identity label or silently softening the rhetoric.
- The closing Periyar, Bharathidasan, Anna and Ambedkar quotations/recollections remain attributed as the poem presents them.
