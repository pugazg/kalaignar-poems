# Love or Valour?

<!-- scan 101 -->

### Love or Valour?

A poetic debate held in Salem on 4 August 1968
on the theme “In Tamil life, which is greater — love or valour?”
Kalaignar M. Karunanidhi's presiding poetic introduction.

What comes to a field that the plough does not reach?
Grass and weeds.
If the plough comes, paddy wealth comes.
What comes when clouds do not arrive in the sky?
Severe famine.
If clouds come, the fruit of delight comes.
What comes from the “Ayan” hill when the Chera does not come?
What comes from Servarayan hill? Iron comes.
If all come together, stability comes;
if all join, this good state comes.
Stability comes.
A chariot moves when many pull it; likewise
honey-iron comes here in Salem.

<!-- scan 102 -->

Whenever I come to this town I wonder:
will the iron here never melt?
Before a demand dries into a fallen leaf,
let us show the swelling support that asks the government to fulfil it.
I came to speak of the wealth of the country and city,
its forests and fruits;
I have turned back toward honey-iron.
It is not because I forgot!

Honey means love;
iron means valour.
Do you understand my course now?
In Salem, filled with people who weave,
poets whose trade is weaving poetry have come
to sing the Tamil way of life that divided land
as neithal, kurinji, mullai, marutham and paalai.

“When will rain fall at Marakkara?”
“Irrigation must be carried out without fail.”
“Mettur must reach fullness.”
I wander with these as my aim;
yet they told me to harvest poetry too.

Gathering poets who conquer time through thought,
to draw the form of poetry,
Ganapathi, head of the Salem Tamil Manram,
welcomes with fragrant Tamil words this quarrel:
Is the valour that fights with a spear-like hand greater,
or the love that fights with spear-like eyes?

<!-- scan 103 -->

One side will say the love given by a fish-eyed woman is greatest;
the other will thunder that valour is the crown of worldly fame.
I am the chairman between the two.
Shall I build a bridge between them or kindle a fire of enmity?
You will say, build the bridge; you will ask for roads —
because I became minister for highways!
But if I build here at the beginning
a bridge between poets dripping Tamil honey,
there will be no path for ideas to leap like deer.
The organ in our head that does not sleep even when this flesh sleeps
is called the brain!
Many in this world do not have it.
Some who have it have only a sesame-seed's worth,
and even that is full of liquor — so they scribble as they please.
These poets, however,
have heads full of brain and have come to this assembly without losing balance.
I will not build a bridge and stop the Tamil battle between them.
When hostility rises among poets, Tamil gains novelty;
so I sow the seed of quarrel among them!
Let us wait and see who wins.

One poet sang, “Jasmine, a white conch on which the bee blows...”

<!-- scan 104 -->

“Not so, not so, Pugazhendi!”
Ottakoothar is said to have leapt up and asked:
“Only after the flower is plucked can a conch be blown at its stem;
is it not strange for a bee to blow upon the white petals?”
Pugazhendi immediately replied:
“Koothar, hear my answer!
Drunk on honey, not knowing front from back,
the bee blew there!”

Did not that quarrel between great poets
give Tamil two more poems?
So poets, I shall cultivate hostility among you — sing!
Like urging racehorses onward,
people under the pavilion, roar!
Some songs may taste like fenugreek;
in these songs let us see valour spitting red flame,
and the manner in which love is spoken in classical Tamil.

Those who were one in kinship like one life
have split into two and clash here.
Will the old bond stick again? you ask.
It will — an old bond that sticks!
Grafted mango kinship is natural in Salem.
So those whose bond will later stick
may now cut and stab each other here.
At the words cut and stab, do not leap up in valour saying,
“Ah, the chairman has praised valour!”
There is an eye-cut even in love;
a body-form that stabs the heart is love's Thai-Pongal.

<!-- scan 105 -->

To fight under the title of love,
a dear one comes — leader in love, Tamilanban.
For a body wrapped only in bone and skin,
is not love the true life?
Our cultured friend, a man of our old town — Erode!
The waves of the Kaveri fight one another
out of love for the woman bathing there.
Erode Tamilanban will speak love with order,
showing not a woman who receives rows of gifts —
jackfruit clusters, ungarlanded red-banana flowers,
a peahen embraced to the chest,
all the gifts a bull might bring —
but two people who receive two lineages,
agree with each other's music,
leave their two lines and consent to become one line,
a bar of bright gold in a giving garden.
Her smile's music will sound as love's music.
Without the music of that season,
can the music of valour's womb arise?
Tamilanban will ask with raised brow.
He stands as one of two formations.

Between two formations I, unable to discern their ranks, am chairman!
Even if neither side digests my thought, I have said it.
These are not merely two teams:
one is love's half-sari,
the other valour's grand procession.
Why should I stand between them?
You ask: is the middle a mortar to stand in?
I say it is an *udukku* drum;
I am today the mischievous priest who beats it
to summon a furious rain of Tamil!
Take up arrows of poetry,
string them with dignity.
Come, friend; come, Tamil-lover;
let this battle begin well.

(Poet Erode Tamilanban completes his poem on “Love.”)

<!-- scan 106 -->

Chairman Kalaignar:

He has prepared a notebook of love,
with laughter and throbbing,
plucking flowers from the grove and mixing sweet pollen,
about the disease that buds in the morning,
becomes a full flower through the day
and blooms in the evening,
until women hide their faces behind the ends of their saris.
Young men listened briskly;
students tasted it with a little irritation;
old men regretted their age.

Now comes a man with a book of rebuttal:
“What is love — an eggplant?
Valour is the chilli!”
He shakes his head to collide,
and comes to show the path of valour in argument.

When a warrior asked an old mother,
“Where is your son even now?”
she, swelling with joy, replied:

<!-- scan 107 -->

“I do not know where the tiger went;
he must have gone to the battlefield.
This is the cave where that tiger lived,”
and showed her womb.
That *Purappaatu*, that praise-song,
that song of valour honoured by noble Tamils!
The warrior who threw his spear in battle and killed an elephant,
then grew weary seeking another spear against the enemy,
rejoiced when he saw the spear embedded in his own chest —
the Kural that says this is valour.

With gold-fast Tamil, the man from Vaniyambadi will argue thus.
If I name the town, should I not name the man?
He is renowned Abdul Rahman,
rich in words like a swelling sea,
a pure man who does not let any harmful deed in the country
rest like a drop of dew upon bamboo leaf.
His song has not left my mind.
He cannot bear the claim that “love is greatest”; he has not languished —
he has risen like a lion.
He will not leave love alone;
he will not let it win!
He comes for a valiant battle.
Do not fear that he will take a sword;
he will take a sheet — the sheet on which he wrote his poem.
With flashing lightning he will wound love badly.
He will say there is no time to love — no, no,
he will say there is no courage to love!
To see what happens to love,

<!-- scan 108 -->

you need not eyes but ears.
In this field-battle,
will love die?
Will valour die?
We shall see in the skill of those who perform.

(Poet Abdul Rahman completes his poem on “Valour.”)

Chairman Kalaignar:

Next,
T. Ku. Natarasan comes to say love is sweet.
Is not Nataraja, moving with Ganga upon his head,
the god of the art of love?
If I say this, this Natarasan will wink and say:
“See! Siva placed love on his head
and valour under his foot” —
and make the man beneath that foot his witness,
claiming victory for love.
He looks valiant, yet speaks of love!
Two scorpions live upon his face — they are called a moustache;
his aim may be elsewhere, yet he has come as a character in this play.
He may call a duck a swan
to compare the graceful walk of beautiful women,
and say “in sweet love...”
He is of the age before domestic sourness;
let us make way for his longing. Come, younger brother!

(Poet T. Ku. Natarasan completes his poem on “Love.”)

<!-- scan 109 -->

Chairman Kalaignar:

Next,
to return the blow struck by the cock of love,
the cock of valour rushes in as Murugusundaram.
Murugu means beauty; Sundaram means the same!
Such beauty upon beauty —
what wonder that instead of singing love
he sings valour?
Sword-flash,
whirling round,
enemy drained,
victory-bride,
poem about it —
let us hear pearl-Tamil,
Murugu-Tamil.

(Poet Murugusundaram completes his poem on “Valour.”)

Chairman Kalaignar:

Next,
Ponni Valavan.
What connection has he with love?
Plenty — because until now he has made no marriage alliance anywhere!
So there is a connection.
In stringing garlands of Tamil flowers
he is a Sambandar of the Tevaram.
A speaker sweet as sugar; but if approached treacherously,
he is of Nakkeerar's principle in cutting off the tongue!
Even if he speaks of love, he will speak fiercely.
He will beat the drum of separation's anguish as though it were valour.
Lover, come;
give us lines of poetry.

(Poet Ponni Valavan completes his poem on “Love.”)

<!-- scan 110 -->

Chairman Kalaignar:

Next,
Rajendran.
His very name holds valour and historical greatness:
Rajendra who took the Ganga;
Rajendran who saw Sivaganga.
Our friend remains in our thought,
a statue in the heart.
He will sing of valour.
Though his body shows bone, his heart is steel.
This dear friend, filled with love,
will answer what was said before and make Tamil valour resound.

(Poet Rajendran completes his poem on “Valour.”)

Chairman Kalaignar:

Next,
advocate for valour Abdul Rahman
will offer another one or two arguments of substance;
I shall give judgement afterward.

(Poet Abdul Rahman delivers his concluding argument.)

<!-- scan 111 -->

Chairman Kalaignar:

Next,
advocate for love Erode Tamilanban
will argue.
My judgement follows.

(Poet Tamilanban delivers his concluding argument.)

Chairman Kalaignar's judgement poem:

Beloved people, elders!
Do not think what has happened so far was empty quarrelling;
it was vigorous quarrelling.
I rejoice and bless this kind of quarrel to happen throughout the country.
To the friend who argued valour is greatest,
love is not something bitter.
The friends who plead that love is greatest
may be cowards — no fault in that!
But only cowards love cowards.
Did not *Mullaikkali* thunder:
“The maiden will not embrace, even in another birth,
the man who fears the horns of the killing bull”?
The Tamil woman garlanded the warrior
who feared not even death while subduing the charging bull.
She told him:
“As you place your face upon a woman's breast,
place your head between the bull's horns
and win the glorious wound.”
Only after that happened did she consent in heart and marry him.
Yet it would be wrong to argue
that valour is therefore merely the first step in the epic of love.
They possessed courage unafraid even of death
in order to attain love.
Remembering this,
does love not appear greater?

<!-- scan 112 -->

“Where is the sword in the battle-hand?
Where the gem-like chest?
Where are the diamond shoulders
that never turned their back
whoever came on the face of battle?”
*Kalingathu Parani* roars so.
Yet the same *Kalingathu Parani* sings love:
“Dazed by the joy of union,
when the moon's light withdraws,
they take moonlight itself for cloth and dress in it...”
*Palaikkali* tells of the warrior
who set out toward the battlefront,
heard his maiden's sorrow,
melted and returned.
The Kural that shows the man
who has sent his hand-spear with the elephant
also pours honey of love:
“When eye meets eye...”
And *Kalithogai*, giving love among deer
like a fruit-segment inside honey,
tells of the stag that gives its own shade
to the doe suffering for lack of pleasant shade,
while also displaying the elephant's valour
as it battles the vengai tree and giving glory to kurinji.

<!-- scan 113 -->

In the legendary Ramayana story,
Indrajit, rare treasure of Ravana,
became Sulochana's husband through savoury love;
when enemy forces grew, he lost his head and became a hero.
Before losing it he threw his enemies into disorder.
The hero who saw Nikumbala,
after destroying a great mass of foes,
fell as a warrior,
son of crowned Ravana.

In the Bharata, Abhimanyu, Vatsala's beloved,
would give strong kisses — one, ten, a hundred —
upon pearl-like lips,
and receive clusters of love-fruit.
That unripe young shoot entered the battlefront,
spared no life,
and then died with unfading fame.
He loved the golden-lotus woman
whose hair was like the Courtallam falls;
did he sell away valour for that love in battle? No.
He remains a stream in epic song.

A king's daughter was won by a poet-king's son —
Ambikapathi and Amaravathi's love.
What happened then?
A heroic death.

<!-- scan 114 -->

Why only old tales?
You will ask for modern examples.
There are many; I shall say some.
Edward VIII gave up his crown for love —
does that mean every greatness accrues to love?
Love is enough to say, “Let the woman die with me”;
it takes courage, a spirit of sacrifice, to say “Let there be no such end.”
Why go so far?
An innocent fellow called Seeni falls for Soni next door
and hurriedly jumps the fence;
is that not a kind of courage too?
Valour for love; love among the valiant —
it is not fitting to separate the two
like butter and buttermilk.

Those defeated in love hang by a noose around the neck.
When love is refused,
they search pond and river saying death alone is the path,
to throw away life.
They do this because love alone possesses them;
if valour were joined as love's guard,
it would protect lovers like a god.

Bharati accused society:
“In drama and epic, when it is love
the people marvel and say it is good;
but in the house, by the well, in the village,
when love appears they roar
and build the bier to kill it.”

<!-- scan 115 -->

Bharathidasan too sang:
“With the fire of love I would leap the sea toward you;
the chain called caste has bound my feet, girl.”
Are these not for people in whom valour mingles with love?
Therefore, people, love!
Love with valour mixed into it.
Love without valour breeds despair;
valour without love breeds harshness.
There is no courage in life;
there is no tenderness in life.

I announce my judgement to the assembly:
Love and valour are twin children born embracing one another.
For this pair there is no family-planning restriction —
beget them freely!

Greetings.

✤

## Translator notes

- The canonical title is `காதலா - வீரமா?`; the contents witness `காதலா! - வீரமா?` is retained separately and not merged into a hybrid title.
- Salem/iron/honey, weaving, bridge/highways and numerous other passages depend on Tamil sound-play and contemporary political humour; English keeps the argumentative chain rather than inventing replacement puns.
- Classical citations from *Mullaikkali*, *Kalingathu Parani*, *Palaikkali*, *Kalithogai* and Tirukkural are translated in their source-facing role here, not substituted with outside editions.
- References to self-harm occur as part of Kalaignar's argument about love and despair; they are preserved as source rhetoric without elaboration.
