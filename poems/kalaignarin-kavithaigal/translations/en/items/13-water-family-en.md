# The Water Family

<!-- scan 144 -->

### The Water Family

Presiding poetic address by
Kalaignar M. Karunanidhi
at the *kaviyarangam* of Kandar College,
Paramathi Velur,
on 28 September 1968.

★

Greetings to the assembly;
greetings to the poets who have come to mount the stage,
and to those who founded Kandar College.
Greetings to the respected teachers
and beloved students.
Siruthondar gave his child as flesh;
today Kandar gives children knowledge —
he gives knowledge to the child.
As outward purity is formed by water,
and inward purity is seen through truth,
Kaveri and this college are fittingly joined;
let us call their founder a grove of generosity
and celebrate him always.
Only where there is moisture is there giving;
on this brave bank of Kaveri there is moisture,

<!-- scan 145 -->

there is giving,
and there is the life that earns musical fame.
Under the title “The Water Family,”
poets of enduring renown will sing.
They told me, “Are you not related to the water family?”
and seized me — a man who writes verse with watery letters —
and placed me among poets who write with ornament and metre.

The cloud's writing becomes rain;
rain's writing upon earth becomes river;
the straight writing of the river becomes pond and well.
The root-letter of all these, they say, is the sea.
One man grieves, “What fate is written on my head?”
and sheds tears;
another says, “I shall rewrite fate,”
and conquers it through the battle-script of sweat.

If I said that thunder is the old man of the sky coughing,
lightning the light from his swollen eyes,
and rain the coward's saliva he spits,
we would never welcome that diseased rain.
So I shall offer an image young people may like:
the fish of the sky are women;
their group-talk is thunder;
the sword-like sweep of their eyes is the lightning-streak;
and the rosewater they sprinkle on their round-moon lover
is the raindrop.
For warriors, another image:
when two valiant hearts clash — thunder;

<!-- scan 146 -->

when great warriors' spears scrape — lightning;
when many warriors pour red water — rain.
In general the sky itself is a battlefield —
whether a battlefield, a pleasure-field,
or a cruel field of disease.
Yet the good men of agriculture crudely called
the rain-giving sky “honour” — *mānam*!
Is it not fitting?
Enough of getting drenched in rain;
come, let us go near the sea.

When Vaishnavas hear “sea,”
they remember Vishnu lying in repose.
Saivites smeared head to foot with ash
remember the story of Siva drinking poison.
We remember the history of Poompuhar,
which no army could destroy
but the sea destroyed without hindrance.
Add and subtract all this,
and how many more benefits come from the sea!
Did not the sea give us pearls
so we could say “teeth like pearls,”
and let women speak words sweet as sugarcane?
The blue silk worn by maiden Earth in her season;
the tall, blue-hued handsome man
who raises wave-hands to embrace the moon-maiden;
a sand-mattress on the shore for roaming lovers;
a treasure-box of imagination for wandering poets.
For minds dark as midnight,
all they see on the shore are crab and snail;
but even the harbour where ships stand
like cattle tethered in a pen —
is not the delight of seeing it also given by the sea?

<!-- scan 147 -->

You gained greatness by humbling King Canute's pride,
gained distinction when Mahatma Gandhi made salt,
opened a route for Vasco da Gama's ships,
gave the first history to Tamils who crossed seas in trade,
became a simile for a broad heart,
remained for long an ever-moving mute,
at times became a deluge,
at times an evil to the world,
and then, like an ascetic mastering anger,
turned turtle-like and drew back your roaring waves.
Sea, can we ever finish telling all your glory?
So let us cross the sea and reach the river.

Coming through rain, embracing the mountain,
like a golden cloth passing through a slender waist,
like a palm-leaf strip thrusting its head through foliage,
like a parting drawn through a woman's hair,
you walk in joyous gait through the groves
and become Kaveri,
a woman adorned with jewels.
They called you woman, they called you Pennai;
because wherever you lay your hand delight dances,
they called you Vaigai, pouring song so wealth may flourish.
There is no Godavari in Tamil Nadu —
there is no line of caste coming here as “gothra-varisai”!
No Godavari for the dry field in Tamil Nadu;
for Tamils abroad, there is a place to live —
we define and provide it.
We bow to you, river — live long.
They called me here for six topics,
then asked me to write and sing seven;
I am singing.

Next, let us go to the well.

<!-- scan 148 -->

I am a frog in the well of the village of poetry.
I know the nation's resources;
I do not know the abundance of song as you do, sir!
If the long sky refuses rain,
if long river, pond and pool dry and wither,
if not even a cup of water remains to quench thirst,
then, as one swallows one's own saliva,
we immediately dig:
a spring-well, a seepage-well,
a deep well, a riverbank well.
People say, “When a well is dug, a ghost comes out.”
Do you understand what that means?
Dig a well and earth piles up — one element;
the air trapped under the ground comes out — another;
fresh water wells up — the next;
the bright sky appears upon the water — the fourth;
and the sun's fiery rays purify that water — the fifth.
It is the arrival of these five elements
that the old people expressed beautifully;
intellectual paupers merely scream “ghost!”

<!-- scan 149 -->

A pond gives beauty to a village;
I too have a pond — a heart — for poetry,
and I have a heart for public service.
The lotus pond is great beauty;
the hidden pond is the Tirukkural;
the heart of scripture is the Tirukkural.
There are sacred bathing tanks,
and bridge-building tanks;
there are tanks that have taken some lives.
There are swimming pools in towns —
should there still be pools of “low people” in villages?
An inlet sluice and outlet sluice are needed for a pond;
an inlet of knowledge and outlet of love are needed for the heart.
Every pond needs a stone bathing-step;
even a steel heart needs a touchstone on which to learn.

“Those who leave when the pond dries
are not true kin;
those who remain, like water-lily and lotus
rooted in the same pond,
are the kin who cling close.”

Singing this, I climb out of the pond.

The sea is vast on earth;
rain is sweet to humanity;
the river has great fame in the world;
the well too has many honours;
all ponds are nectar-mouths of the globe.
All that is fine.
But all of them arise only when the cloud-cluster sheds tears:
rain comes, the sea swells,
rivers run, wells spring, ponds fill.
Why did night's darkness vanish,
why did dream-joy break?
The tears shed by the morning flower are dew;
the tears shed by the coconut spathe are toddy;
the banana's tears become milk;
the poor man's tears become a spear,
become a sword.

<!-- scan 150 -->

“Are not the tears shed by those
who suffer beyond endurance
the army that wears away wealth?”
So let us turn swiftly from tears to sweat.
In an oyster's sweat a pearl is born;
in a sculptor's sweat a statue is born;
in a farmer's sweat hunger flies away;
in a warrior's sweat the enemy flies away.
The sweat of the powerful is rosewater;
the sweat of the humble is red water.
In the furnace, flame drinks the sweat that falls;
in the battlefield of love,
every drop of sweat becomes sandal paste and perfume.
Literature refuses to call that “sweat” —
so it becomes fragrance!

When poets of distinction are here
to give such spreading description
from the broad sea down to the pond of sweat,
is it right for me alone to sing so much?
Honey, fruit, millet, nectar,
flesh, life, Tamil, mother —
I offer greetings. Live long!

First, Murugusundaran comes to cross the sea,
giving lines sweet as palm-jaggery,
giving us a sea-song:

(Poet Murugusundaram finishes his poem on “The Sea.”)

Chairman Kalaignar:

Next, rain.
Natarasa Pulamai Nambi,
who drops good poems like *naval* fruit,
comes to pour a rain of verse;
if you have an umbrella, hold it up!

<!-- scan 151 -->

(Poet T. K. Natarasan finishes his poem on “Rain.”)

Chairman Kalaignar:

Next, river.
Our dear Tamilanban,
lover of tongue-sweet words, word-archer —
the river-topic is his flower.
The topic is river;
let the Tamil-river flow without obstruction,
and over the loudspeaker too let it flow freely:

(Poet Erode Tamilanban finishes his poem on “River.”)

Chairman Kalaignar:

Next, well.
Ramarasan digs a well;
he ties a Tamil pot to the rope;
he pours out a new flood.
Drink and rejoice — drink water,
drink well-water and rejoice:

(Poet Ramarasan finishes his poem on “Well.”)

<!-- scan 152 -->

Chairman Kalaignar:

One may live without *samsaram*, household life;
but can one live without *minsaram*, electricity?
*Samsaram* has not entered him until today —
but for that reason, do not touch *minsaram*!
He is my friend, so I warn him.

Next, pond.
In olden days Akbar and Aurangzeb saw battlefields;
in their line Abdul Rahman has found a pond.
To speak of it he too has entered a field — the field of poetry.
When we find a pond, shall we leave at once?
Let us squat on the bank and rest:

(Poet Abdul Rahman finishes his poem on “Pond.”)

Chairman Kalaignar:

Next, tears.
A Tamil poet-bull named Kandasamy
comes into this assembly
with a grieving heart to shed tears.
Let us ask what troubles him;
if we can, let us remedy the grievance:

(Poet Kandasamy finishes his poem on “Tears.”)

<!-- scan 153 -->

Chairman Kalaignar:

Next, sweat.
To show white pearl-like sweat,
do we not need a black background like the centre of the eye?
Ponnivalavan comes to sing in tune —
a man rich as gold in verse.
He will sing with sweat falling
and drive away all our weariness:

(Poet Ponnivalavan finishes his poem on “Sweat.”)

Chairman Kalaignar's concluding poem:

“Because I am the irrigation minister,
this Water Family theme is useful to me.”
So I flew toward Paramathi Velur.
Poets have sung on many topics;
one topic dragged another into playful quarrel
and completed our theme —
completed our head-*bag* too!
I will not give a one-sided judgement.
But just one thing:
from the sea, rain must increase;
rivers must run;
wells and ponds must fill with water.
In the Water Family one thing must decrease,
and another must disappear.

<!-- scan 154 -->

Sweat must decrease —
for those who work,
as facilities increase,
sweat must decrease.
Tears must disappear.
Because we always desire happiness for everyone,
tears must disappear!

Greetings.
Long live!

## Translator notes

- The poem treats sea, rain, river, well, pond, tears and sweat as one “water family.” That structural sequence is retained in full.
- Several stretches depend on Tamil homophony and word splitting (`நீர்`, `நேர்`, `மானம்`, `கோதாவரி`, `சம்சாரம் / மின்சாரம்`, `தலைப்பை / தலைப்-பை`). English cannot reproduce all of these simultaneously; the sense and the Tamil term are both kept where needed.
- Religious, historical and political examples — including Poompuhar, King Canute, Gandhi, Vasco da Gama and caste criticism — remain source rhetoric rather than being externally harmonised.
