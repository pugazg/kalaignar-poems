# Thank You, Thank You!

<!-- scan 216 -->

At the Salem exhibition on 1 August 1971,
at the *kaviyarangam* “Kalaignar in Perspective,”
Kalaignar M. Karunanidhi took part
and, in thanks for the praise offered to him,
wrote this immediately on the stage
and sang it there.

The poets in this *kaviyarangam*
called me father,
mother,
child,
leader,
servant,
beloved woman,
and beloved man.
I dedicate these lines of thanks to them.

Festival chairman Vezhavendan,
with a noble gaze,
has spread out a poetry-market here
from morning onward.
Poet Kannappan explained in song
the figure of the father.

Like the roar of a shouting sea-wave
you gave your poem;
young poet Muthulingam,
you gave my mother back to me again.
Through the mouth of Aara Amudhan,
I became

<!-- scan 217 -->

a child whose games never end!

One bearing the name of the black-gold Kamaraj leader
accepted me here,
in flashing Tamil,
as a leader too —
a Kamarasan.

Avinasi Mani, the poet
who once worked in my house as a servant-boy,
has today turned me myself
into the servant in his song —
what times these are!

Even after seeing my wife seated beside me,
what courage has this Murugu Sundari
to fall in love with me?

And Kannadasan —
you who separated,
wandered in many directions,
then corrected your heart
and hurried back home —
my beloved man!
Do not go like Kovalan
to sell the anklet.
I, the Kazhagam's Kannagi,
will stand guard at the gate.
Do not be troubled!

Thank you! Thank you! Thank you!
My thanks to everyone!

## Translator notes

- The poem is an improvised stage-response to a sequence of poets assigning Kalaignar family, political, servant and romantic roles. The English keeps those role reversals rather than recasting them as narrative exposition.
- The closing address to Kannadasan uses the *Silappathikaram* Kovalan/Kannagi pair together with `கழகம்` (“Kazhagam”) political identity. The translation retains “Kazhagam's Kannagi” rather than inventing a non-source institutional gloss.
- Personal and political references are translated as source-stage humour; the English does not independently interpret the circumstances behind Kannadasan's “return home.”
