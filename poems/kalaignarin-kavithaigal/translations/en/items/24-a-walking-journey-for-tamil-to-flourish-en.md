# A Walking Journey for Tamil to Flourish

<!-- scan 237 -->

Presiding poetic address by
Kalaignar M. Karunanidhi
at the Pari festival on Parambu Hill
on 12 April 1973,
for the *kaviyarangam* titled
“A Walking Journey for Tamil to Flourish.”

Every mountain sings of abundance!
Every Tamil art sings of culture!
Every wave of the sea dances!
Every cluster of enemies trembles and runs!

In the forest where peaks rise,
where swift creatures run and play,
where proud herds of deer move,
where paired cuckoos sing their music,
where forest peacocks spread rhythm and expression,
where birds gather,
where silk banners and victory parasols turn and whirl —
let us worship the fame of generous Pari,
who poured out bounty like a flood,
and who received the circling radiance
of pearl-like Tamil.

<!-- scan 239 -->

You have given us the apt title “walking journey”
and made a way for the Tamil people,
precious as the eye,
to grow.
Live long! Live long!

Let us labour without weariness every day
so the people may live.
Anger is not evil
when harm comes to Tamils.
Are we not a pride of lions?
Shall we bow our heads to the howling of jackals?
Before tigers in full fury,
shall the base make a show of bravado?

Where has my Tamil gone?
Does he stand like a vertical pillar,
without courage to oppose the degradations here?
Does he lie there
with no pulse in his limbs?
Alas, poor fellow —
write a song about him for the Sangam!
Set up a golden statue for him!

The Pandya had a moustache
like two chisels laid above the lips,
bright enough to proclaim
that humiliation never came to him.
The Chola's moustache spread outward
toward the ears,
as though crying the same racial call.
The Chera's long moustache,
curving slightly,
grew toward the eyes
like the swinging trunk of an elephant.

<!-- scan 240 -->

Because those moustaches once trembled,
there are stories of enemy mountains
being ground to dust.
Today we need that courage —
we need it greatly.
Store valour in good action;
banish cruel action entirely;
recover the feeling of the Tamils of old.
Recover the feeling of honour.
Recover the feeling of self-respect.

Poets here,
you will sing of eight paths.
But tell us:
who showed the Tamil people blocked paths?
Those who make a habit of striking us
will hold court without hindrance
for as long as we remain bent.
So let us look here
at some of the paths we can reach.

The path of Kapilar —
not merely a poet's path,
but the finest path of friendship,
a path of compassion.

When Pari,
whose palanquin, sceptre, chariot and army
had been defeated on the battlefield,
fell dead,
Kapilar suffered endlessly
to arrange marriages
for Pari's two daughters,
two ripe mango-fruits.
In the end,
through Kapilar's effort,
the sons of King Kari
became husbands to Pari's daughters.

<!-- scan 241 -->

Having fulfilled his unique duty
to his friend who had ruled a land,
to Pari, lord of Tamil,
the great poet Kapilar,
master of texts,
thought:
“Now nothing prevents me
from joining my Pari.”
He sat facing north
and gave up his life.

Today there are dear friends
who, if a friend is dying,
hurry to place a stone upon his head!
For all of them to change their path,
is not Kapilar's path
a path worth learning?

The path of Avvai —
old Avvai, walking with a staff,
taught paths by which the sceptre of rule
might remain upright.

The path of Ilamperuvazhuthi —
a good path by which the Tamil people may prosper.

Walk the path of Valluvar,
and will enemies dare enter the heart and come near?

The path by which Ilango renounced household life
became the path of *Silappathikaram*,
through which he gave Tamils a noble ethic.

The path of Appar's life
is a path of love.
So too is the path of Vallalar Adigal.

The path that framed pearl-like principles —
that is the path of Arignar Anna!
The precious path of Anna!

Poets have come
to sing of these paths.
Let us give them the way;
let us receive their sweet words.

*(Poet Subramaniam sings on “Kapilar's Path.”)*

<!-- scan 242 -->

Next —
I heard that Adigalar wishes to perform virtue.
Forgetting the old saying
that “to receive is disgrace,”
Abdul Rahman has come
seeking Avvai's path.

I merely said the *Aathichudi*!
Dear Rahman,
“Let anger cool.”
Without abandoning courage,
give us your song.

*(Poet Abdul Rahman sings on “Avvai's Path.”)*

Next —
there is one called Ilancheran.
Like me, his head is bald.
Let it be!
Is not a tender coconut bald too?
Ilanchera, one of my own kind,
find us a pleasant path.

*(Poet Kovai Ilancheran sings on “Vazhuthi's Path.”)*

Next —
Muthu Sambandam,
who takes nourishing Tamil
and makes poems like clusters of fruit —

<!-- scan 243 -->

now let him sound forth,
his song,
Valluvar's song.

*(Poet Muthu Sambandam sings on “Valluvar's Path.”)*

Next —
a Pandyan comes to sell an anklet.
“What is this?
Are you changing the *Silappathikaram*?”
you ask?
No, no!
It is Shanmuga Pandian,
showing Ilango's path.
That is what I meant.

*(Poet Shanmuga Pandian sings on “Ilango's Path.”)*

Next —
“To walk the path, we need the feet of Appar;
to walk the path, we need Appar's feet” —
and Vadivelan comes in beauty:
Singara Vadivelan comes.

*(Poet Singara Vadivelan sings on “Appar's Path.”)*

<!-- scan 244 -->

Next —
Mariadasu.
If he says Vallalar's path
is the path proper to humankind,
is not Mariadasu right?

*(Poet Mariadasu sings on “Vallalar's Path.”)*

Next —
Mudiyarasu, who studies melody and sings,
now studies Anna's path and sings.
Poet-king dear as the eye,
may our government always endure.

*(Kaviyarasu Mudiyarasan sings on “Anna's Path.”)*

They have told us eight paths.
We have listened.
Now —
what is our path?

The Singampunari road,
the Tiruchirappalli road,
the Thanjavur road —
the road our Anna laid down for us.
That is our road.
We are coming along it.

Greetings.

❀

## Translator notes

- `தமிழ் வளர வழிநடைப் பயணம்` is rendered **A Walking Journey for Tamil to Flourish**, preserving both the literal walking-journey frame and the poem's repeated political/literary `வழி` — path/way — architecture.
- Item 24 intentionally owns scans **237 and 239–244**. Physical scan **238 belongs to item 23**; the English provenance therefore preserves the source's interposition and does not create a false continuous run.
- The poem's moustache imagery, courage rhetoric and references to Kapilar, Avvai, Ilamperuvazhuthi, Valluvar, Ilango, Appar, Vallalar and Anna are retained as Kalaignar's rhetorical sequence.
- Several guest introductions depend on Tamil sound-play — notably baldness/tender coconut, `சிலம்பு`/Silappathikaram, and `அப்பரடி`/Appar's feet. The English keeps the governing sense while documenting the loss rather than silently replacing the Tamil conceit.
