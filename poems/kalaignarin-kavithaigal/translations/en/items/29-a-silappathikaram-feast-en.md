# A Silappathikaram Feast

<!-- scan 273 -->

Presiding poetic address by
Kalaignar M. Karunanidhi
at the evening *kaviyarangam*
entitled “A Silappathikaram Feast,”
held at the Kotrappandal in Poompuhar
on 25 April 1975.

Greetings.
To our Navalar,
a form of many-coloured Tamil;
to our dear Professor,
a treasury of thought;
to ministers Mannai and Sadiq,
as precious as the eye;
and to all who came by Anna's path — greetings.

To Sivagnana Selvar,
with the sound of the anklet ringing;
to good Ganesan,
rich in ancient Tamil wisdom —
my head-bowed greetings.

To every loving heart
that answered the call
and has come with eagerness
to restore ruined Poompuhar
to beauty —
my humble greetings.

To the poetic hearts
who have brought noble Tamil
to serve us a feast
from *Silappathikaram* —

<!-- scan 274 -->

my affectionate greetings.

O rare Tamil! O nectar!
Fragrance of a sweet flower! Honey!
You came as mother and ruled me;
you came as Anna and made me a man of action.
Bowing at the flower-feet of Kanchi Anna,
I begin this poetry assembly.
Bless us, mother —
may Tamils live,
may Tamil land live!

From the summit of Kudagu hill,
the lovely maiden Kaveri came dancing.
The Karnataka man who ran to reach
the dancing beauty said, “*Aṇai*!” — a dam, an embrace.
She crossed the embrace of his hands,
entered the stone embrace of the Kallanai,
and, guarding her chastity,
leapt toward Thanjavur.
Hearing the grievance of the flower-maiden Kaveri,
the Chola king made the waves of Poompuhar's sea
her husband and protected her.

If a woman's greatness lies
in not forgetting the home of her birth
while making the home she enters prosper,
would that not suit
our eye-jewel Kaveri as well?

Thus the city into which Kaveri entered,
the city where Kaveri enters,
became Kaveripoompattinam, I would say.

Karikala Peruvalathan, Tirumavalavan —

<!-- scan 275 -->

for tender friendship
his scabbard held the sword;
for wicked enmity
the sword rose from its sheath.
With Uraiyur as his capital,
he later married another town as capital too.
For this man with two capital-wives,
the younger one —
sweetest of the two —
is Poompuhar,
welcoming us here today.

“High-maned horses that came by sea,
black pepper sacks that came by land” —
so *Pattinappalai* thunders
of the port-city that shone in those days.

There was Maruvurpakkam,
with warriors who rose in battle;
a quarter where city men,
intoxicated by desire for women,
walked about;
a day-market to which people
from other lands came daily;
a royal street,
a street of flag-bearing chariots,
a merchants' street,
a street of truth-filled farmers;
streets crowded with physicians,
astrologers, bards and performers,
with elephants and horses everywhere.
There was the splendid Pattinappakkam,
spoken of as a painted quarter.

There were halls proclaiming
the Chola's northern victories:
the debate hall,
the pearl pavilion,
the festooned gateway,
the tall-stone hall
affirming Tamil victory in the ancient world,
the Ilanji hall,
the Pavai hall drawing lovers of art;
a Murugan shrine,
near it a Tirumal shrine,
an Arugan shrine,
near it a Buddha shrine;
a precinct where Muthamizh resounded,
and every day,
a Chithirai-festival crowd.

<!-- scan 276 -->

Ilango's Gunavayir Kottam
portrays all these.
All of them, mother,
were destroyed by sea and time.
Yet the wealth of the anklet
given by the Chera —
an imperishable treasury,
an undying sacred lamp — remains.

Awakened by that lamp's light,
with an unforgettable love of language,
there arose a new Puhar —
not one of the empty “complaints”
that useless men raise every day!
A new Puhar, Poompuhar,
created surging from Tamil breath
fragrant with hot blood and valour.

To Kannagi, lamp of the home;
to queenly Madhavi;
to Karikala Chola,
with a diamond-adorned chest;
to Ilango's cultured anklet,
royalty of poetry —
to give all these a beautiful new form,
what thanks can I offer
Tamil Nadu that gave birth to me,
and my own mother and father?

To make many special chapters our right,
we demanded autonomy;
we said we would gladly accept even death for it,
and would endure even the loss of office —
was it not this *Silappathikaram*
that taught us to feel so?

Deceivers came,
prospered through Tamil,
rose in life,
and erased our history.
Today the anklet that caresses us
is at least a tower-lamp
showing us some part of our history.

<!-- scan 277 -->

With the name *Silappathikaram* —
“Let us establish a poem-epic
belonging to all three crowned kings;
Adigal, you must grant it” —
there arose this epic,
an epic of awakening.

Eight incomparable poets are here
to offer a feast from that literature.

“Laughter, weeping, degradation, wonder,
fear, pride, anger and joy —
beyond these are the eight *meyppādu*,”
said the elder grammarian.
Following that path,
they will create eight flavours here:
laughter,
sorrow,
degradation,
wonder,
fear,
valour,
anger,
and joy —
a contest sung without hostile intent.

Through tears one may sometimes gain laughter.
If one marries a husband
who obeys only through fear...!
When people do not grow angry
even after humiliation,
warriors may feel wonder.
Joy and sorrow are children of one mother,
following one another like shadows.

These eight flavours —
Tamil like sweet *pittu*,
curd tied thick,
a bowl filled high,
poured out for you;
a silk flag,
a victory parasol,
a string of pearls —
come to the Chola city!

<!-- scan 278 -->

Come and explain each flavour!
We called — and eight have come.
Would the one who composed *Silappathikaram*
by taking up “eight”
leave the eight flavours out?

> Number and letter,
> five branches of *iyal*,
> four modes of *paṇ*,
> eleven forms of dance born from music —
> on this earth,
> with golden-ankleted Madhavi of Poompuhar,
> Kovalan joined in the evenings,
> weary and entranced,
> caught in desire hard to release.

And the joy he shared
with Kannagi, impossible to destroy,
had no count!

Kannagi and Kovalan too —

> Joined beneath the flower-garlanded canopy,
> they clasped hands as husband and wife;
> all pleasures whose name does not fade
> they drew close to themselves...
> and stood as though they had seen
> the impermanence of life upon the earth.

Not only in joy —
in sorrow too,
in clinging to a person's feet in worship,
the characters Madhavi and Kannagi
compete with one another.

> Kanakan and Vijayan,
> with tongues they could not guard,
> joined the guest-kings
> and spoke without knowing
the power of rare Tamil.
> When the Chera heard those words,
> he said, “Beat the war-drum!”
> and the stone that would be borne
> upon the crowned heads of the cruel —

that scene explains the flavour of valour,
the splendour that raised the Tamil victory flag.

Sweet honey-syrup,
clear nectar that never cloys — Kovalan —

<!-- scan 279 -->

when the Pandya famed in every direction
had him killed as a thief,
Kannagi surged like a wheel of fire.
To make the king who gave that judgement
bow his head,
she took the anklet and broke it.
The Madurai king,
as though his own bone had been broken,
was struck with wonder
and fell dead at once.

“I am a Pandya of a rule
that never strays from justice —
am I that Nedunchezhiyan king
who has now failed in justice?”
How much wonder lies in that question!
How much trembling!

There are beings in this world
who have no kinship at all
with any of the eight flavours —
wonder, laughter, sorrow, anger,
valour, degradation, fear and joy.

When Kalaivanar gave
explosive laughter like thunder
as the flavour of comedy
to the educated and the unlettered alike,
some would sit like pounded tamarind,
unwilling even to bare their teeth!

When great leaders,
who accepted repeated prison terms
and the wilderness of repression
as sacrifice for a beloved principle,
pass away,
thousands, tens of thousands,
hundreds of thousands gather
with grief-filled hearts
at the cremation ground,
longing to pay their final respects.
And if at that moment
a clowning performer walks in —

<!-- scan 280 -->

a foolish crowd may shout!
Clapping,
cheering,
celebratory dancing —
our ears and eyes close by themselves.
Those who do not know
the right place and meaning
for sorrow and joy —
I say they are lower than a donkey!

Tolkappiyar, master of the old treatise,
separated out one hundred and two
manifestations of emotion
and wove his thought.
Of them,
there are poets here
to show the chief eight flavours
and many branch-flavours
through poetic grace.
There is also a woman here
to show them through *abhinaya*.

But if one watches the dancing of some women,
one may wonder whether one committed
a great sin in a former life!

For desire burning like fire,
for anger pouring like honey,
for the emotional “bhava”
that hisses like an enraged cobra,
and for coyness that gathers
pomegranate-red cheeks —
there is not even a small difference,
rise or fall, in the face.
The cosmetic-coated mask
does not move at all.
Have you ever seen,
through *abhinaya*,
even an atom of change
on the golden girl's statue-face,
apart from the mischief of her limbs?

“Thimi-thimi,” they dance,
shaking the hall;
“mithi-mithi,”
they stamp as though trampling mud.
If they keep dancing like that,
the dance-stage will become a broken wall;
the art itself will become murder, I say.

Tolkappiyar placed “joy” at the end
and completed the garland
of eight kinds of expression.
Today, however,
let us begin the poetry assembly with joy
and hear firm valour at the end.

Is valour merchandise for the last stall?

<!-- scan 281 -->

Do not ask so, my dear.
For some party leaders,
valour has indeed become
a commodity whose price can be asked!

When there is war,
ours is the crowd that says,
“I first! You first!” —
not the crowd of people
who make quarrelling their profession.
We come in the line of the fighters of Vaikom!
Can a haystack play with this fire?

Why remember others
in this good assembly?
Let our poetry gathering begin in joy
and end in world-famed valour.
Now do you understand well
why I said we may hear valour “at the end”?

O Tamil maiden,
showing the beauty of the waist in *Manimekalai*
and the beauty of gait in the *Silambu* —
see, the line of poets has set out
to arrange a line of songs before you.
Let their skill begin!
I call the poets.

### The poets are introduced

First,
poet Vairamuthu,
who will sing of **joy**
from the pearls inside the anklet.

*(Poet Vairamuthu sings on “Joy.”)*

Next —
see how sorrow follows joy!
Perunthevan in appearance,
Ilanthēvan in age —

<!-- scan 282 -->

he will scoop and give us
in rich Tamil
the sorrow called “sorrow”
from within the anklet.

*(Poet Ilanthevan sings on “Sorrow.”)*

Next —
there are people who laugh at sorrow.
That stone-hearted poet Kannappan
comes with laughter.
He is not coming like Kovalan,
nor coming to Madurai
with the ornament called an anklet.
He comes to this Poompuhar poetry assembly
to display the **laughter**
that flourishes within the *Silambu*.

*(Poet Kannappan sings on “Laughter.”)*

Next —
there are those who leap in anger
at people who laugh uselessly.
Natarajan of Chidambaram
raises his leg gracefully in dance;
here T. G. Natarajan comes straight,
lifting poetry in the Tamil field.
Without getting angry,
sing to us of **anger**, ayya!

*(Poet T. G. Natarajan sings on “Anger.”)*

Next —
when we see one who is angry,
fear may arise.
To remove it by bringing one who is fit,
we have called poet Tamilanban.

<!-- scan 283 -->

O rare Tamil that speaks in the assembly without fear,
pour poetry in language dripping with flavour!

*(Poet Tamilanban sings on “Fear.”)*

Next —
though the wise know that one must not sleep in fear,
to drive forward a fierce struggle
and win for principle,
fear itself is evil; it is not manliness.
To warn the Tamil people
that if fear arrives, **degradation** follows,
Abdul Rahman comes.
The flavour he sings is degradation —
but what he gives is a downpour of Tamil!

*(Poet Abdul Rahman sings on “Degradation.”)*

Next —
Tamil honour says
that if degradation comes, death is better.
Kuruvikkarambaiyar wonders
how so many remain alive
even after degradation has come.
The little village that gave us Kuthoosi Gurusamy
has also given us precious Shanmugam,
and plunges Tamil Nadu into **wonder**.

*(Poet Kuruvikkarambai Shanmugam sings on “Wonder.”)*

<!-- scan 284 -->

Next —
there is a poet to sing of **valour**
who shows valour in action.
My heart always holds moisture enough
to grow this crop of the Ponni.
In the month when Valavan lived,
let us hear the poem sung by Ponnivalavan.

*(Kaviyarasu Ponnivalavan sings on “Valour.”)*

### Kalaignar's conclusion

From morning until this poetry assembly,
I have listened to Tamil sounds
sweet as sugarcane.
I have enjoyed them;
I remain unable not to enjoy them.
For a while,
with a happy heart,
I have passed time in peace.
After drinking, step by step,
the Tamil honey nurtured
by crowned kings and ancient poets,
how can I now manage
to give a conclusion?

People who came to Poompuhar!
People who gained new feeling!
Before I wish you farewell —
see the full Chithirai moon tonight.
Give kisses on the seashore
to children who are stamped-gold treasures.
Do not ask,
“Only to the children?”
That is a matter
between maiden and bull!

Look at the Poompuhar moon.
Think how it resembles
the victory parasol
of the Chola king who ruled the Tamil world.

<!-- scan 285 -->

To reach our ideal,
set the literature of the anklet
firmly within your minds.
And while you stand
beside the sea of wandering waves,
offer your greetings too
to the government
that walks on Anna's path.

Greetings!

## Translator notes

- `சிலப்பதிகார விருந்து` is rendered **A Silappathikaram Feast**. *Silappathikaram* and related Tamil literary titles are retained rather than replaced by English-only labels because they are active components of the poem's literary rhetoric.
- Scan 274 exploits `அணை` as both a dam and an embrace in the personified Kaveri/Karnataka passage. The English exposes both senses instead of fabricating a new pun.
- The Puhar city description draws on source-visible classical references, including *Pattinappalai*. The translation preserves Kalaignar's reconstruction and etymological/political claims as source claims rather than harmonising them with outside scholarship.
- `மெய்ப்பாடு` is retained in explanation as *meyppādu*, the Tolkappiyam category of embodied/emotive expression. The eight source labels are kept distinct across the poem and guest-poet sequence: `உவகை`, `அழுகை`, `நகை`, `வெகுளி`, `அச்சம்`, `இழிவு`, `வியப்பு`, `வீரம்`.
- The embedded *Silappathikaram* passages are translated only to the degree required by this poem's source text; Phase 4 does not silently substitute wording from a separate edition of the epic.
- The modern political comments about autonomy, repression, parties, Vaikom and the Anna-path government are translated as Kalaignar's source rhetoric.
- All eight guest-poet hand-offs remain explicit because their order is the organising structure of the latter half of the *kaviyarangam*.
