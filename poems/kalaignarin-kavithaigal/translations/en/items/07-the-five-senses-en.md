# The Five Senses

<!-- scan 80 -->

The Five Senses

A presiding poetic introduction by
Kalaignar M. Karunanidhi
at the *kaviyarangam* titled “The Five Senses”
held at Islamiah College, Vaniyambadi,
on 24 March 1968.

★

Elders of this great college assembly,
those filled with affection,
young lions!
Receive my greetings with hearts in which a mother's love grows,
as I come to sing Tamil that bows to no enemy,
falls before no army,
and lives through many ages.

We have arranged a *kaviyarangam*
on the five senses — body, mouth, eye, nose and ear.
Friend Rahman said lovingly, “Sir, come.”
He said with his mouth that it was a poetry gathering;
my whole body thrilled — had they really counted me a poet?
I asked, “Really?”
He winked and said yes — perhaps it was a joke!
Then came a letter;
I seized it and read it — with my eyes, of course!
Again the invitation: come sing.

<!-- scan 81 -->

A small treatment for the nose injured in a car accident;
I stayed silent, wondering whether I could go.
My friend did not let go.
The telephone rang sharply;
in my ear I heard Rahman's voice.
He invited me by mouth and thrilled my body,
sent a letter for my eyes to see,
and though there was illness in Kalaignar's nose,
said he would not let me off even three-quarters of the way;
without minding expense he called by telephone to cool my ear.
After all that, if I failed to come,
would you leave me untouched?
Fearing otherwise,
I tied up this unopened bud of poetry
and ran here this morning;
I came in the evening believing it would bloom by evening!

They say one beautiful moon makes the lily bud bloom;
here there are five or six beautiful poet-moons,
so surely even the bud I brought will bloom?
If it does not, blame the moon;
blame those who thought me a poet.
In North Arcot, where colourful hills stand,
there is a friend, a jasmine branch in my grove of friendship,
whose heart trembled whenever trouble came to me —
that sugar-cane-sweet “Mullai.”
When coming here there was no holding me back;
so I came close.

“They say the body dances and subsides,
the mouth sings and subsides,

<!-- scan 82 -->

the eye seeks and subsides,
the nose withers and subsides,
the ear listens and subsides;
therefore controlling the five senses is good.”
How can that be?
Will the ear reject the song of the green bird?
Will the body reject the embrace of the green parrot?
Will the mouth refuse to speak ancient Tamil?
Will the eye reject a moon like a jackfruit segment?
Will the nose reject the fragrance of a dew-wet flower?

All those great sages of the worlds
who performed penance to subdue the five senses —
what suffering they went through!
Viswamitra gave up throne and jewelled crown
and lived as an ascetic in the forest;
do you not know how he became Menaka's faithful friend?
Likewise, when eye, ear, nose, mouth and body —
the five senses — ran like unbridled horses,
like a flood in a wild river,
Arunagiri stood helpless,
blind though he had eyes,
deaf though he had ears,
with wounded body and lamenting mouth.

Everything must remain within measure,
and that measure must be steady.
I told these two stories for that reason.
If a knife is in your hand,
do not stab with it;
but do not hesitate, with intelligence,
to cut fruit and eat it.
To pretend, “I shall attain liberation,”
and refuse to use the five senses is deceit;

<!-- scan 83 -->

to let all five loose and wander like a temple bull is also wrong.
You who have come on stage,
each taking one sense apiece — one word!
Leave that evil aside.

In this college hall, those who let poetry mount the stage
will say their own theme is greatest.
Poets! When you argue that your chosen subject is the greatest,
a warning:
if the body is too large, people call it an elephant;
if the eye is too large, an owl;
if the nose is too large, a capsicum;
if the mouth is too large, they say chatterbox;
if the ear is too large, they mutter “winnowing tray.”
So speak of your subject knowing the proper measure.

If trouble comes, I shall ward it off;
if trouble comes among poets, I shall rejoice!
Take up the staff of Tamil and play a duel of words;
take up arrows of verse and attack one another;
at the same time, take up ripened love and support one another.

Taste, light, touch, sound and smell —
expound the kinds of the five.
Put enmity, blame, victory and boast in your poems;
put laughter, friendship and the feeling of “we” in the ledger of the heart;
then rise to battle!
A little cockfight is for money;
a king's fight is for a country;
your fight is for song;
the fight we watch is for Tamil.

First, on the theme of the body —

<!-- scan 84 -->

Sahul Hameed will pour a poem flowing like ghee.
What will he say?
Without the body there is no eye, no nose,
no mouth, no ear.
He will say the body is the support of all.
Is that true?
That is — is it true that he is going to say that?
Valluvar too stands beside him:
“Touching the bodies of one's children is delight to the body.”
Between the debate over truth and falsehood,
let us taste guava fruit.
Let us wait a little;
let us hear Sahul Hameed's Tamil.

(Poet Sahul Hameed completes his poem on the “body.”)

Chairman Kalaignar:

Next,
Murugusundaram, a man of Salem —
will the iron there never melt?
Not for one month or two, not for one year or ten;
from the day the Congress came to rule,
we have all been melting, but their heart did not melt!
Honey-iron: if that does not melt,
with the honey-Tamil he has learned
he will sing so that our hearts melt.
Murugusundaram comes...
He will argue that one cannot sing without a mouth
and ask me for judgement.
I shall be bewildered.

<!-- scan 85 -->

He will say even the judgement must be spoken by mouth!
I shall answer: why not write it with the hand?

I will give his mouth, as a morsel,
Valluvar's thought:
“When one gets those able to speak in ordered sweetness,
the world quickly listens and acts.”
He will sing of the mouth;
we shall listen with our mouths shut —
for is the mouth not the doorway through which food enters?

(Poet Murugusundaram completes his poem on the “mouth.”)

Chairman Kalaignar:

Next,
Rajendran comes to the eye of this assembly to sing of the eye.
I have seen his poetry with my own eye;
it is honey, sugar, rock-candy.
He will argue:
“What use are words from the mouth
when eye meets eye?”
Valluvar too gave the word “eye” abundant space in the Kural:
“Is she Death? Is she an eye?” in one;
a verse beginning with cruel brows and ending in “her eye”;
another proclaiming “two glances dwell within her painted eye”;
another on the flower-like-eyed woman;
and “The great ornament called gracious vision —
by that truth this world exists.”

<!-- scan 86 -->

Do not think that because so many Kurals stand on his side
I cannot give judgement.
With the brain that is trapped in none of these five senses,
we shall think, open our eyes and decide;
until then let us close our eyes and lose ourselves in his poetic delight.

(Poet Rajendran completes his poem on the “eye.”)

Chairman Kalaignar:

Next,
Tamilanban is going to break the nose!
Though the spectacles needed by both eyes
are carried by the nose between them,
it generously surrendered the right to be called “nose-glasses”
and let them remain “eye-glasses” —
from where did such broad-heartedness come?
He will beat the drum for the nose and enter the battle.
Valluvar too spoke of the nose
that makes the anicham flower wilt merely by smelling it.
Without the nose there is no breath;
so if my friend asks judgement in his favour,
I shall simply exhale through my mouth and show him.

Rather than stand before the friend who sings of the nose,
let us think about the course of his poem.
Betel is presented with a wedding invitation;
here we have been invited with betel to drink the fragrance of Tamil.
Let us drink.

(Poet Erode Tamilanban completes his poem on the “nose.”)

<!-- scan 87 -->

Chairman Kalaignar:

Next,
like a sea that sings in waves,
like Mamallapuram that sings of art,
there is Ponni Valavan who sings poetry.
Valavan of the Kaveri was Karikala Valavan;
this one is dark-bodied Valavan — yet
majestic-bodied Valavan.
He comes to speak of the ear.

“Wealth among wealth is the wealth of hearing;
that wealth is chief among all wealth.”
He will show this Kural and ask for the prize.
True — for argument's sake! But...
“Words of such quality that they bind those who hear
and make even those who do not hear desire them” —
only when the mouth speaks can they enter the ear and become wealth.
So I shall save myself with that reply.
Let his argument proceed;
let classical Tamil song enter our ear and sweeten it.
We shall lend our ears; come, sir, with your poem!
Valavan has arrived.
See the complexion I described; see the poetic skill.
If you place the two of us side by side,
you will see a two-coloured flag.

(Poet Ponni Valavan completes his poem on the “ear.”)

Chairman Kalaignar's concluding poem:

In a land that drapes poets in silk,
body, mouth, eye, nose and ear — Tamil

<!-- scan 88 -->

has woven a silk cloth and draped us all.
We listened to songs roaring as they attacked one another.
If the moon and Ganga quarrel
upon Siva's red matted hair adorned with konrai,
it becomes a headache for Siva;
but under my leader(ship),
this poets' fight is a battlefield fight —
a fight joined with Tamil rock-candy!

The one who said the body is greatest will not wish to live without a mouth;
the one who said the mouth is greatest will not wish to be deaf;
the one who praised the beauty of the eye will not dare reduce the beauty of the nose;
and will the one who praised the nose say, “I will go about bumping into things without eyes?”

Those who climbed this stage and sang possess all of them.
So is this a useless quarrel?
No — it is a quarrel of vīṇā-Tamil,
a quarrel that sharpens knowledge.
If this quarrel takes place throughout the country,
learning will grow; novelty will arise;
Tamil will gain new life.
Continue this pure work!
As long as breath passes through the nose,
serve Muttamil!
Even after it ceases, guard Tamil through your fame.
Turn everything the eye sees into poetry;
turn it into poetry of awakening,
and begin the duty of putting it into action —

<!-- scan 89 -->

begin the duty, begin the duty!
Hear what is good with the ear; cast away what is not;
open the mouth to sing the welfare of classical Tamils.
Even if you say only one thing, say a good thing.
If a body exists without all I have said,
then that body is not *mey* — truth — but *poi*, falsehood.

Greetings.

✤

## Translator notes

- Tamil `மெய்` carries both “body” and “truth”; the closing line explicitly exploits that double meaning, retained with the Tamil terms in the final couplet.
- Much of the poem depends on chains of puns among the five senses and on Tirukkural citations; English keeps the argumentative structure even where the exact sound-play cannot be reproduced.
- Parenthetical notices marking other poets' completed contributions are retained as source structure; their separate poems are not present in the canonical item.
