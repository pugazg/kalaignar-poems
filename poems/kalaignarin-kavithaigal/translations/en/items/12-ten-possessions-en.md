# Ten Possessions

<!-- scan 138 -->

### Ten Possessions

Presiding poetic address by
Kalaignar M. Karunanidhi
at the *kaviyarangam* titled “Ten Possessions,”
held at the Children's Theatre, Chennai
(Kalaivanar Arangam),
in connection with Tiruvalluvar Day,
on 16 September 1968.

★

Greetings.

On this fitting sacred day
when we hold a festival for Valluvar,
greetings to all who have come;
greetings to the reception committee;
greetings to the Tamil man
with clustered moustache,
pearl-like speech,
strong ideas,
seed of awakening —
to the man who feasts on Tamil
like a peacock dancing in a flower-garden;
greetings to the *Silambu Selvar*.

<!-- scan 139 -->

Valluvar spoke of ten “possessions.”
I was asked to show them
in peeled, juicy Tamil.
Again and again I scratched my head;
imagination would not come.
I was shaken.
Usually I take up the page,
write, and finish —
that has been my way until now.
But today I stand
like a wandering mendicant with his begging bowl,
after going house to house and gaining nothing.
You ask why the poem will not come.
What shall I say?
Tears come first —
but I shall still say it.

There is an old story that Pattinathar once burned a house,
saying one's own deed will burn oneself
and the rice-cake will burn the house.
Today some “Pattinathars” —
men of Madras city —
are burning many houses.
Do you think only houses are burning?
Are they not the stomachs of the poor?
“Our cot and mansion are only this mud house,”
they say,
living there with their little children.
Men with not a trace of pity,
men like poison-filled snakes,
men like frenzy-struck demons,
do deeds at which even Yama would recoil.
Curse them, Tamil Nadu —
curse them to be blown away like cotton!
Curse also the traitors who offer incense to them;
may they too be scattered to dust!
Curse them!
When I tremble with thoughts such as these,

<!-- scan 140 -->

how can poetry send its messenger to me?
In ungovernable anger I cry,
“Wretch — perish!”
But Valluvar, embodiment of love, restrains me:
“If, knowing wisdom, one learns to restrain oneself,
that self-restraint, knowing its strength,
will bring distinction.”
That is possession of self-control. What can I do?
People have lost cattle, calves,
even the single house they had,
and wander in the middle of the street.
I press down my heart and circle about,
wishing that those base men
who seeded this condition should fall.
“To hearts joined with darkness there is no grace,”
says the master of the Kural,
bestowing the possession of grace;
and then:
possession of love,
possession of knowledge,
possession of energetic action,
possession of character,
possession of modesty,
possession of forbearance,
possession of discipline,
possession of enterprise —
thus Valluvar sets out the ten possessions
and tries to console me.

But the people who possess no property,
trapped like mangy dogs
in the great tongue of blazing fire —
can their ruin be told with one tongue, sir?
Men with black tongues...
If I say, “Curse the scoundrels,”
Valluvar's sacred word joins mine:

<!-- scan 141 -->

“As the sun scorches the boneless worm,
virtue scorches the loveless man.”
Let us sing virtue and destroy unjust men;
let us rejoice in a new song of prowess,
saying we have driven wickedness
from Tamil Nadu that once lived outside the path;
let us bring down evil;
let us wipe out the creatures who set fires.

While four-and-six poets have come here
and make a river of song swell
like Palar, Thenpennai and Kaveri,
pardon me for speaking about the disorder in Madras.
My heart could not bear it;
that is why I lamented.

Tamil Nadu raises a statue to Valluvar and honours him;
it is also a warrior's house
that will put a nose-rope on those who leap against it.
In five letters we say, “Know Valluvar!”
Those who do not know him bark the two-letter “val.”
Put a leg between that “val” and reduce three letters —
we shall take up the *vāl*, the sword,
to smash deceit to powder!
If we take up one sword,
they take up two and bark “vāl-vāl”!
Someone may say,
“I made Valluvar ride the bus free of charge” —
not Ma. Po. Si. sitting here, no, no;
some friends say that after Valluvar's portrait was hung,
accidents became cheaper!

<!-- scan 142 -->

There are strange men even to insult Valluvar,
whose every atom of thought awakens feeling;
fortunately they fell in sixty-seven.
If Tamil cannot be the language of government,
and when asked, “Then should another language dominate?”
what sort of possession of knowledge is it to say yes?
What sort of possession of love for Tamil?
When I think that such men existed,
I wonder how Tamil Nadu could have remained
for twenty years like a painting without a wall.
That Valluvar himself survived among them
is enough to provoke wonder.
Is freedom only for a country that lived and fell?
Freedom also for great Valluvar,
so he may give light to fallen Tamils;
freedom also for flourishing Tamil.
With the whip-strike of freedom's light
let us drive away the dark crowd of destruction.

A mother said, “Give your head,”
and many Tamils gave it,
falling into the mouth of fire.
They were shot and went to the cremation ground;
their wives were left widows.
They were imprisoned, dragged the oil-press,
and died writhing to protect Tamil
like fish cast onto dry earth.
The party that committed all this,
the party that imposed repression —
yesterday I saw an English newspaper
scribble, in irritation, a half-formed piece saying
“not enough sacrifice.”

<!-- scan 143 -->

Where else in India, sir,
did eight people burn themselves alive?
Here in Tamil Nadu they fell into fire for language!
Our hearts boil at that newspaper's lament.
Soon there will be no one even to buy the paper
from the stall where it hangs!
Because it wrote demeaningly
of Tamils who gave their heads so Tamil might live,
I left today's Valluvar theme
and wandered toward those headless men.
Forgive me.

Alliteration and rhyme have not yet been born —
they are only ten months along!
The metre and prosodic bond
have not even had their baby-shower.
There are poets here whose names are inscribed in fame,
who can offer verse like ripe jackfruit rooted deep.
I shall remain among them
like the fibre in a flower-garland and gain its fragrance.
Had Tamil's old kings lived today,
they too would have given these poets world-renowned prizes.
My prize is only this:
to praise them so the town may know them.

Greetings.
Long live!

(In this *kaviyarangam*, poets Athmanathan, Ponnivalavan,
Kudiyarasu, Murugusundaram, Abdul Rahman, Poongundran,
Tamilappithan, Anandan, Muthulingam and Pulamaipithan
sang respectively on the ten themes:
possession of grace, self-control, love, knowledge,
energetic action, enterprise, discipline, character,
modesty and forbearance.)

## Translator notes

- The canonical title `உடைமைகள் பத்து` (“Ten Possessions”) is authoritative. The contents witness `உடன்பிறப்பின் பற்று` is a distinct source-valid variant and is preserved only in metadata, not merged into a hybrid title.
- The sequence around `வள் / வாள் / வாள்வாள்` depends on Tamil letters and sound. The Tamil forms are retained in transliteration where the English pun cannot reproduce the letter-count joke.
- References to the 1965 anti-Hindi agitation, self-immolation and political repression are translated as Kalaignar's source rhetoric; Phase 4 does not fact-check or harmonise those claims.
