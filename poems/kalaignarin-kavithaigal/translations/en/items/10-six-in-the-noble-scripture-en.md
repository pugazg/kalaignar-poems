# Six in the Noble Scripture

<!-- scan 116 -->

### Six in the Noble Scripture

A presiding poetic address by
Kalaignar M. Karunanidhi
at the *kaviyarangam* titled “Six in the Noble Scripture,”
held in Chennai on behalf of Tamil Vattam
on 10 August 1968.

★

Learned elders seated in the middle of this assembly,
great people rich in knowledge,
beloved mothers,
and poets of distinction who have come with Tamil-rich verse
to show six figures from the noble scripture, the Tirukkural —
I bow to you.

Twelve months, and four more beyond that —
sixteen months have passed
since I took office as minister.
In between,
at five or six poetry gatherings
they asked me to preside,
keeping me in office without letting me forget classical Tamil!
They did not invite me out of indulgence saying I was a poet.
Every day
countless people send slips;
I wander among petitions

<!-- scan 117 -->

with no rest.
Even a spinning top, after circling, lies down;
I roll from town to town like a kicked ball.
Though the body has grown lean,
life itself has not grown weak;
in the heart —

<!-- scan 118 -->

with Tamil giving flame,
how can weariness settle?
O charging-bull Tamil of language —
sea-rising, mountain-Tamil of delight —
grove-leaf, flavour-filled Tamil —
you climb my body, climb my life and keep me living;
I bow to you.

I have no wish to oppose grammar.
Grammar itself is the beloved child that sulks with me.
Even if not one of my songs agrees with grammar,
I have the courage that the song I give will not kill Tamil feeling.
Like a neem twig entering a sugarcane mill,
I have bravely entered this gathering of poets.
In this flower-garden where rare songs bloom,
your rare songs become flowers in Tamil's garland;
you are bees dancing around them;
I am only a splinter moving among you!
If an ant can move an iron pillar,
if an ant can make an iron pillar move,
then even my bare song can defeat you!

V. K. C. Natarasan, worthy organiser
who founded Tamil Vattam and conducts this Tamil festival,
said, “Come.”
Because he invited me with affection,
I said I must come lest he circle around me again.
So I came to preside.

<!-- scan 119 -->

A *vattam* — circle — is a name for classification,
a component that works to a plan.
The dot on a maiden's forehead is a circle;
the moon pouring light is a circle;
the far-raying sun is a circle;
and the marks some students receive are a complete zero!
For the act of love, the bed is a circle;
for the sweet-speaking child, the cradle is a circle;
for women's life, chastity's path is a circle;
for warriors' greatness, the battlefield is a circle;
for the farmer's plough, the field is a circle;
for a friend's relationship, the house of love is a circle;
for the ascetic's purity, the mind-cage is a circle;
for the base man's life, the circle is unknown —
only after seeing him do we arrive at the title “base man.”

First, under the title “Warrior,”
Ganesan will beat the drum.
“I shall die amid opposition,”
my leader's two eyes shedding tears;
“I shall beg for that death...”
Would the Tirukkural forget?
It did not forget.
The old *Purappaatu* is an opening song
where even deer-kind display valour
and put male-kind to shame.
Poet Sa. Ka. Anjar will speak;
he will sing the “Warrior.”

<!-- scan 120 -->

(Poet Kamban Adippodi Sa. Ganesan, M.L.C., completes his poem on the “Warrior.”)

Chairman Kalaignar:

What shall I say of the new speed he gave,
making even reclining letters leap and spring?
The Kural drew the grammar of honour for warriors:
“Like the kavari deer that cannot live when its hair is lost,
they give up life when honour comes under threat.”
Some who forgot it changed the line:
“Like the kavari deer that cannot live when life is lost,
they shave their hair when honour comes” —
and go bald in the election race!
“Better the spear aimed at an elephant and missed
than the arrow that killed a forest hare” —
this is honour's language.
Yet today's “Kural warrior” says:
“If a cat crosses the road, life is finished!”

If a warrior sees the field,
the farmer's son sees the paddy-field.
Going toward war is the warrior's work;
sleeping upon the haystack is the farmer's work.
The warrior's aim is to take the enemy's crown;
the transplanting field's aim is to take a bundle of seedlings.
Farmer and warrior have many likenesses;
there is only one difference:
the farmer makes people live;
the warrior makes people die!

To speak the farmer's full greatness
comes Sarathi, lover of the plough —
Parthasarathi,
turning from charioteer into charioteer of song,
driving a chariot of classical Tamil —
no, no, driving a plough!
He sings of the farmer.

<!-- scan 121 -->

(Poet Na. Parthasarathi completes his poem on the “Farmer.”)

Chairman Kalaignar:

Because we have said farming is foremost,
the farmer has no master;
and one who sings of the farmer has no master either — know it!
“When she sees those who sit saying ‘we have nothing,’
the good woman called Earth laughs.”
Seeing those who grieve, “I have nothing,”
she says:
“What is lacking, my son?
Look at the soil-richness in my cracked belly;
dig and water-richness will swell;
dig far enough and I shall give diamonds and gems.
Do not live in anger — increase the farming clan!”
Thus the earth-maiden stands laughing.
Farmer's son, understand the earth's smile;
labour and obtain new prosperity at once.

Next,
a friend comes.
Supporting the friends who come after,
loving them,
even while lying down to sleep I have kept them in mind;
even when they resolved to ruin me,
I approached with friendship and sorrow,
and because their fall could no longer be prevented,
all the friendships I ended make a long story.
In what should a friend be first?
In games of pleasure?
Or in the culture of rebuking frankly?
On what should a friend lead?
Professor Ezhil Mudhalvan will string letters into a garland;
a Tamil cuckoo in a grove of poetry.
Let him sing; let the friend speak.

<!-- scan 122 -->

(Professor Ezhil Mudhalvan completes his poem on the “Friend.”)

Chairman Kalaignar:

One chapter for friendship,
another for examining friendship,
one for old intimacy,
and separately, with care, chapters for “evil friendship” and “unfit friendship” —
perhaps Valluvar too suffered greatly through friends, as we have!
If a friend is as Ezhil Mudhalvan described,
what better emblem than Pisiranthaiyar and Kopperuncholan?
At a wedding, when the war-scroll arrived,
Mohammed Khan set aside honeyed feast,
joined Desing's army,
and became a page that will not fall from the history book.

We have stood among men long enough —
warrior, farmer,
and companion sowing friendship.
After so much time,
we need a woman to rest our weariness!
A poet comes to obtain one for us.
“When will she be born? When will she grow?
Will it be our grandson's wedding by then?” you ask.
Chandha-Kavimani Tamilazhagan
brings the woman he has given.
Look at her;
lend your ears to the Sindhu-Tamil
inside the little casket given by the rhythm-poet.

<!-- scan 123 -->

(Poet Tamilazhagan completes his poem on the “Woman.”)

Chairman Kalaignar:

The poet said of women:
if she says “rain,” rain will fall.
It is not right to place a chaste woman under that harsh test
and throw blame on her if rain does not fall.
The Kural that praises woman by saying
“rain falls when she says fall”
should establish the thought that
a good woman is *like* rain that falls when asked.
If an old idea raises its head, drive it away.

Next: why should renunciation follow woman?
Why should saffron hide all our eyes?
For some renunciates, when a woman arrives,
renunciation itself gets renounced.
There are many in Tamil Nadu who have taken two renunciations:
first renouncing the sweet householder's life,
living on fruit juice, milk, curd and water-pot;
then when the heavy body has grown fat,
openly renouncing renunciation!
The clever ones remain like fruit hidden behind leaves.
Is all this concealment proper?
Poet Thooran, rich in art,
comes near and opens his heart.
Do not think he renounces;
he opens the poetic heart.

<!-- scan 124 -->

(Poet Periyasami Thooran completes his poem on the “Ascetic.”)

Chairman Kalaignar:

Renunciation may be good,
but it must remain pure.
To continue merely as costume is evil.
For a heart that catches like camphor in fire,
renunciation is cotton in the Aadi wind.
By day he says life is poison;
by night he says panchamirtham is necessary.
The mind needs strength to condemn this without fear.
Do you not know the story of sage Viswamitra,
who gave up royal bed, jewelled crown and ornaments,
went to the forest,
and became Menaka's faithful friend?
“No shaving, no growing the hair is needed
if one abandons what the world condemns.”
One who rejects this is one kind among base men.
To sing their many kinds,
Tiruchitrambalam comes,
bringing sweet Tamil.

<!-- scan 125 -->

(Poet Tiruchitrambalam completes his poem on the “Base Man.”)

Chairman Kalaignar:

Good people who listened with smiling faces to all the poems spoken,
and my dear Navalar seated like the full moon!
I shall give the conclusion; after it you may leave.
A manly warrior,
a loving woman,
a friend rich in companionship,
a base man surrounded by harm,
an ascetic accustomed to penance,
a man who farms —
these six were chosen from the noble scripture
and they asked that explanations be given;
they left out the minister!
Perhaps they thought it improper to keep him nearby and criticise him.

<!-- scan 126 -->

“The minister is one who masters instrument, time, method
and the difficult deed to be done.”

“The minister is one able to discern,
act after choosing,
and speak decisively.”

“Beside a minister who plots ruin,
seventy crores of enemies may be preferable.”

Better seventy crores of enemies
than a minister who seeks degrading deeds.
Those who do not agree
with these banana-sweet Kural thoughts on ministers —
how can they attain distinction?
Leaving undying light,
they will hang like bats
inside the darkness of the hall.
For those of us who rule the land,
Valluvar said sleeplessness, learning and courage
must never depart.
Though what I learned is little,
I stayed awake all night to write this poem;
there was no other rest.
Because courage is present,
without ornament, metre, linkage or prosodic feet,
I have come to mount the poetry stage.
The Kural spoken for ruling the earth
I altered for ruling poetry!
So, people of the poetry gathering,
I ask you: draw the bow hard — but throw gently.
Why a separate explanation for a minister?
If he does not fear enemies, he becomes a warrior;
if he does not fear blame, he becomes a base man;
in showing love, he becomes a woman.

<!-- scan 127 -->

More than ploughing with a good plough,
if he ploughs well with words, he becomes a fine farmer;
though opportunities are many,
if he gives up the comforts that come, he becomes an ascetic;
and because he understands the Kural that says
“Friendship is not for laughter alone; its purpose is to rebuke when one goes too far,”
he becomes a capable friend.

For a woman, greatness lies in guarding herself;
for a warrior, in the honour-wound;
for a friend, in gratitude;
for an ascetic, in the grave;
for a base man, in every greatness he happens to see;
for the potter, an earthen pot!
My greatness is simply to be here among you.
If that greatness remains, it is enough for me.

Greetings.
Long live.

## Translator notes

- `அருமறையில் அறுவர்` refers to six human types drawn from the Tirukkural; **Six in the Noble Scripture** keeps that compact title without silently expanding it to “Six in the Tirukkural.”
- The poem repeatedly plays on `வட்டம்` (circle/association/zero and related senses), agricultural and martial vocabulary, and ministerial language; English preserves the sequence but cannot reproduce every sound-pun.
- Tirukkural lines are translated in the form required by Kalaignar's immediate argument rather than replaced with an external published translation.
- Parenthetical poet hand-offs remain source structure; the guest poets' full poems are not present in this canonical item.
