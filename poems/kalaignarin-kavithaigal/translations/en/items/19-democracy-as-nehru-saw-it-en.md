# Democracy as Nehru Saw It

<!-- scan 205 -->

A presiding poetic address by
Kalaignar M. Karunanidhi
at the *kaviyarangam* titled
“Democracy as Nehru Saw It,”
held in Chennai on 14 November 1970.

★

Navalar of golden character,
ministers of distinction,
poets who have come carrying Sangam Tamil,
learned elders —
accept my greetings.

I bow at the feet of our Anna
who sleeps beside the Bay of Bengal,
and begin this presiding address.

With the punnai tree spreading shade,
with Mother Tamil's lap giving comfort,
with Chennai city shedding light,
the Tamil Government that strives
to drive away former darkness
strings a poem in Muthamil
for Nehru,
elder son of the great Gandhi;
in the arena of poetry
it garlands the Pandit
who gathered fame in the arena of the world.

<!-- scan 206 -->

I entered a garden full of flowers
and found a scene waiting for me;
I turn it into poetry for you.

Champak whispered something
into the ears of the smiling jasmine;
the two ran to chrysanthemum
and spoke into her ear;
then all three murmured to mullai,
and the four began walking
the whole flower-garden together.

Whom were the flower-women seeking?
Were they searching merely for fibre
to bind themselves into a garland?
I wondered.
They spread fragrance in the breeze
and searched and searched;
I could not tell for whom.

“My knees hurt,” said Mullai.
“My feet are red,” said Champak, showing her teeth.
Jasmine and Chrysanthemum too were greatly troubled.
Then, from a corner of the garden,
a sound of lament came.
The flower-women cried, “There! There!”
and flew toward it.

“O lordly flower! Mountain of fame!
Have you gone, my beloved?
O king! King of principle!
Where is the Death that robbed you from me?
You lifted me, kissed me,
and placed me upon your heart —
and today you have left only me
and gone so far away.
What justice is this?
You gave honey to my lips;
in return

<!-- scan 207 -->

I gave you mine — have you forgotten?
You held me to your chest
until the flood of happiness overflowed.
Was your heart stone,
to leave this woman?”

Hearing the cry,
the fragrant flower-women drew near.
Rose stood there,
weeping like a sluice-gate burst open,
on the point of fainting.
Jasmine and Mullai came beside her:

“Why, Rose, are you still crying?
A good man lived so the world might live;
a strong man lived to gather us close.
Will what is gone return?
Stop lamenting.
Queen of flowers — show us a smile!”

They consoled her,
but beautiful Rose found no consolation
and sobbed:

“I called dew the spit of the night;
I called my lord's kiss nectar.
That day he gave me nectar
and stirred desire in me;
today he has left me drenched
in this spittle-dew — what shall I do?
The uncrowned king will never wear me again;
whenever I think of it,
thorns pierce my whole body.
What use is this redness
to one with no smile in life?
What use is fragrance
to one with no joy in the heart?

He sat upon India's throne;
I sat upon the throne of his heart.
He is sleeping; he will rise,
come down the steps,
and call this maiden.
Before then,
in the theatre of his heart—

<!-- scan 208 -->

I put on my red dress to dance.
But before I could,
the monkey-hand of Time
turned his ‘garland’ into a funeral garland
and turned my life into night.

Go away; leave me alone.
On the sacred man's birthday,
to make the river of my tears
an offering at his feet —
that is my duty.”

Hearing Rose's words I too grew weary.
Thinking of her withering
from separation from her king,
I turned back without speaking.
Today I came without plucking any flower,
even if my wife's hair should lose its fragrance.
If I pluck a flower today,
it is only to string a garland
for the great hero Nehru.

He tore down the flag of the white rulers
who tore away our lap and seized our freedom;
he held high the flag of liberation,
a warrior of principle.
To receive blows,
to endure repression,
he laboured tirelessly
along the path laid down by the Mahatma — a lion.

He spent the beauty of youth
inside the darkness of prison — a sacrificer.
His anger could crackle like mustard in hot oil,
yet, we say, he poured that oil
into the sacrifice of freedom.
Though born amid mountains of money,
he stood as a mountain of character
that embraced the poor.

The spinning-top is a figure for swift labour;
the Pandit's labour would defeat the spinning-top.

<!-- scan 209 -->

Thunder in his speech;
youth throbbing in his writing;
a measure of honey in his manners;
an explosive roar in action;
tenacity in his nature;
the freedom flag in his hand;
a little hair upon his head—
(do not look at me and ask what survives here!)

Because he stood face to face
against all that opposed justice,
he became *Nehru* — the one to whom things “occurred” in this play of `நேர்/நேரு`.
When great enemies crossed India's long border,
people worried, “What will happen?”
He wiped the grief from their hearts and said,
“Nothing will happen while I remain” —
and so he was Nehru.

He asked a child,
“If there is courage and purity of heart,
will suffering happen?”
The bud-and-flower answered,
“Only joy will happen, Uncle!”
Thus he became “Nehru Mama.”

His own position on religion and belief in God
was distinct,
yet he governed along a good path
without wounding the minds of others.
At the proper time he wrote in his will
that no religious ritual was needed after his death,
and shed light for the world.
He took non-alignment as a good principle
and shaped a policy suited to the words
our Poongundranar uttered for the world to praise:
“Every place is our town; everyone our kin.”

<!-- scan 210 -->

The strange pattern of time
may seat a donkey as king of the forest;
in an auction some may squat at the summit of a temple tower.
But Jawahar did not become leader of the Indian world that way.

Salem mango,
Chennai seashore,
Korkai pearl,
Kollegal silk,
Tanjore temple,
cool-water Cauvery,
Kanchi at the loom,
Pandyan of broad shoulders—
though differences of thought existed,
when Nehru died,
Anna, who had known the prison cell,
shed tears and wept in grief.

In a land that honours dignified politics,
let us praise Nehru, equal to the eye, in song.

“Army comes leaping —
enemy comes scooping —
firebrand comes —
black mark comes —
Chinese thorn comes,”
some mocked;
yet he said dawn's Venus would come,
plucked the foe away,
and today lies in sacred sleep —
Nehru lies in sacred sleep.

Our *Kalingathu Parani* tells
of mothers beating their breasts and crying
before sons lying with mouths closed,
asking whether any wound marks the jewel-like lips;
of the demonic dance that is the face of war,
of dogs and jackals feeding,
of battlefield cruelty
where fruit, flower and tender bud all fall together.

<!-- scan 211 -->

From that Kalinga,
King Ashoka took up the path of virtue after embracing peace.
Along that moral road,
to protect the world,
Lord Nehru clipped the wings of vultures
and released a white dove into the sky.

“Practise the foremost virtue;
help establish just justice;
let equality be the substance — democracy;
beat the drum and proclaim it.”
This is the poem of the art-rich bard
from wave-beaten Puduvai,
sung to transform our condition.

Jawahar cried, “Democracy, democracy!”
Yet we shall not forget
that some acted to make their own solitary rule flourish.
So that money-rule might fall in a moment,
Nehru, leader by character, strove too;
but time was not enough,
and Time itself would not wait.

If my poem continues longer,
time will not tolerate it
and the poets will not tolerate it either.
So I end this opening poem
and call the first poet.

A male poet greatly eager
to sing of women's rights,
a warrior-poet who has given
many clear-Tamil songs
for the army guarding the rights of the soil:
let him say women must possess
not merely the right to serve food,
but the right to serve in the army;

<!-- scan 212 -->

let him say we will not accept
new rights only in dress.
Poet of freedom,
my handsome poet,
give us a tasty song-*appam*;
let the audience eat it like a home-made *appam*.

(Poet S. D. Sundaram completes his poem on “Women's Rights.”)

**Chair — Kalaignar:**

Next:
he asks for freedom of speech.
It is his habit always
to speak in the middle of songs anyway;
so now he asks for freedom of speech
for himself alone in the *kaviyarangam*!

Some today ask for the right to abuse;
because of that I fear
the right of Tamil culture itself to breathe may disappear.
If friend Subbu has to ask,
“Time is up — will you give me the right or not?”
and I still have not given it,
that is my fault.

A sprig of coriander scents the curry;
Kothamangalam Subbu gives fragrance without confusion.

(Poet Kothamangalam Subbu completes his poem on “Freedom of Speech.”)

<!-- scan 213 -->

**Chair — Kalaignar:**

Next:
he asks me for freedom of writing.
He does not know my condition.
*Parabrahmam*, *Thookkumedai*,
the Rising Sun that gives the light of art—
I have lost many writings like these;
he does not know the story
of how even their first letters were banned.
Yet he asks me for freedom of writing.
Because I am now in a place that can grant rights,
I urge Murugu Sundaram to write his poem.
Come — pour Tamil honey.

(Poet Murugu Sundaram completes his poem on “Freedom of Writing.”)

**Chair — Kalaignar:**

Next:
with creative verbal grace,
a gait like the flow of the Cauvery,
a pure purpose and subtle intelligence,
the poet Rahman strings verses.
He asks now for the right to vote / the right of the word — `வாக்குரிமை`.
I shall not say,
“Take it and then give it only to me”;
if I said that, it would no longer be voting right.
Come, friend.

<!-- scan 214 -->

I have granted the right to vote.
We shall give travel expenses for the song you sing;
only do not ask for a sum for the vote!

(Poet Abdul Rahman completes his poem on “Voting Rights.”)

**Chair — Kalaignar:**

Next:
call him a king,
or call him a thundering drum — neither is wrong.
Though he wandered making playful contact
among those who write in coarse style,
through the friendship that rubs close
he returned again with incomparable affection
to this humble man who bears government responsibility:
Kaviyarasu has returned.

And as soon as he arrives,
he asks for property rights.
Poor man, wounded heart!
There is justice even in his asking:
ten or twelve children!
Never mind — I grant property rights.
Let it grow; come and give.

(Poet Kannadasan completes his poem on “Property Rights.”)

**Chair — Kalaignar, concluding:**

In this poetry arena
we have granted the rights the poets asked for.

<!-- scan 215 -->

Understanding the condition of the nation,
to cherish and protect good rights,
we shall extend a hand for relationship
and raise our voice for our rights.
With that cry let us disperse,
and bow to Nehru,
the complete human being.

Long live!

## Translator notes

- The floral opening personifies Rose as Nehru's bereaved beloved and uses garland/dew/thorn imagery as memorial rhetoric; the English keeps the allegory rather than converting it into biographical exposition.
- Several passages exploit `நேர் / நேரு / நேரும்` and `வாக்கு / வாக்குரிமை` (word/voice/vote) sound relationships. Tamil forms are retained or explained where English cannot reproduce the wordplay without inventing new source meaning.
- `பரப்பிரம்மம்`, `தூக்குமேடை` and `உதயசூரியன்` are preserved as source-named writings/works in the freedom-of-writing passage; Phase 4 does not silently substitute outside bibliographic titles.
- The Nehru, China-border, non-alignment, religion, democracy, war/peace and political-rights passages are translated as Kalaignar's source claims and commemorative rhetoric, not independently fact-checked or harmonised.
- The guest-poet sequence remains distinct: women's rights, speech, writing, voting/voice rights and property rights.
