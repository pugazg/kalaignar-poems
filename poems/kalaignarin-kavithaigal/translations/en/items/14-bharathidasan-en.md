# Bharathidasan

<!-- scan 155 -->

### Bharathidasan

Presiding address by
Kalaignar M. Karunanidhi
at the *kaviyarangam* titled “Bharathidasan,”
held at Bharathi Kala Mandram, Bombay,
on 8 December 1968.

★

Friends in Bombay who guard me always
as the eyelid guards the eye!
Brave men who pour out blood like oil
to protect Mother Tamil, cool as moonlight!
Warriors who, when told “cut it and bring it,”
will bind it and bring it back!
Friends who call me here again and again
and rejoice like a poor man
opening a box and looking repeatedly at the money inside!
You stand on the soil of Maharashtra,
land of Shivaji, that mountain-mouse turned great warrior,
with unwavering love and integrity.

There, in Tamil Nadu, is sea-singing Mamallapuram,
art-singing epics and beauty-singing sculptures;
Thanjavur has wet fields — *nanjai* —
and there is a temple there to Siva who swallowed poison.

<!-- scan 156 -->

Madurai once produced so much red paddy
that even tying elephants to thresh it was not enough;
there, because the fish ruled,
there was a fish-emblazoned flag,
and there still towers the temple of Meenakshi.
There are Tirunelveli and Salem,
Courtallam and Coimbatore.
Because life's troubles drove you,
you came to Dharavi to release your great sighs.
Though you live in Matunga,
your whole mind is there, in Tamil Nadu.
I say this as though keeping it ear-to-ear:
for India's unity,
and for Tamil Nadu's rights,
we have adopted both as high principles;
let us work to multiply their benefits.

With this introduction I begin.
Yesterday Ranga said he had repeatedly and painfully invited me:
“Come to Bombay.”
Did he invite me after suffering,
or suffer after inviting me? I do not know!
Still, having undertaken the task,
we have mounted the stage to complete it.
Elders of Bombay who increase flavour through classical Tamil!
You have created a *kaviyarangam*
in the name of lion-poet Bharati;

<!-- scan 157 -->

you have founded a golden Tamil forum.
Poets who have mounted the stage
to thunder jewel-like verse!
Can you stand after climbing a hill?
Can you stand on a hill?
Did I ever say you could not stand
on a hill of fame?
Have I said it even today?

They praise Bharati as a “great poet” — *mā-kavi*.
I do not accept it!
Was Bharati a poet made of ground flour — *mā*?
No, no — was he a *paniyaram* poet?
Was he a poet who filled prosody and ornament to the brim?
He was indeed *Mahakavi* —
a lion-great poet,
a head-poet,
a head-poet of innovation,
a poet of national liberation.
With turban twisted,
punching moustache,
a sun-bright gaze that made enemies tremble and run —
Bharati!
Head —
*pa* —
hand —
with turban twisted and fist clenched,
that moustache and gaze making enemies quake!

“You fools who roam searching,
saying there are a thousand gods” —

<!-- scan 158 -->

have you seen the anger in that song?
“We shall dance, we shall sing the *pallu*;
we shall dance saying we have attained joyous freedom!”
Before freedom arrived he declared it with certainty,
a charging bull, a king whose language thundered.
And there was a younger one to him,
like the younger Marudhu.
The moment Bharati said, “Sing,”
he sang:
“Wherever I look, there is Sakthi, brother;
the seven seas are her colour.”
Bharati praised him as a brave poet.
He shortened the far-travelling name Kanaka Subburathinam
and fitted himself perfectly with the name
“Bharati's servant” — Bharathidasan.
He tightened his war-band to enter the field and protect Tamil.

Upright gaze,
compressed brow,
voice of a waterfall,
moustache like a sheaf of paddy.
In writing poetry he learned Bharati's alchemy —
look at this man:
he learned the alchemy of *adhirasam* too;
how much sweetness lives in his song!

He takes us to the slopes of *Sanjeevi Parvatham*
and makes us listen to musical lovers' talk.
The woman says, “I shall give you a hundred kisses without hurting you.”
The man replies, “Then give me one kiss as the advance!”
Is that not the argument of *adhirasam* sweetness?
Was that not always his way?

<!-- scan 159 -->

“Who owns all those labouring shoulders
that plough and plough,
make the little village, the bunded field,
the river and the well-cut canal yield their grain?”
Is that a question mark — or an exploding volcano?
Are these lines not enough
for the world to garland him as the Revolutionary Poet?
“If this country exists for one man's need alone,
it is better that such a country perish.”
Is this not the song of a whole human being?
The man who hears it and weeps is half a man,
a deficient man,
a half-and-half man.

“The world is in a dark room, brother;
even the man who says caste exists is himself still here!”
He begins so,
and ends:
“Some people roll everything they can get into their own hands.”
He is a rolling poet — a cigar-rolling poet;
a poet who overturns —
a poet who turns upside down every superstitious habit.
Noise, uproar, agitation, procession!
Would not the Pondicherry cuckoo sing
so that the world might live in peace?
“Let us make a new world;
let us uproot the evil world that wages war.”
He calls the world as a poet of peace:
“Is it good that the world flourish by peace,

<!-- scan 160 -->

or good that it weaken by growing religious division?”
He asks, melting like wax in fire
before Gandhi's anguished idea.
“The liar is a coward;
the man who plunders another's land is a giant rat;
he who seeks welfare for all the world's people is wise.”
A poet of ideas — a somewhat dark-hued poet;
a poet of time — a poet of all three times.

What a wonder, the waterfall-song
he found as a form for delightful Tamil!
“When the mullai bud sees a guest coming,
it blooms like the face of a gentle woman — what a wonder!
Whether it sees someone known or unknown,
smiling is simply its habit.”
Hear another:
a woman pleasing to the eye dances;
Dasan loads sweetness into language:
“Anklets coo at her feet;
her lotus hand floats in the sky marking rhythm;
her beckoning finger is a fresh champak bud;
a red coral stalk that captures the eye.”
And another:
“Nothing was caught in his throat;
yet the husband gave a little cough.
The woman came near and said, ‘Dear?’
Would the dear man sleep then? ‘Sit near me,’ he said.”
His own special teasing!
The flavour belonging to his poetry!
They call him the poet of simile —

<!-- scan 161 -->

can one choose only one or two examples?
“The darkness loosened its bundle
like lime mixed into a tub of indigo.”
“When rice is served,
let the poured ghee run down
like sap along a bending banana-stem.”

Revolution? Peace?
Surging simile?
Ideas? Honey that intoxicates through Tamil?
Whatever anyone argues is greater than another,
I will not agree;
I will say each surpasses the other!

How many books he gave!
He lit the Family Lamp in a Dark House,
poured *Isai Amudhu*,
and won us the *Pandiyan Parisu*.
We have the *Kurinji Thittu* too.
While conducting the magazine *Kuyil*,
with *Azhagin Sirippu* he captured the Tamil movement.
He gave peace a sound verdict.
Then, longing for love,
he left the poetic maiden to pine
and parted with death's unexpected kiss.
He flew away saying, “I shall see my teacher Bharati.”

What do you think the two poets,
meeting in the world beyond death,
would have said?
Bharati, overwhelmed, dances:
“People say we are not in Tamil Nadu today —
shall we believe it?
‘When one says the words “land of classical Tamil,”
joyous honey pours into the ear.’
I waited in pain for someone to turn this song into action.
Today,

<!-- scan 162 -->

have you seen the wonder, brother?
Anna gave our forefathers' land the name Tamil Nadu!
We live there — is it not true?”
Bharathidasan greets him and replies:
“The pulp inside fruit,
the juice inside mature cane,
the honey in the dew-wet flower,
the sweetness in boiled syrup,
the milk the rich cow pours,
the cool tender water the coconut gives —
all these I call sweet;
yet Tamil I call my very life.”
“I wrote so.
Today Tamils everywhere take Tamil as their very life
and labour to spread it in all eight directions, sir.
We have not died; we live in the homeland!”
“That is true,” Bharati says,
embracing his own Dasan,
and laughing a Bharati-laugh.
Together they sing:
“Our life and our wealth —
blow the conch that they are undying Tamil!”

Let us join them in song,
see an advancing Tamil Nadu,
remove the foul caste-feud,
and hold festivals for mighty Bharati and his Dasan.
When we hold the festival,
let us hear their festival-ideas.

<!-- scan 163 -->

First, a poet comes to sing on “Simile.”
My friend, my special dear friend,
Anandam — a poet whose Tamil flows like the Porunai river,
who says even a golden flower has fragrance.
A useful tool for my teasing,
who will testify for me like a lizard on the fence,
yet is a mountain of character with no creed of beating his chest for wages.
For the first time he has come to Bombay;
yesterday he flew in an aeroplane for the first time too.
When he sat down neatly I told him, “Put on the seat belt.”
He looked straight at me and tightened the belt around his waist!
The air-hostess laughed until her ribs hurt
and quickly fastened the aeroplane belt.
The old proverb says, “Like a villager seeing an elephant.”
Change it after seeing him:
“Like a villager seeing Bombay.”
The moment he landed he stood open-mouthed,
looking at the sky-high buildings,
looking at the airport;
in doing so he even lost
a costly object he had in his hand!
This is that poet.

<!-- scan 164 -->

He will sing all the beauty of the heart-winning Poet-King's similes.
My guileless friend will give us Tamil in a flood,
like water plunging into a hollow:

(Poet Anandam finishes his poem on “Simile.”)

Chairman Kalaignar:

Next, on the beautiful theme of “Joy,”
our dear S. D. Sundaram sings.
The poet whose dream said, “Let joy flourish on earth.”
In politics he stands in the opposite row,
but unlike some, his “opposition row” is not Satan's row!
He will create a new row for Mother Tamil.
A worthy man who writes verse without a row of liquor;
simple dress, simple gait,
a new-minded heart that will not let old ideas bend it.
With lines sharp as tamarindless medicine,
he corrects dirty minds that have never bathed.
Usually his song is public poetry;
today he has been caught here to sing inner poetry.
On the theme “Joy,”
my friend, come and offer your garland of letters:

(Poet S. D. Sundaram finishes his poem on “Joy.”)

<!-- scan 165 -->

Chairman Kalaignar:

Next, on “Womanhood,”
comes Ganesan,
a lofty Tamil man of truth,
to offer distinguished verse.
This Chettinad worthy will not lend without interest;
a sacred body without a shirt,
a form of fame that does not care a shirt-button for opposition!
He wears khadi,
a body made splendid by khadi.
He will wrestle — wrestle any enemy who insults Tamil.
He poured sweat for the paddy of freedom;
today he comes to add flavour to words.
With poetic Tamil that can give life even to stone,
he will give us a ripened song:

(Poet Sa. Ganesan finishes his poem on “Womanhood.”)

Chairman Kalaignar:

Next, should we not add flavour
to an assembly already gathered?
For that comes Abdul Rahman.
His words explode like a gun loaded with shot,
and distil flavour.
He is a skylark;
his home is Vaniyambadi.

<!-- scan 166 -->

If Bharathidasan was servant to Bharati,
this man is servant to that servant
in crafting Tamil verse.
Once a lamp inside a pot,
from now on he is a lamp set upon a hill;
refined gold,
a veena that wins back fame.
He will give a song of flavour.
Taste it — I shall not interrupt:

(Poet Abdul Rahman finishes his poem on “Taste.”)

Chairman Kalaignar:

Last night in a Bombay hotel
I slept wrapped in my blanket.
A lovely woman of the dream-world seized my hand:
“I waited for you, my dear — have you come?”
“What is your name, lady?” I asked.
With delight she said, “Muthamma.”
“Dream-beauty, explain one thing;
do not misunderstand me —
your face looks like Muthusamy!
Has only the inner masculinity changed into womanhood?”
She burst out laughing
and showed me the moustache she had hidden with her hand!
I recognised the trickster at once.
With no way to escape, the maiden said,
“Dear, I am ready to entrust my heart to you;
but this moustache ruins my desire.”
“If worship could send a moustache away, would it go?
Flower-like girl, be reborn and come as a new woman,” I told her.

<!-- scan 167 -->

She vanished; I woke.
Now you know how yesterday's comic dream arose.
Tamil-loving Muthusamy had announced in this very forum
that he wished to become a woman quickly and marry me,
and that if there were another birth
he was resolved to marry me.
Remembering that, I slept —
and so I dreamed!
When revolutions happen in which Mariyappan becomes Mariamma,
if Muthusamy became Muthamma,
our cabinet would have two women!
Enough of that.

I call my friend Muthu
to sing of the revolution made by the Poet-King's songs.
Do not stay away today out of shyness at yesterday's memory;
come among us and give good Tamil:

(Minister Ma. Muthusamy finishes his poem on “Revolution.”)

Chairman Kalaignar's concluding address:

Bharati's Dasan asked,
“Would you sell your eyes to buy a painting?”
and said women were “a heap of guava fruit.”
He asked:
“Even a horse locked alone in a stable
has permission to neigh —
should a woman in the home be forced into modesty and fear?”

<!-- scan 168 -->

He said,
“To attain love is the nature of life;
can it be imprisoned in a cot?
Maiden, begin!
Joy — plunder, plunder, plunder in the mango grove!”
He asked,
“Let us open a road for love
and find a door to close the road to pregnancy — what fault is there?”
When did he ask it?
Long before the present.
Today the government campaigns for family planning,
saying, “Do not become like Kuchela.”
The great poet preached that child limitation was necessary
thirty years ago as a progressive idea.

A man, father of four or five children,
lies trembling on the verandah.
His wife comes near.
Listen to how the poet's language flows like the Ganga:
“My dear wife came softly to me;
‘Are you tired?’ she asked,
touching me with her flower-hand.
‘There is frost in the street,’ she said.
I said yes.
I understood her heart — what need was there for words?
Ten months later, one day at noon,
just as a creditor came and tormented me,
while my whole mind worried over the eldest girl's fever,

<!-- scan 169 -->

an old woman came and said,
‘By Muthalamma's grace,
at an auspicious hour your wife has borne a child.’
Contagious poverty, the creditor's trouble,
the storm that keeps striking — and child after child!”

That Bharathidasan honoured womanhood.
Looking at human beings trampled like earth,
he said, “Do not sell yourself!”
“Even if enemies offer me crores, I will not touch it,”
he sounded like a war-trumpet.
He stood as a good ray of light in black darkness.
He called the man who merely lies about without serving “a tortoise.”
He lit garland after garland of poetic lamps.
He became Bharathidasan, heir to Bharati.
For a leader, a poet, or any great scholar,
the true qualification is the lineage that remains after him.
Today wherever we look
we see Bharati's lineage,
the descendants in Bharathidasan's path.
Poets speak word-pearls — poets with many teeth,
and toothless poets too!
When it comes to the vote,
people give it to Bharati and Bharathidasan
with no other aim.
Will the two poets come to rule our kingdom of poetry?
Why should they still need to come and rule?
They already rule our hearts.
Long live they!
Long live their fame!
Greetings!

## Translator notes

- The source repeatedly exploits the sounds of `மா`, `மகாகவி`, `அதிரசம்`, `தாசன்`, political “rows,” clothing, caste and place names. English retains the underlying argument and keeps Tamil terms where the joke would otherwise disappear.
- Quoted lines from Bharati and Bharathidasan are translated as embedded source quotations and are not silently replaced by outside published English translations.
- The Bombay/Muthusamy dream and family-planning passages are preserved as Kalaignar's 1968 stage rhetoric, including humour that reflects its period; Phase 4 does not modernise or censor the source voice.
