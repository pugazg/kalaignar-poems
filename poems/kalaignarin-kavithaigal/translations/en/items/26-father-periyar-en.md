# Father Periyar

<!-- scan 254 -->

Introductory presiding poem by
Kalaignar M. Karunanidhi
at the “Father Periyar” *kaviyarangam*
held by the Tamil Sangam
at Salem Nehru Kalaiarangam
on 11 January 1974.

Honourable ministers,
district comrades — greetings.
To the poets of prosperous Tamil Nadu,
to the elders of Salem, rich in affection,
accept my heartfelt greetings.

Though he knew well
that I have no rest,
our friend sent an order
in the form of a request:
I must come and conduct this *kaviyarangam*.
He is a friend of the plough,
a true friend who labours.

There are those who say,
“We shall overturn us like a boat” —
and traitors who climbed aboard this very boat,
clung to office,
and now say, “We shall grind it to dust.”
There are sinners who declare,
“We shall become the sword
that strikes his back.”
While I am deep in thought,
working to break the conspiracy
of all who surround us in opposition,
he orders me to write poetry!

<!-- scan 255 -->

From yesterday morning until midnight,
more than eight important events in Coimbatore!
After all that,
will the spring-eye of imagination open?
Even so,
like a flag that moves with the wind,
I move at my friends’ command
to sing poetry.

I remember Ganapathi,
whose support and urging,
because he believed I possessed the power to write verse,
brought me to the chair of this poetry assembly.
I remember R. V. Ganapathi;
I continue this beautiful Tamil *kaviyarangam*.

First let us worship
the *sāmi* who let us live and grow upon this earth —
Rām-sāmi,
Periyar Ramasami!

Great people who have come
to sing and to witness this poetry assembly!
Was it not Periyar
who made Tamil consciousness —
which had lain stretched out
like the reclining lord of Srirangam —
rise with a thrill and thunder in all eight directions,
performing many deeds of courage?

Street stage,
art stage,
music stage,
cinema stage —
were they all once nothing but
“ammāmi, aththimbēr?”
and *jalatharangam*?
He changed that,
made them Tamil stages,
stages of our own.
Is it not fitting
that we hold a poetry assembly for him?

That is why the poet-king Bharathidasan sang of him:

> Like crops to the farmer,
> like milk to children,
> like curd to the hungry,
> like a mother to the poor,
> like a faultless penance to the worthy —
> to all people born in the Tamil land,
> he was like life itself.

<!-- scan 256 -->

“If you build a house
only to stuff straw inside it,
why build the house at all?
Why not simply make a haystack?”
Was the E. V. R. who said this merely an atheist?
No, no!

“He is nature’s son,
the bridegroom who wedded this soil,
a messenger of the future greatness of Tamil Nadu” —
so Va. Ra.,
that astonishing great man from the *agraharam*,
once drew him beautifully in an essay.

Like a singular light
born in molten gold,
he surged upward;
the red sky itself,
where the red sun rises,
seemed to flee in shame.
Periyar rose,
and crores of young bulls of modernity
stood firm,
saying blood itself was the stake.

If a limb is diseased,
we agree to cut it off
to save the life.
But when human society suffers
from the diseases of caste and religious division
that cripple life,
we hesitate to remove the disease.
“Is that right?” he asked.
The lion’s voice sounded;
the little foxes howled —
was that not the story that unfolded here?

He had a beard like Tagore of Bengal,
a gaze upright like a raging tiger,
a body with the presence of a red hill,
and in eyes white as conches,
two black-bee pupils —

<!-- scan 257 -->

light lived there until death.

A spinning top rests
once its turning is done.
But even after he had become very old,
he kept circling like a top.

Like a volcano,
a scorching flame,
a drama of nature,
a thunderclap that made opposition tremble,
a great torch of ethnic consciousness,
a cleaving sword that cut down humiliation,
an Ingersoll who denied even God,
a Socrates of Athens who never stopped speaking,
a Voltaire with a diamond heart
who kept asking “Why?” —
so lived our Father Periyar.
Today he has fallen
into the lap of death.

When Mahatma Gandhi said prohibition was needed,
Periyar cut down the coconut palms
standing thick in his own grove
so that liquor pots would not be filled.

Whom did we call the Hero of Vaikom?
As though saying,
“Place the knife at untouchability’s throat!”
he entered the field like a striped tiger,
won victory,
and received from the people of the world
the title **Vaikom Veerar**.

He was a helmsman of the Congress,
a commander for the victory of the khadi movement,
a man of action who stood on Gandhi’s path.
Then the time came to fight within the party,
and at the Kanchipuram conference
he walked out.

V. V. S. Aiyar was called
a distilled patriot.
But when caste feeling invited conflict
at the celebrated Gurukulam in Cheranmahadevi,
when one law was practised for one caste
and another for another,
could Periyar’s heart bear it?
He bent the bow for battle.

<!-- scan 258 -->

He began to make history.
As its result,
he became the father of the Self-Respect Movement,
the guardian leader of the Tamil people —
Periyar became
a man of a new path.

The Justice Party
had set out to perform great work
for the welfare of the backward classes.
But because it remained
at the feet of princes such as Pithapuram and Panagal,
and in the walking-stick grip
of inexhaustibly wealthy men,
he transformed it.
With the aid of the great Anna,
he succeeded in bringing
a party that had lived in mansions
to the people.

When deceitful schemes spread like nets,
trying to make Tamil people
wear Hindi like a monkey’s garland,
was it not Periyar
who cut through them
with an unafraid heart?

Is there a hamlet or village
his feet did not reach?
Even believers who would run a league
at the sound of his name,
once they heard him speak,
would reel as though intoxicated
by a Tamil song.

A rain of reason would fall;
beauty would flow;
darkness would pass.
Clear speeches would shine,
their power flashing —
like the roar of a sea breaking its sluice,
like clouds flowing over a mountain peak.

A rain of reason would fall!
Arrogance would perish!
The system of slavery would end!

<!-- scan 259 -->

When Ayya spoke,
to the Kazhagam people
it was like eating *athirasam*;
to the wicked,
like an atomic bomb exploding!

I joined him,
accepting his leadership,
back in 1938,
when I was still a student in Arur.
Later, when I was attacked in Puducherry,
Ayya himself applied medicine
to my wounds.

During the days
when I studied lessons of principle
in the Gurukulam of the *Kudiyarasu* office,
I never imagined
that I would one day become
the head of a Tamil government,
or that I would give,
with state honours,
my final salute
to the father who raised me.

I still remember the song
I wrote in that Erode Gurukulam:

> The ancient world,
> praised and worshipped for so long —
> look, it shakes beneath the Erode earthquake!
> The word E. V. R.
> is a cyclone rocking
> the very pedestal of Aryanism in this land.
> Let his white beard merely move;
> let the sign in his eyes be seen —
> there are Black Shirts ready,
> swords that return
> only after cutting down cruelty.

I remember it even today.
Periyar did not die young;
he lived to a full old age
and then took rest.
Yet if the Taj Mahal suddenly sank into the earth,
would we console ourselves by saying,
“After all, many years have passed
since it was built”?

<!-- scan 260 -->

If the great tower of Thanjavur tilted and fell,
would it be easy to bear
merely by saying it was very old?

He stood as high as the Thanjavur tower;
he made history like the Taj Mahal.
He will live, he will live —
that is my blessing.

To sing of the Periyar who lives on,
I now call,
one after another,
the poets of rich Tamil.

## Translator notes

- `தந்தை பெரியார்` is rendered **Father Periyar**. `தந்தை` is an honorific and political/familial epithet in the source, not a biological description.
- The scan-255 sequence `சாமி → ராம் சாமி → ராமசாமி` is name/ritual wordplay. The English keeps `sāmi` and the staged Rām-sāmi → Ramasami turn visible rather than inventing an English homophone.
- `அம்மாமி அத்திம்பேர்` is retained as a source-specific Brahmin-household/stage register; the point is the displacement of a culturally exclusive stage idiom by a Tamil public stage.
- The Vaikom passage contains a sound-play between `வைக்கம்` and `வை கத்தி` (“place the knife”). The English translates the rhetorical force and records the wordplay here.
- References to Congress, khadi, the Cheranmahadevi Gurukulam, the Justice Party, anti-Hindi agitation, caste, religion and Aryanism are translated as Kalaignar’s source-framed political history; Phase 4 does not fact-check or harmonise those claims.
- Karunanidhi’s 1938 association, Puducherry injury, `Kudiyarasu`-office apprenticeship and later state-honour reflection are preserved as first-person source claims.
