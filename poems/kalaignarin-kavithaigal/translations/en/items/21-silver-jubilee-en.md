# Silver Jubilee

<!-- scan 218 -->

Extracts from the presiding poetic address by
Kalaignar M. Karunanidhi
at the *kaviyarangam* held in Rajaji Hall
on 15 August 1972 by All India Radio, Madras,
for the twenty-fifth anniversary — the Silver Jubilee —
of India's independence.

To everyone gathered in this assembly,
to the poets of beautiful Tamil,
to Navalar and the ministers,
and to Silambu — bone in form, yet love embodied —
my bowed greetings.

People of this sacred motherland,
you have accepted me as your servant.
I bow to you today,
promising that I shall act
without breaking the word I have given.

Young woman of India!
With flowers in the hair you have gathered up,
with darting fish-like eyes below,
you stand in a new picture,
marked with dots and patterns,
writing out for us:
“Silver Jubilee of Freedom.”
Young maiden! Mother of awakening!

<!-- scan 219 -->

Beyond the Vindhyas,
in heroic Tamil Nadu,
I bow in greeting
to the warriors who raced like horses in competition
and leapt ahead of one another
into the fire of sacrifice.

For twenty-five years
we have watched this lamp burn.
We stand worshipfully before our grandfathers,
our forebears,
who poured their blood as oil
and lit that lamp.

In a lovely grove where flocks of cuckoos sing,
shall we listen to the forest shriek of owls?
Shall a woodland where deer roam,
a garden where peacocks dance,
be turned into an elegant pavilion for monkeys?
Shall we build a house for the breeze to enter
and then let fire come in?

Our country — our people — our government.
Even if, like the face of the moon,
there are small blemishes,
it is our country — our people — our government.
We shall tell the world in surging Tamil
that lion-hearted warriors and golden women
will guard it.

It is impossible today
to speak of every one of those people.
So the friends at the radio
asked that a counted few be apportioned among poets.
Their invitation fell upon my ear like honeyed sound,
and leaving behind a world of clamorous noise,
I came running to hear the ordered music of poets.

A Bharati has come to sing of Bharati.
The sweet Tamil songs of Subramania Bharati

<!-- scan 220 -->

may be heard anew
through the new song of Suddhananda Bharati.
Though he is one who has renounced everything,
there is one attachment he has not renounced —
his attachment to Tamil.
Though he has learned many languages,
he has not forgotten his mother tongue.
He cannot shine here
if he does not sing of Bharati here.
So now he sings.

*(Poet Yogi Suddhananda Bharati sings on Bharathiyar.)*

Next —
V. O. Chidambaranar,
our noble one who ran a Swadeshi ship from Thoothukudi
until the British trembled,
then became destitute,
and pulled the oil-press in Coimbatore prison
while guarding his principles.

We already earned honour
by raising a statue to him in Chennai.
On the fifth day of next month,
Prime Minister Indira Gandhi
has gladly agreed
to unveil another statue in Thoothukudi.
Our postal department too
has thought of commemorating his surpassing fame.

Like Chidambaranar, who went to prison in sacrifice,
our friend Sundaram,
who also knew imprisonment and sacrifice
and whose mind creates classical Tamil verse,
has come to offer a poem.

*(Poet S. D. Sundaram sings on V. O. C.)*

<!-- scan 221 -->

Next —
V. V. S. Aiyar,
a warrior of action and truth,
was caught in a furious whirl of water
and one day disappeared from life.

Who is this young shoot
who has come today
to make that freedom fighter rise and walk before us again?
The highly gifted Soundara Kailasam,
who sings fitting Tamil like an Avvai of our own age,
has sent her poem gracefully to us
through her daughter.
Young woman, sing the lullaby you have learned.

*(On behalf of poet Mrs Soundara Kailasam, her daughter sings on V. V. S. Aiyar.)*

Next —
Subramania Siva,
the incomparable martyr
whose lion's voice made those who ruled us tremble;
who, despite the cruelty of disease,
travelled the motherland on foot day after day
to break the Mother's chains;
who was imprisoned in the Tiruchirappalli fortress;
who was like a rising sun that drives away darkness.

Who is fit to sing of him?
Can food without salt be satisfying?
Can a *kaviyarangam* without Kothamangalam Subbu have flavour?
A sprig of coriander scents the curry;
my friend Kothamangalam Subbu's poems

<!-- scan 222 -->

spread fragrance everywhere,
and sweeten the hearts of the Tamils who hear them.
At this age, instead of sitting idle chanting “Siva, Siva,”
you have come eager to sing of Siva.
If you say you will not mount this assembly,
will the assembly not lose its savour?
Do not let it turn tasteless.
Give the gathering flavour, Subbu!

*(Poet Kothamangalam Subbu sings on Subramania Siva.)*

Next —
attachment is not wrong; it is not sin.
But when attachment ripens into frenzy,
it can bring strange and dangerous consequences.

There was a young man who believed
that unless imperialism was driven out of India,
his own body could not rest;
who was bold enough to think
that to drive out one foreign power
it could be right to seek the aid of another;
who became an elder guide to Netaji Bose
and even tried to obtain German military assistance.

He was Champakaraman Pillai,
born in an honourable old family
in the Tamil soil of Nanjil,
who died abroad in fame,
yet possessed the pride to wish
that after his death his ashes
should be scattered in Tamil Nadu.
To sing the valour of that martial Tamil
comes Vaanampadi,
a poet from the flower-rich banks of the Kaveri,
a cultured friend of long standing,
who gladly offers us a feast of verse.

<!-- scan 223 -->

*(Poet Vaanampadi sings on Champakaraman Pillai.)*

Next —
violence and terrorism
are not good methods; they harm a country.
The noble path laid down by Gandhi and Anna
is non-violence.
Yet some among our forebears argued
that in those days,
when foreign rule had to be removed
and the fetters of slavery cut,
seeking another path was not necessarily wrong.

Among those who believed so
Vanchinathan grew like a storm.
In those days a British official named Ashe
was the representative of colonial rule in Tirunelveli.
When the train halted at Maniyachi,
Vanchinathan's group surrounded it as planned.
Vanchi pulled the trigger of his handgun;
the Englishman died.
Driven by a sense of self-respect,
Vanchi then shot himself and died by suicide —
and his family suffered.

If the *Kanchi thinai* is about guarding,
did not Vanchi too guard freedom?
Without forgetting that history,
Arunachalam comes now to give it voice.
Let us hear his poem.

<!-- scan 224 -->

*(Poet K. C. S. Arunachalam sings on Vanchinathan.)*

Without looking to wife,
mother or family,
believing it a duty
to guard the honour of the motherland,
he entered the field of struggle with a sacred heart.

To volunteers who gathered rank upon rank
as people gather to watch a temple chariot,
he was a fitting leader,
a warrior who gave courage,
a generous man who roared;
unafraid of authority or domination,
he held the flag he had taken up,
refused to loosen his grip,
and though beaten,
would not let it go —
and died there.

Kumaran who does not die,
Kumaran who guarded the flag,
a pole star shining in the sky of sacrifice —
Chidambara Raghunathan will show him to us here
with grace.

*(Poet Tho. Mu. Chidambara Raghunathan sings on Tiruppur Kumaran.)*

Next —
we have written down seven names here.
Were these alone the people who brought us
this immeasurable freedom?
How many more great figures,
like the finial of a temple tower,
like a lamp upon a hill,
won immense fame?
How many martyrs remain unseen,
like anchoring roots hidden under the soil?
Naidu, Naicker, Mudaliar —
those of an earlier generation in Tamil Nadu

<!-- scan 225 -->

who sowed the seed of longing for freedom —
Varadarajulu, Periyar E. V. R., Thiru. Vi. Ka., Jeeva —
are all these to be put on a list of people whose names are unknown?
If we continue without fatigue,
will we not find countless martyrs?

To sing generously of their fame,
my close friend,
a lover of Tamil,
wherever he may be,
with classical Tamil surging like the Aadi floods,
a king of poets whose beautiful verses multiply,
Kannadasan —
we have all come patiently,
knowing what is in his mind.

O parrot of freedom!
O cuckoo floating upon comforting words!
Let us hear your gentle music.
Come, come — give us your poem.

*(Poet Kannadasan sings on the unnamed martyrs.)*

“Painted groves, to make you beautiful,
how many comrades once
poured out their blood at your roots?
Lotus-filled tanks, in the time that gave you to us,
how many of our pure brothers
fell away and vanished —
shall we tell the world?”

These are lines of the Revolutionary Poet Bharathidasan.

<!-- scan 226 -->

Holding those lines as good and true,
let us pass victoriously through
the Silver Jubilee of national freedom,
and with hearts rejoicing
wish that the incomparable Golden Jubilee
may come swiftly.

Accept my greetings,
my salutations,
and my thanks.

## Translator notes

- `வெள்ளி விழா` is rendered **Silver Jubilee**, the source's twenty-fifth-anniversary frame for Indian independence.
- The translation preserves the source's named freedom-fighter sequence and its guest-poet hand-offs rather than treating the passage as an external historical summary.
- The Vanchinathan, Champakaraman Pillai and other historical descriptions are translated as Kalaignar's source claims; this layer does not fact-check or harmonise them with outside accounts.
- The final Bharathidasan passage is kept as an embedded quotation and not absorbed into Kalaignar's own voice.
