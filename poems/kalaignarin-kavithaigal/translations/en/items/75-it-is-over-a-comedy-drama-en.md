# It Is Over—a Comedy Drama!

<!-- scan 455 -->

It is over, mother—
a comedy drama!

A thousand thunderous salutes roar and roar—ah!
Processions march to the beat of drums, march and march.
Welcome arches, like hunchbacks with broken backs,
along every road of the city
bow and offer praise, praise and praise.
Flags and festoons hide the clouds above,
swaying gently as though holding up a parasol.
Nine elephants stand holding garlands, stand and stand;
at the mahout's command
they place them around the neck, place them and place them.
The new hall gathers still more splendour, splendour upon splendour;
a flood of light like the moon in the sky
spreads everywhere, spreads and spreads.

The Queen reads a speech prepared for young people,
reads and reads—
but the crowd gathered there
falls about laughing, laughing and laughing!

> “Young people! Spears of the younger generation!
> This very day, take your vow and set out!
> Enjoy the springtime called life—
> and dedicate at least a little of your youth
> to service of the country!”

As soon as this passage is heard,
the hall shakes and shakes;
the great crowd bursts into loud laughter
and rises, rises and rises!

<!-- scan 456 -->

⟦continuation from previous page⟧

The startled Queen asks what has happened,
asks and asks.
Someone there says,
“Madam, the speech you read is wrong,”
says it and says it.
The Queen flares up:
“Why? How?”—question after question.
The man trembles like a startled deer,
trembles and trembles:

“I am sorry, Mother!
Your speech was prepared
to offer greetings to the young.
But those summoned to this durbar
and gathered here
are people advanced in age,
with hair gone grey!
Looking at them and addressing them as
‘Young bulls!
Indrajits!’—
that is why mockery and ridicule have overflowed.
Who could do anything but laugh, Mother?”

The dignitary who gave that answer slips away,
slips away and slips away.
Poor lady—the enraged grande dame
embraces sorrow, sorrow upon sorrow.

It is over, mother—
a comedy drama!

## Translator note

The source deliberately repeats verbs such as “march,” “read,” “laugh,” “ask” and “tremble” to create a stage-farce rhythm; the English preserves that repetition rather than compressing it. The poem calls the central figure simply the “Queen,” so no outside identity is inserted.
