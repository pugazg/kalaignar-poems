# Anna, a Poetry Assembly

<!-- scan 230 -->

Presiding poetic address by
Kalaignar M. Karunanidhi
at the Anna Memorial Day *kaviyarangam*
held in Rajaji Hall, Chennai,
on 4 February 1973.

★

I bow at the feet of our Anna
who sleeps beside the Bay of Bengal,
and begin this presiding address.

Eight poets have come,
accepting my chairmanship,
to weave silken poems.
In a Tamil Nadu where some roam about hurling abuse,
let us praise these weavers of poetry.

It has been more than a year
since I last mounted such a stage and sang.
So when Arivazhagan and Veerasamy called,
I did not say, “All right, let us see.”
I said instead, “Is that so? I am coming,”
and agreed at once.

<!-- scan 231 -->

I did not come because
at any cost you must hear my poem.
I came to hear the fine verse
of these poets of rare distinction.

I had no time to write poetry,
yet the artistic heart would not leave me alone.
From morning there were meetings with visitors;
then the launch ceremony
for a trial boat-run on the beautiful Cooum;
by four o'clock another event at the harbour
in the name of our Bharati;
and between all this,
every telephone bell that rang demanded an answer.
Somehow I scribbled something
and came carrying a letter called a poem.

We are holding a *kaviyarangam* for Anna —
but do you know, friends?
Anna himself is a *kaviyarangam*!
On the arena of the world
he made an arena of flowers;
he is the divinity who sleeps in sacred Arangam.
I worship that divinity.
I sing facing that direction.

You may ask:
“What offering did you take for Anna's worship?”

For those who once wriggled on the earth like worms,
we replaced huts
and built houses with upper floors —
is that news not a flower
laid at Anna's feet?

To make it our goal this year
to restore sight to a hundred thousand people —
is that not a plantain fruit
placed in Anna's sanctum?

For leprosy-afflicted beggars,

<!-- scan 232 -->

we established clean homes —
is that not incense and lamp
fit for worshipping Anna?

To tell Anna that no small town,
large town, hamlet or village
shall henceforth remain without electricity —
is that not the light of camphor?

Anna dearly loved betel leaf and areca nut.
Tell him of the law granting house-sites
to agricultural labourers,
and his mouth will redden
as though we had offered him betel.

We devised a plan to make the Cooum fragrant, Anna —
that is the kumkum
with which we decorated your threshold.

Saying these things,
yesterday morning all of us walked together,
bowed at Anna's tomb
where the breeze sings its lullaby,
and returned with every eye turned into a pond,
while Tamil welled up like a river in flood
and hundreds of thousands sang his qualities.

Our Anna,
king of consoling speech,
great ruler of the South,
who unfolded ideas like a lotus in mud
and said every lamp we light
must cast its light upon the poor,
seemed to tell me:

“My younger brother,
enough of praising and glorifying me!
Some people wander about slandering our Kazhagam.
Is it not their crime
to pour oil and set a poisonous wick alight?
Today every rice-bag belly
swings a cardboard sword.
Until Death itself came asking for my life,
I allowed those who gather only to ruin us
no farther than the hall of my own house.
But you —

<!-- scan 233 -->

you led them all the way into the kitchen,
and today you are tasting the result!

What is done is done, younger brother.
All this hostility will fly away like cotton in the Aadi wind.
Work without forgetting the counsel I gave you:
turn opposition into dust with a dismissive smile.
Let them howl like owls with their booming noise;
let their arrogance and haste end in harm to themselves.
Show patience with the poise of Tamil culture.
Gather together the younger brothers
who keep me always in memory.
Call out our principles like the mango cuckoo;
the owls will disappear,
ashamed by the sound of their own voices.”

Thus our Anna gave us his blessing.

Born in Mamallapuram, a place radiant in the mind,
he became a Pallava.
Against conspiracies raised against him,
he became a Chola who could see the enemy's back.
In Kongu, a mountain of virtue,
he became Kumanan in generosity.
In the labour of nurturing beautiful Tamil,
he became a Pandya king.
In wearing the victory garland
and conquering the enemy's field,
he himself became the Chera.

Against those who cleverly claimed
that “namaskaram” was good Tamil,
he brought the Tamil word *vanakkam*
into everyday usage.
He changed an age
when people argued that the eye-bulging phrase
“vivaha subha muhurtham”
was the mark of the learned,
and blended lyre-like Tamil
into the resonant word *manavizha* — wedding celebration.
He planted such words
in the hearts of ordinary people
so Tamil once confined to poets might grow among all.

<!-- scan 234 -->

Was it not Anna
who turned *agrasanadhipathi* into *avai thalaivar* — chair of the assembly?
Otherwise one might have imagined
that the office of *agrasanadhipathi*
stood even above the President of the Republic!

When he mounted a stage,
it sounded like the music of a crown;
when he wrote upon a page,
it became scripture for Tamils.
To make the heads of his opponents
bend like stalks of ripened paddy,
our Anna had mastered the rule of words.
Even lion-like lawyers
could lose before him in argument;
Anna had no skill at all
in stubborn argument for its own sake.

“I bow my head before Kamban's Tamil,
but I do not accept the *Kamba Ramayanam*” —
with such courage and purity of heart
he brought Somesundara Bharathi into agreement.

Anna had not seen Nedunchezhiyan.
He had not seen Anbazhagan.
I can say it with certainty:
Anna had not “seen” them — at Thillai.
Yes:
he had seen them at Annamalai University,
in the city of Thillai,
where he delivered a speech of boundless fame.
Calling out “O fallen Tamil Nadu,”
he classified the greatness by which we once lived
and gave us a deep treasure-chest of ideas —
a chest of ideas,
not a broken-eared box
invented in the imagination of dark-hearted people
to overturn the Kazhagam.

<!-- scan 235 -->

It was a treasure-chest of thought,
a camphor chest given by the sage of Kanchi.

As Periyar's right hand;
as a motor-boat carrying the intellectually poor to shore;
as a warm sun that forgave the faults of the small;
as a master who could make fanatics submit;
he conducted politics with method and principle
and proposed a plan to end the begging bowl.
Some who once sat here with fortune
now go along the street abusing us.

If you quarrel with a boil,
it is your own face that is spoiled.
If you say “never mind” and apply *punugu*,
the boil may fall away.
To the boil that came to spoil the beauty of the face,
what harm is the fragrance of civet?
My friends may roar like tigers,
but if the foul boils can fall away
through the *punugu* called patience,
do not think of it as perfume —
apply it as medicine.

In the movement built through labour,
when there came a difference of opinion
with E. V. R. Periyar,
Anna separated in tears,
saying he would form another Kazhagam;
yet he thundered to the enemy
that we would remain like a double-barrelled gun.

But today some gather loose fragments,
run to slash the mother's belly with a drawn sword,
and fall into the ghostly lap of the enemy.
Those of such degraded character
will soon be crippled.

<!-- scan 236 -->

Whenever Anna waged a righteous struggle
against a ruling party,
he never had the habit
of sending cadres first to test the depth.
He himself entered the field first;
he himself went to prison first.

“The states must remain like deer,
and at the Centre there must roar a tiger
ready to pounce upon them” —
Anna did not accept such a policy for the Republic.
He demanded rights.
Some leap about without understanding
the harm that comes when the tiger's beauty
is defined as hunting the deer.
That was not Anna's policy.
He sounded the drum
for the need to decentralise power.
We shall march in formation along that road,
calling it the good road;
we shall cure the disease of domination.

About this man who was like a university,
poets now sing under many headings in this *kaviyarangam*.

❀

The poets then sang on the following themes:

**Mother Anna** — poet Erode Tamilanban  
**Leader Anna** — poet Ka. Vezhavendan  
**Guardian Anna** — poet Ma. Muthusamy

<!-- scan 238 -->

**Actor Anna** — poet Murugu Sundaram  
**Anna of the Film World** — Dr Avvai Natarajan  
**Chief Minister Anna** — poet Kothamangalam Subbu  
**Writer Anna** — Kaviyarasu Ponnivalavan  
**Orator Anna** — poet Abdul Rahman

### Kalaignar's concluding words

Mother! Beloved leader! Guardian of righteousness!
True actor! Peerless Chief Minister!
One who captured our hearts through writing and speech!

Did the child called Time
pluck you away and run,
saying, “You have ripened in wisdom”?
What can we do, Anna?

Thinking that you are still with us,
we shall defeat cruel deeds,
conspiracies and dances of deceitful hatred;
and to see the Divine in the smile of the poor,
we shall labour forever as we labour today, Anna.
There, in that smile,
we shall see you, Anna.

❀

## Translator notes

- `அண்ணன் ஒரு கவியரங்கம்` is rendered **Anna, a Poetry Assembly** to retain the poem's governing conceit that Anna himself embodies a *kaviyarangam* rather than merely being its subject.
- Item 23 intentionally owns scans **230–236 and 238**. Physical scan **237 belongs to item 24** and is not inserted here; this non-contiguous provenance is source-authenticated and must remain visible.
- Tamil/Sanskritised public-language contrasts such as `வணக்கம்`, `மணவிழா`, and `அவைத்தலைவர்` are kept partly in transliteration where the political-linguistic contrast would otherwise disappear.
- The `கண்ட தில்லை / தில்லைநகர்` passage depends on Tamil sound and spacing; the English explains its turn conservatively without rewriting the Tamil witness.
- Movement history, welfare claims and state-autonomy/decentralisation rhetoric are translated as source claims, not externally harmonised.
