# On the Path Called Life

<!-- scan 185 -->

A presiding poetic address by
Kalaignar M. Karunanidhi
at the *kaviyarangam* titled
“On the Path Called Life,”
broadcast by Chennai Radio
on 14 April 1970.

★

Greetings
to everyone who has come,
and to the poets who have come to shape verse.

The long world that had sunk in sleep
walks forward, showing the light of blue eyes;
year after year rolls on at full breath,
ready to stamp many seals upon time.
In the month of Chithirai
we see abundance smiling,
and we take pride in it.
The year that has gone was *Saumya* —
the year that gave our Saumyan to the Tamils.
Anna too wore “Saumya” as a pen-name
and piled up stories dripping flavour — then went, dead.
As “Saumya” disappeared and “Sādhārana” came,
so that

<!-- scan 186 -->

Saumyan has disappeared,
and this *sādhārana* — this ordinary man — has come before you.

Without an arena many people play round-games in politics;
without monkeys we see plenty of monkey-business in society.
Scratch one sore and it multiplies;
rebuke men of low quality and the same thing happens.
In such a situation,
when some cannot tolerate this ordinary man
coming to rule the earth,
what will become of me
if I also come to rule poetry?

In the old day,
one who poured oblations into a thousand sacrifices ruled;
in this day,
one who knows poetry and composes a thousand songs rules.
Can the mind tolerate it?
Is this proper?
We can stop it!
We can suppress it at once!
We can break it!
Break an arm,
cripple a leg —
so some may mutter.
But this ordinary man thinks instead:
“Will darkness recede and dawn arrive?
Can we still help the poor?”

If the monstrous demon-darkness withdraws,
if the evil attachment to private ownership falls away,
then the principles of an ordered common ownership
may dwell freely everywhere
in the lives of people who work the land —
so this ordinary man thinks,
to enrich the path called life.

<!-- scan 187 -->

Our policy says the path cannot be enriched through violence.
To remove the division called low and high,
we need calm light and peace.
Every revolution must be a revolution of the mind;
every uprising, an uprising within;
every awakening, in writing and speech;
every thought directed toward putting social justice into practice.
Had these existed,
a golden age of advancement,
an age of newness,
would have arrived long ago.

Is there a human being who has understood
what life itself is?
We say birds, animals, creeping things
“live.”
But of rational man we do not merely say he lives;
we say he conducts a life.
One who lacks the confidence
that we ourselves conduct our lives
is hardly human.
Many move life forward saying,
“Somehow we are passing the day.”

When two people meet in the street
and one asks, “How are you — well?”
will the answer ever be,
“Yes, I am well”? Tell me!
“Somehow I am here,” he drags out,
answers in great weariness,
and moves away from his friend.
Why this exhaustion in life?
He says the wife whose lips gave honey is false;
he says the child he gave her is false;
he says, “Ah! one must swim the ocean of family life.”
If you ask when all this begins—

<!-- scan 188 -->

I can tell the age without fail: after seventy!
Poor fellow—
yet you say even between twenty and thirty
there are young renunciates
who call pleasure itself the highest bliss of renunciation.
Except for one or two,
I have doubts about all of them.
They have not subdued the five senses;
I would say one or two senses are simply in famine!

Some others say in daylight,
“All this is false,”
and at night,
“All this is true.”

The strong man who thinks
he alone was born to live,
like a bird swooping low to seize fish,
makes a habit of torturing the weak.
It is good for him to reform himself;
otherwise the laws made to reform him
will reform him,
and stop the swagger of the wicked.

Kuppan, who bends lower each time he is struck,
believing his clan deity will help him,
bends and bends until a hunchback grows upon him.
Medicines that make the hunchback stand upright
will soon arrive —
in the new house where his clan god gets a lamp!

We have seen a crowd of children
scoop handful after handful of sweet sugar
into their mouths.
Some people, instead of enjoying life's sweetness
with a sense of measure,
turn their lives into mud trampled by cattle.

<!-- scan 189 -->

The tiger eats only as much as hunger needs;
the snake, even after hunger has passed,
swallows deer and lies stupefied.
Though we see both scenes in the forest,
those acting in life's drama still do not learn — what can we do?

“Death at six, death at a hundred —
let what must happen happen,”
says the lion-like hero who wages a righteous struggle.
“Death at six, death at a hundred —
let what must happen happen,”
says the one who ceaselessly consumes every pleasure.
He is no hero;
he is lower than an animal.

One who lives only for
his house, his wife, his children, his circle
is not conducting a life either.
One who says,
“Our country, our rights, our language —
I shall do everything I can to protect them all,”
that one is a public human being.

Therefore,
do not float in a jar of honey
like a bee drunk on nectar;
do not preach the renunciate's line,
“What do I need?”
do not squeeze the poor saying,
“Everything is mine!”

On the path called life,
art and education are signboards shown by poets;
office and devotion will be explained by bards;
fame and wealth will be poured out by poets.
First: education.

They say what one has learned is a handful of earth.
For the learned, earth is in the hand;
for the unlearned, I say, it sits upon the head!
From a handful of clay a pot may be shaped—

<!-- scan 190 -->

but the earth piled on the head
is not even fit for the kiln.

When they said, “Learn while young,”
why did some take it to mean,
“Pick up a stone while young and throw it”?
Kamban is called great in learning;
should one who is great in learning
become great in quarrelling?

If the uneducated are “people with two wounds on the face,”
why do the educated shine as people with thirty-two wounds?
Have you thought about it?

I am not one who saw college;
but stone has never soaked into my hand.
Poetry wells in the heart,
a little art wells there too;
fame rises on the earth.
Those close to me said
what I had learned in Tamil was enough
to rule the town called Tamil;
I agreed without heart.
Today, by your grace,
I have escaped!

On the path called life
there is no time for dry stories.
Let us hear Puviyarasu,
who has come to sing
of rich “Education.”

(Poet Puviyarasu completes his poem on “Education.”)

**Chair — Kalaignar:**

Next: office.
To remain in the forest for a lifetime is not so great;
to place one's lips on the tip of a wasp's sting
and endure the pain is not so great;
but to remain in this office—

<!-- scan 191 -->

that is the greatest trouble, O Kanchi Ekambane!

Those whose entrails burn
thinking, “I sit at the summit of the temple tower,”
should know that I do not possess even the happiness
of the flock of pigeons
that build nests and live upon its upper storey.
Offer me sympathy;
keep a sense of friendship.

Office is a cruel knife:
for some it cuts throats.
But if office is accepted as responsibility
only to help others,
can the same life-taking knife
not be used to save many lives?
Call office the knife that cuts fruit;
it is evil when it becomes a sword of vengeance.

King Louis, who strutted arrogantly
thinking he would remain in office forever,
had his head shaved clean off by history,
and a new order arose — we shall not forget it.

If, through good servants fit for Valluvar who speaks virtue,
one rules the earth while rejecting slander,
poets will gather to speak of our merit
and the nation will bless us.

One who clings to office like a monitor lizard
later says, “Never mind the lizard — just release my hand!”
Some began biting because it tasted sweet as sugarcane,
then slipped away saying,
“Good heavens, it burns salty!”

When the poet Rahman is here to sing of office,
I myself have already sung too many lines.
This is not infatuation with office;
it is infatuation with song!
Let us see honey of office drip
from the tongue of our friend Rahman,
one of the good poets who rain upon our country.

<!-- scan 192 -->

Split the Tamil word `பதவி` (*padhavi*, office):
remove the letter `ப`,
and it cries `தவி! தவி!` — “suffer! suffer!”
That is why we suffer when there is office!
Let him sing;
then we shall see.

(Poet Abdul Rahman completes his poem on “Office.”)

**Chair — Kalaignar:**

Next: wealth.
Scholars say it is called *selvam* because it keeps going —
“we go, we go,” *selvōm, selvōm*.
Would it not also be fitting to say
it is called *selvam* because it sits in the iron chests of rich men
until the creeping `செல்` eats it away?

They say Lakshmi is the goddess of wealth;
yet a crore of Lakshmis labour in poverty.
The same Valluvar who said,
“The wealth of wealth is wealth of hearing,”
made us understand too
that this world does not exist for one without material means.
If you are satisfied saying,
“Ear-wealth is enough,”
Radio will fill your ears with four cries of “Long live!”
and tell you, “Go safely,”
without even giving travel expenses!

The wealthy should be like Seethakathi,
like the generous Tamil Kumanan,
like Pari,
and smile in this world.
That is the grammar for those who possess wealth.
For one “possessed by wealth,”

<!-- scan 193 -->

the grammar is Valluvar's line:
“Even if one begs water for a cow,
there is no degradation worse for the tongue than begging.”

When Tamilannal is here
to pour a rain of wealth upon all red-tongued poets,
it is not proper for me
to seize all the wealth myself.
I call him: give us wealth, Tamilannal!

(Poet Tamilannal completes his poem on “Wealth.”)

**Chair — Kalaignar:**

Next: art.
...
After the *kaviyarangam* ends,
one says “the work is to *kalai* — disperse — the hall,”
and offers thanks!
`கலைத் திலகம்` is a title;
but split `கலை - திலகம்`,
and the maiden becomes a tree struck by blight!
Art joins politics in this age;
but when art is made a hostage,
what politics can there be in any age?

In *Kalithogai* there is a love-scene
of art giving its own shadow to the hostage;
and we see every day in the art world
the sight of one fearing one's own shadow.

When the moon's phase decreases,
its light decreases;
we see that every day in the sky.
Where art is full,
there is compassion in the heart,
and the quality that feels another's sorrow.
When an artist sees a poor person in tears,
he sees a character he himself has created,
understands that person's state through imagination,
and performs the duty of removing the sorrow.

<!-- scan 194 -->

Those who said “art for art's sake” have fallen silent.
They understood that beautiful light exists for the house,
not merely for the lamp.

There are so many kinds and counts of art:
beautiful art,
fine art,
dance,
song,
the sixty-four arts —
apart from northern art and southern art!

My friend Vaali has come
to take pearl-like Tamil
and offer all these arts here.
To prove it wrong to say
“Vaali is one with a tail,”
here comes a Vaali without a tail.
If everyone with a tail is Vaali,
are all people with legs *kālis*? No, no!
Let Vaali come,
stringing garlands of thought
in jewel-coloured Tamil.

(Poet Vaali completes his poem on “Art.”)

**Chair — Kalaignar:**

Next: fame.
When crabs raise their heads on the seashore
and you stretch out a hand and run to catch them,
they vanish into hiding before you know where they went.
Fame is like that.

<!-- scan 195 -->

Seek it and embrace it — it runs away.
Give it a crore — it withers.
It does not swoon before mansions;
it does not hesitate when hidden away.
It clings to the person of integrity;
it kicks the arrogant away.
It scatters flowers at the feet of the self-sacrificing;
it bows to the feet of the courageous.
On the greatness of that rare fame,
the worthy poet Mariyadas
will compose in sweet Tamil.

(Poet Mariyadas completes his poem on “Fame.”)

**Chair — Kalaignar:**

Next: devotion.
If devotion means setting out
fruit piled in the palm, *appam*, flattened rice and puffed rice,
those who speak of God will not all agree.
Going on pilgrimages
to holy places, temples and sacred tanks is devotion;
placing holy places, temples and sacred tanks
with grace inside the mind is devotion too.
Kannappa lifting his eye
and placing it upon God is one devotion;
Sakkiyar closing his eyes
and worshipping God by throwing stones is another.
If one cries, “My Lord! Shiva!”
and praises God
but never once looks up at the poor,
where is devotion?

<!-- scan 196 -->

Ekalavya offering his thumb
was the old devotion to a guru;
“Hey, guru! Offer me your hand!”
is the new devotion.
The maiden calling her husband god
is old devotion;
poor husband — kneeling before the maiden
is the husband's devotion!

There are many devotions within devotion.
To let loose their whole sequence
in powerful words,
Mudiyarasu comes.
I invite him without saying,
“Finish quickly, king!”
Come!

(Poet Mudiyarasu completes his poem on “Devotion.”)

**Chair — Kalaignar, concluding:**

You who have come to the path called life,
you who have seen many roads:
receive education — receive it without blemish;
receive office — receive it with culture;
receive wealth — receive it with character;
let art spread fragrance through your life;
let fame gather without your going in search of it;
and if the flood of devotion flows toward
country, language and people,
God's grace will reach you.
He is in the smile of the poor.
Do not forget this.

Greetings.
Long live!

## Translator notes

- `சௌமிய` / `சௌமியன்` and `சாதாரண` / “ordinary man” form a calendrical-name and political-self-description sequence. The English keeps the source names rather than replacing them with external calendrical glosses.
- The poem repeatedly builds arguments out of Tamil morphology: `பதவி` → `தவி`, `செல்வம்` / `செல்வோம்` / `செல்`, `கலை` and Vaali/`வால்`, among others. Tamil forms are retained in notes or in-line where the humour depends on sound.
- The references to private ownership, common ownership, social justice, law, office, religion and devotion are translated as Kalaignar's source rhetoric rather than neutralised or independently harmonised.
- Embedded Tirukkural, literary and devotional references are kept visibly distinct from Kalaignar's surrounding commentary; the English does not silently rewrite the final-cleared Tamil wording.
