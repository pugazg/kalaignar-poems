# Pongal Festival

<!-- scan 267 -->

Presiding poetic address by
Kalaignar M. Karunanidhi
at the Pongal-festival *kaviyarangam*
held by the Madras District DMK
at Kalaivanar Arangam
on 14 January 1975.

Greetings,
people of deep affection,
elders,
and good young treasures
who hold the mother who bore you
and the golden land of your birth
as one!

There was no room
for all of us to be born from one womb;
that is why,
born from the wombs of a crore mothers,
we became sibling Tamils!

To all of you
I offer Pongal New-Day greetings —
accept them!

Poets who have come
to take colourful Tamil
and paint pictures of thought in verse — greetings!

Under the heading Pongal,
you have come overflowing to sing;
I know it well.

<!-- scan 268 -->

Did I say
you came after letting food boil over?
I say you came
with feeling boiling over.
Is that not the truth?

Let us praise the sun!
Let us praise the sun!
The sound of the anklet rings;
it sweetens the mind.
A thousand moons,
a thousand women,
a thousand suns,
a thousand men —
they praised the sun,
they praised the sun.
Tamils became thousands of crores;
they sang many a prefatory song.
Praising the sun,
they gathered at Pongal;
praising the sun,
they danced at Pongal.

Mango-leaf festoons,
turmeric and plantain,
temple drums and festive designs —
they beheld them all.
“Pongalo Pongal!
Pongalo Pongal!”

Though they lived divided
among Kurinji, Mullai, Neithal,
Marutham and Palai lands,
Tamils lived understanding
a common culture.

Though called the ancient people
who arose before stone and soil,
they learned to clear forest into country,
to dig ponds and increase prosperity.

A bow appears on their shoulders —
it displays valour.
Rice appears in their fields —
it displays agricultural skill.

<!-- scan 269 -->

New words appear again and again —
they display the wealth of the language.
Not only letter and word:
Tolkappiyan, like ripened grain,
gathered grammar too
for the prosperous life of Tamils.
With guava-fruit Tamil,
Valluvar wove the Kural for the world.

Let us rejoice
as though rain had fallen after drought,
hearing the poetry of truthful speech!

The curved anklet,
Manimekalai of distinction,
Cintamani,
and Kundalakesi
became ornaments of Mother Tamil
through the work of poets of that age.

How many books!
How many poets!
A treasury for people
who learned and understood,
who felt and knew —
so Tamils lived in those days.

One day,
thinking of the great books of ancient Tamil,
I lay down upon my bed.
A woman lay close beside me,
without the least shyness,
and embraced me.
Though I made no attempt
to gather her into my arms,
I had no mind to push her away either.
Let her embrace me, I thought.

Then another woman arrived —
a rainbow dancer,
a sorceress who gathered music
like a skylark.
“What is this?
Two women — one bed?”

<!-- scan 270 -->

You ask?
Be patient.
The first woman's name was Sleep.
The other was the Dream Woman.

Every scene I saw in that dream
was a Tamil scene,
the splendour of Tamils.

In a hall,
a musician pours a rain of song;
the people sway
like bees drunk on nectar.
As the auspicious concert ends,
some among the audience eagerly ask:
“Sing ten more songs — *Pattuppāṭṭu*!”

The exhausted musician clears his throat:
“I shall sing.
What sum will you give me in addition?”
A poet springs up and answers:
“We shall give you *Eṭṭuttokai* — the Eight Collections!”

The musician understands the play and protests:
“What justice is this?
For my ten songs,
only eight collections?
That is a *Kuruntokai* — a short sum — for me!”

“Oh! Do you want *Ainkurunuru* then?”
the poet snaps.
“I do not want one hundred;
I will not touch it, will not touch it.
Will you give four hundred, great poet?
And let it be four hundred in *akam*,
four hundred in *puram*,”
twists the master of music.

Four hundred in *puram*,
four hundred in *akam* —

<!-- scan 271 -->

“Enough talk of money for song!
We shall give fresh milk for your throat;
if necessary we shall give the *Muppāl* —
the Three Divisions — now sing!” they say.

“O poet who promised the *Muppāl*!
From now on my throat will speak
only for the *Porutpāl* — the division of wealth!”
With that the musician rises and leaves.

My long sleep broke;
I awoke because a *Nedunalvāḍai* —
a long cold wind — had struck me!

Every memory of the dream
was sweet as sugarcane within my heart.
Thinking of Tamil,
soaking my heart in Tamil —

our Tamils once exercised power
across the roaring sea.
Yet today Tamils themselves
have consented to erase
the nectar of ethnic culture,
the dignity of art,
and literature —
like holding dirty spit in the mouth,
brushing filthy teeth with it,
then rinsing and swallowing it
back into the body.

The dirt-filled gut must be cleansed.
For that we need a good washerman called medicine.
Bathing cleanses the outside of the body.
Inner cleanliness comes through medicine;
purity of the mind comes through truth.

Making a path for that truth,
placing explosives beneath the Vedic fortress —

<!-- scan 272 -->

for sixty years,
without fear,
our Anna's leader Periyar fought.

It is on the stove he lit
that today
Pongal boils over,
pretenders fade,
falsehood burns.

“A woman with ten heads,
twenty hands,
a mouth roaring like the sea,
hundreds of eyes,
the face of a cow,
the body of a human,
named Makara Sankranti,
with a mountain of a nose” —
that fabricated tale,
written to deceive,
vanished like a snake before thunder,
destroyed by the roar from Erode.

Following that,
our Anna saw Pongal
as a festival of ethnic consciousness,
as the great festival of the ploughing farmer!

❀

## Translator notes

- `பொங்கல் விழா` is rendered **Pongal Festival**. The poem repeatedly exploits `பொங்கல் / பொங்கு` — Pongal, boiling over, and overflowing feeling — so the English keeps “boil/overflow” visible wherever possible.
- The dream on scans 270–271 is constructed almost entirely from classical Tamil literary titles and number/price meanings: *Pattuppāṭṭu*, *Eṭṭuttokai*, *Kuruntokai*, *Ainkurunuru*, *Akanānūru*, *Puranānūru*, *Muppāl*, *Porutpāl* and *Nedunalvāḍai*. The Tamil names are retained because replacing them with plain English titles would erase the joke; short glosses expose the relevant wordplay.
- `ஞாயிறு போற்றுதும்` and the anklet imagery deliberately activate *Silappathikaram* associations. The English does not silently expand the poem into an external literary commentary.
- The spit/gut/medicine passage and the grotesque Makara Sankranti figure are preserved as the poem's rationalist polemic, not neutralised or presented as independently verified cultural history.
- The Periyar and Anna framing, including the claim of sixty years of struggle and the redefinition of Pongal as an ethnic/farmers' festival, is translated as source rhetoric.
