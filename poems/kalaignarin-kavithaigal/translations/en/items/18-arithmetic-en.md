# Arithmetic

<!-- scan 197 -->

A presiding poetic address by
Kalaignar M. Karunanidhi
at the *kaviyarangam* titled “Arithmetic,”
held under the auspices of the Salem Tamil Sangam
on 2 August 1970.

★

Bees gathered in Salem city
to taste sweet Tamil fragrant as cardamom!
Poets like red sugarcane,
who have become, with good character,
aerial roots for the extempore poet Bharati
and for Bharathidasan!

Friend Ganapathiyar,
a pure gem in a lofty, adorned face,
has founded this Sangam
so Tamil rain may fall upon the world;
like waves of a roaring sea,
he circles Tamilakam without rest,
pledging lifelong service.
He caught hold even of this spinning-top called me
and dragged me here,
determined to expose my poetic power in public.

When Kamban and Koothan lived,
there was no room for everyone one saw
to wander about calling himself a poet.
But because this Karunanidhi is Chief Minister,
no one has yet dared
criticise my poetry to my face.
So far, that is profit enough!

<!-- scan 198 -->

That is why this humble man too has become bold.
He invited me without quite saying:
“Come punctually by nine-thirty;
do not arrive late
and then speak elegantly afterward.”

Since this is a poetry arena on arithmetic,
I too, who keep accounts in everything,
shall collect travel expenses punctually —
not only for myself,
but for my whole poetic family.
Do not worry, poets!

In former times arithmetic stood first in education;
hence the teacher was called *kanakkāyar*.
Accounts are of many kinds,
and those who keep them are of many kinds.

The capitalist reading the worker's sweat —
that is the money account.
A partner asking wages for his affection —
that is the office account.
A woman marking lines of charcoal in the kitchen
for what remains unpaid —
that is the milk account, the curd account.
Poor child: the account the parents write out for school
so he may somehow escape —
that is the home account.
Political geniuses who care nothing for sin or merit
keep one account before ordinary people:
the vote account!

In the herd: cattle count, goat count;
but do not keep an account only inside the liquor pot!

From Adi to Thai is a year;
so for thirteen months,
on interest at one hundred and three per hundred—

<!-- scan 199 -->

“Altogether five hundred and three!
Pay our debt without delay!”
That is the interest account in Chettiar country.

The treasure of classical Tamil:
the Eighteen *Keelkanakku* works!
The accountant's son does accounts;
the son who goes to marry a woman
does another kind of account!

In that account,
saying twenty plus eighteen is a marriage account;
taking twenty and eighteen together is a love account.
Now: no more than two — that is the family account.
To compete with Kuchela in this count
is a cruel account!

Because the account he made went wrong,
the man called Hitler kissed suicide.
A tiger will erase the account calculated by a deer;
then a hunter will bring down the tiger,
asking, “Why did you make the wrong calculation?”
That is the world!

In this body, eight spans tall,
we move because strings have been tied without count.
Though we have only one tongue
that speaks honeyed words,
through talent we make it into two
and wander about double-tongued.

God has given all of us
two hands in proper count;
why then does God alone
carry the burden of so many hands?
Fortunately,
if humankind had been created
with hands beyond count,
one hand would print counterfeit notes,
one hand would adulterate goods,
one hand would drag young women—

<!-- scan 200 -->

one hand would betray,
one hand would steal,
one hand would hold a knife,
one hand would dare cut a throat.
Today these jobs are done one by one,
and the police we have
can restrain the offenders.
Otherwise,
all those varieties of hands would sprout everywhere,
kill in varieties,
revel and dance;
the country would convulse.

Human beings must live by measure;
so the old saying tells us:
even if you cast something into the river,
measure it first.
There is nothing without calculation.

We cry proudly that man set foot on the moon.
How many crores were spent — calculated exactly?
If even a tiny error arose in the scientific calculation,
would those feet have returned?
Would they not have scattered in space,
leg one way and head another?

If a woman said,
“My husband gave me kisses beyond count,”
and kept a recording beside the bed,
would not even that have a count?
Therefore, there is nothing without arithmetic!

And if I too speak without keeping account of time,
continuing to recite line after line,
the poets who came to sing of arithmetic
may become angry beyond count.

<!-- scan 201 -->

So I call them now.
First: multiplication.

The multiplication of wind becomes a storm;
the multiplication of seedlings becomes a crop;
the multiplication of sweet juice becomes flavour;
the multiplication of a river becomes floodwater.
What becomes of the multiplication of Murugu's song?
Gold,
flowers,
and new poetry!

Salem city has so many distinctions.
When we asked for an iron plant,
people once said, “Salem? A city?”
Today, for Salem to become a great city,
Servarayan hill has given iron ore.
Even if Tamils delight
in that hill-fruit
and in Salem's mango,
my friend Murugu Sundaranar asks:
“Can either equal my crimson-pomegranate poem?”
He will give Tamil for us to drink.
They say the heart melts before *Thiruvasagam*;
his *vāsagam* too is like that.
Friend Murugu!
Tomorrow is Adi Perukku;
for now, sing and make it multiply!

(Poet Murugu Sundaram completes his poem on “Multiplication.”)

<!-- scan 202 -->

**Chair — Kalaignar:**

Next: subtraction.
A thief enters the house while everyone is awake;
only afterward, when he is caught,
does he “wake up,” staring about,
and claim it was merely a game.
My friend Tamilannal has spent his life fruitfully
trying with full breath
to subtract deeds like these,
destroy evils,
condemn them fiercely.
He will speak on “Subtraction”
in classical Tamil overflowing with delight,
mixing honey,
adding taste,
multiplying goodness,
and dividing it out.
Without wasting time,
I clear the road for him.
Come and give it!

(Poet Tamilannal completes his poem on “Subtraction.”)

**Chair — Kalaignar:**

Next: division.
The poet who comes to gather
and offer the poetic nectar called division
is one who lives by the path laid down by the Prophet.
They say Vijayan is a hero
who plays with the bow;
this man is a Tamil hero
who plays with words.
When I return after many victories
to receive an award,
if someone asks what reward I want,
I shall say: “Give me Abdul Rahman.”
He sings now.

(Poet Abdul Rahman completes his poem on “Division.”)

<!-- scan 203 -->

**Chair — Kalaignar:**

Next: addition.
Kannadasan, who once stood in the subtraction account,
has today joined us in the addition account.
We are friends who joined in Salem.
Whose permission do we need
to join again in Salem?

They call my friend *Kaviyarasu* — King of Poets.
I become angry:
I am only a minister,
and he is a king, is he not?
Even if he is a king,
I shall say he is my spy.
For ten years, sir,
he went around in disguise
circling the enemy camp.
Today he has taken a new birth
and returned here.

I sent him flying as a cuckoo to sing;
but will a cuckoo's song carry
inside a gathering of owls?
So I called him back into my own garden.
Cuckoo who has come to the garden!
Parrot perched upon my shoulder!
Let the poem of addition resound
and plunge us into delight.

(Poet Kannadasan completes his poem on “Addition.”)

**Chair — Kalaignar, concluding:**

Addition and subtraction,
division and multiplication—
we trusted that they would form an alliance,
coo and embrace one another,
and so we gave them our vote!
But—

<!-- scan 204 -->

this alliance,
even if it is an alliance of quarrels,
is in Tamil a golden ornament,
an unyielding lion-band of song.

Which is greater:
addition, subtraction, multiplication or division?
To answer,
I alter one Tirukkural
and announce a new judgement.

The Tirukkural says:
“Shaving and growing are needless
if the world casts away what it condemns.”

My judgement says:
“Division and multiplication are needless,
if addition and subtraction are cast away!”

That is my verdicting voice.

Greetings.
Long live!

## Translator notes

- `கணக்கு` ranges across arithmetic, bookkeeping, social calculation, political calculation and domestic “counting.” The English title **Arithmetic** foregrounds the four operations that organise the second half while the translation keeps “account/count/calculation” where the Tamil shifts register.
- `பதினெண் கீழ்க் கணக்கு` refers to the classical Eighteen *Keelkanakku* works; it is retained as a literary title rather than turned into an arithmetic joke alone.
- The poem depends heavily on Tamil lexical play: `கணக்குப் பிள்ளை`, marriage/family “counts,” the many hands of gods, Salem wordplay, Murugu/*perukku* (“multiply/increase”), and Kannadasan moving from “subtraction” to “addition.” These are preserved as source rhetoric rather than silently replaced with invented English puns.
- The closing verse deliberately rewrites a Tirukkural into the four arithmetic operations. The English distinguishes the quoted Kural from Kalaignar's comic verdict and does not alter the final-cleared Tamil witness.
