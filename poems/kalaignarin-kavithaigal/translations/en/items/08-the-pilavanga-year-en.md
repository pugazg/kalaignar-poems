# The Pilavanga Year

<!-- scan 90 -->

The Pilavanga Year

A presiding poetic address by
Kalaignar M. Karunanidhi
at a *kaviyarangam* broadcast by Chennai Radio
on 13 April 1968.

★

Greetings.

Pushing the “Pilavanga” year behind,
“Keelaka” has burst open like a clove pod.
Let us wish that people everywhere in the world
may live without riots and without rancour.
Though I say the year's birth is in Thai,
this has entered common practice, so let us offer greetings.
Why practise such thrift in blessing,
blessing only one day in a whole year?
With full mouth and full heart I will bless on many days,
and I greet the poets who have come to sing on the radio.

Sleep has broken; long eyes have opened.
The maiden Chithirai, a golden woman bearing a seal,
has risen.
Poets of ten-and-a-half purity,
and learned Valluvar whom this earth praises —
let us enter the Kural he composed
and taste the thought it offers here.

<!-- scan 91 -->

There is no fame merely by birth;
fame comes from the excellence one earns —
so he sang in the Book of Virtue.
Good people have come to open that book in song
so that we do not forget it.

I am a bird that does not fly,
a chick of poetry distressed because wings of grammar have not sprouted;
a bird that does not fly,
a bird that does not fly from nest to nest,
a poetry chick whose wings have not sprouted —
let only a tail not sprout, as it has for some!
That is the little poet I call myself.

Do not think I say this from modesty.
Feet, metre, linkage, ornament and prosody —
all of them only block my song!
You must grant me an exemption;
otherwise I shall rise saying I must walk away from you.

One day while asleep I saw a dream.
Do not ask, “Then what — do dreams come while awake?”
Gandhi dreamed while awake and won freedom;
Anna, smiling through the abuse of many,
dreamed while awake of good government,
and soon obtained it.
Let that stand.

What was this servant's sleeping dream?

<!-- scan 92 -->

You are eager to know!
Six women, six lovely maidens,
with bodies like hammered gold,
soft as lily flowers,
beauties whom anyone would long to drink in with the eyes.
South of the village called Honey-Pond,
under the shade beside the public tank,
I sat in a car with my eyes closed in sleep.

“We are jackfruits held within the root;
is there anywhere in the world a maiden like us?” they said.
“What is your request? Tell me,” I asked
those women with kovai-fruit lips.
Before I could finish,
“Choose one beauty from the six;
live your life rejoicing with her.
Tell us now without delay —
time is passing without interest!” they said.

“Are you girls from a Chettiar household?” I asked.
“There is no caste that butts and clashes, no religion —
we are humankind, that is all,” they said.
I too had desire,
but the choosing bewildered me.
All six had one complexion, one nature,
no difference in age;
in the twin fruit trees upon their dew-flower bodies,
in the grace of their walk,
in the spear-army of their eyes,
in the shopfront of their lips,
there was no contradiction; even their smiles were one.
If one woman's body was silk,
all six were silk;
if one was crystal,
all six were crystal;
if one spoke in parrot-speech,
all six were parrots.

<!-- scan 93 -->

If one spoke the path of grace,
so did all six;
if one spoke in the language of the Kural,
all six spoke the same.

What was I to do? How could I choose?
With spear-eyes they wounded my heart,
made music in my chest for love,
and by a wonder that made earth itself,
the six maidens together
plucked out my eyes and left me suffering in confusion.

Very well, I thought: ask their names,
then choose the one we want.
Each gave a name:
“Possession of Grace” was one;
“Self-Control” another;
“Sweet Speech” another;
“Forbearance” said a flower-maiden;
“Freedom from Anger” smiled another;
“My name is Not Doing Hurt,”
said the sixth, stepping forward.

If self-control is present, grace comes dancing;
where sweet speech lives, forbearance reigns;
a mind without anger
becomes a thumbai-grove where non-harm flowers.
Only the names differ — what else differs?
“Good women of equal age, equal nature, equal face —
who is the mother who bore you?” I asked.
“Tamil,” they said.
And their father? Valluvar!

<!-- scan 94 -->

They embraced me crying, “Anna!”
“Dear sisters, to take your hands
I shall bring six poets here.
After hearing the poems they speak,
you may garland them and rejoice,” I said.
They agreed, and the poets arrived.
But — one of those poets was a woman!
“Where is the male poet for the sixth woman?” they asked.
I started awake.
With the dream shattered, I came to this poetry gathering.
The poet who broke the dream, Soundara Kailasam,
is now in the middle of this assembly.
So, since it is good for her now to speak to the assembly,
and since she will speak on “Sweet Speech,”
I shall speak no more and step aside.
The lady will thunder on the theme “Sweet Speech.”

(Mrs. Soundara Kailasam completes her poem on “Sweet Speech.”)

Chairman Kalaignar:

Next, self-control.
Knowledge stands as the field in which self-control bears fruit;
like a chutney it cures disease —
it cures the disease of arrogance too.

<!-- scan 95 -->

We say feet are for walking, not kicking!
If a knife is only for folding away, it is unnecessary;
a knife is needed to cut fruit, not take life.
Courage rising from restraint is true courage;
the loud courage of the tongue is counterfeit courage.
The five senses must be drawn in like a tortoise,
but does that mean wandering the town
mute, blind and deaf?
The eye restrained at the eyelid-door;
the tongue restrained in the prison of the mouth;
breath restrained in the air;
the body restrained in the grave;
a good mind restrained in the light of reason.

To thank those who ordered me to preside is assembly-modesty;
to bless those who begged me to preside would be swelling of the head.
“Guard the tongue, whoever you are” — the first step of restraint;
to keep a “mad dog” inside the mouth is the upper stair of arrogance.
If it rolls out from there, the heart-pressure itself may collapse without form!
To offer all this in the assembly,
Manikkavasagam comes.
Let us receive him.

(Poet Manikkavasagam completes his poem on “Self-Control.”)

<!-- scan 96 -->

Chairman Kalaignar:

Next, forbearance.
The crescent waits and becomes the moon;
those with forbearance wait and come to rule the land.
The crane waits until prey comes;
do not call that forbearance —
it is strategy the bird learned upon the earth.
The one who makes enduring insult the highest virtue,
Valluvar said, is like earth that bears those who dig it.
Those who insult possess neither true greatness
nor the art of scribbling abuse.
Following behind those who praise is bad;
attacking those who insult is worse.
The heart of one rich in forbearance is a wave-filled sea;
those who strike it seeking to stain it
will end up gasping like fish on dry land.
It is easy to stab with deficient words;
it is hard to bear them with a full heart.
To pour this out under the title “Forbearance,”
comes the eager poet Aatral Arasu.

(Poet Aatral Arasu completes his poem on “Forbearance.”)

Chairman Kalaignar:

Next, freedom from anger.
“Anger kills the self,”
said Tamil sage Valluvar in a Kural.
Those in anger leap up, eyes reddened;
and those floating in drink stagger,

<!-- scan 97 -->

eyes reddened — the two are alike.
Jealousy is no good;
helplessness is no good;
ignorance destroys a country;
like non-killing, one great goodness
is freedom from anger.
If alienation enters, home is ruined, country is ruined.
Anger has a hundred faces:
teeth grinding,
lips pressed,
eyes bulging —
a hundred forms.
Its hundred faces shift like drunken frenzy;
Noor Muhammad's bright song will cool it.

(Poet Noor Muhammad completes his poem on “Freedom from Anger.”)

Chairman Kalaignar:

Next, not doing hurt.
This too is an “-amai,” but a good one;
not learning it is a great evil.
“If struck on one cheek, show the other,” said Jesus.
“To punish those who do hurt, do them good
until they are ashamed,” said the Kural.
All this talk of *innaa* — hurt —
those who pour out whatever comes to the tongue,
saying “my tongue is simply trained so,”
within the ranks of intelligence —

<!-- scan 98 -->

will stand neither in front nor behind;
they will lie in a corner.
Suratha, poet of flavour,
will explain all this with stacked words.

(Poet Suratha completes his poem on “Not Doing Hurt.”)

Chairman Kalaignar:

Next, possession of grace.
“This world is for those with wealth;
the other world for those with grace” —
Valluvar lit a lamp in darkened hearts.
There are many inquiries into what that “other world” is.
We know those who say it is a good world
where some living beings leave this one.
Should we believe fibre can be peeled from stone?
People peeled words and called them heaven and hell,
scattered sesame and said one could travel there.
Let the whole truth bloom like a rose born among thorns.
Where is knowledge? Where is fruit? All becomes waste.
Heaven written in palm-leaf and letters —
helping the poor here is heaven;
practising simplicity is sweet heaven;
speaking sweet words here is heaven;
living after death through one's work is deathless heaven.
For attaining this world, he said, one needs grace;
pleasant poet Thooran has come to explain it.

(Poet Thooran completes his poem on “Possession of Grace.”)

<!-- scan 99 -->

Chairman Kalaignar:

I thought he would heap flowers — poetry-flowers;
I was deceived.
I waited thinking that if he gathered flowers
I could string them into a garland.

Instead,
he has beautifully tied one garland for each person:
one for the feet of Mother Tamil;
one for Valluvar's shoulders;
one each for Virtue, Wealth and Love;
and because one garland remains,
what great person shall we seek to wear it?
On this New-Year day let us all wear it.
The world cannot be reformed in one day;
let us strive on many days — first
reform ourselves, then set out to reform the country.
“If a ruler is easy of access and not harsh of speech,
is he then fit to rule?” —
he would not hesitate to enter the witness-box.
Easy of access, disciple of Gandhi;
not harsh in speech, servant of Buddha;
it is good that such a one rule;
he is fitting testimony to the majesty of the Kural.

He speaks sweetly.
He does no hurt.
He guards restraint.
He honours grace.
He does not fall short in forbearance.
He does not know the surge of anger.
This

<!-- scan 100 -->

path of love will make peace prevail.
On the other path turmoil is born:
lifting knives, raising riots,
turning mustard seed into mountain —
a storm that disturbs the sea of loving peace.
To turn it into a breeze requires clarity,
great knowledge.
That is what we spread through the Kural.
We shall see victory in our principle.

Greetings.

✤

## Translator notes

- `பிலவங்க` and `கீலக` are traditional cyclic year-names; the title retains **Pilavanga** rather than replacing it with a Gregorian equivalent.
- The six women in the dream personify six Tirukkural virtues: grace, self-control, sweet speech, forbearance, freedom from anger and non-harm.
- The source uses repeated Tamil suffix/pun patterns (`-ஆமை`, including `ஆமை`, tortoise) that cannot be reproduced fully in English; the conceptual and comic progression is retained.
- Parenthetical poet hand-offs are source structure; the guest poets' poems themselves are not contained in this canonical item.
