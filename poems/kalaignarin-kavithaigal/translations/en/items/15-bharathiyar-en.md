# Bharathiyar

<!-- scan 170 -->

### Bharathiyar

Presiding poetic address by
Kalaignar M. Karunanidhi
at the Bharati Festival *kaviyarangam*
held at Vani Mahal, Chennai,
on 12 September 1969.

★

Greetings.
Narana Duraikannan, sculptor of fine writing,
gave the welcome poem.
In elegant Tamil he said here,
“Karunanidhi has reduced his harsh words.”
No, no — I have merely learned
to raise the rod sharply and let it fall softly. That is all!

Elders seated in the midst of this assembly,
learned great ones,
beloved mothers,
and distinguished poets who have come
with Tamil-rich verse to show us the form of rare poet Bharati —
I bow to you.

Twelve months plus another eighteen —
thirty months have now passed
since I assumed office as a minister.
The lion who used to raise angry questions is not here,
but he stands in sculpted form on the seashore.
To sing his fame, Avvai has invited me.

<!-- scan 171 -->

Ah! There are eighteen Avvais —
which Avvai do you mean, you ask?
You shall know:
the nineteenth Avvai,
a jewel among masters of the threefold Tamil arts,
is the one who invited me!

(Next, Kalaignar, chairing the *kaviyarangam*,
sings on Bharathiyar's distinction to complete his introduction,
then calls the participating poets in order
and introduces them to the assembly.)

First, Va. Mu. Sethuraman
will explain through song
the Tamil consciousness Bharati saw.
Va. Mu. Sethuraman, come!
Give us Tamil:

(Poet Va. Mu. Sethuraman finishes his poem on
“Bharati's Tamil Consciousness.”)

Chairman Kalaignar:

Bharati understood that the whole story
told by the grove-cuckoo about love
was a device of imagination
born from the enchantment of evening beauty.
He asked:
“Even if it is imagination,
if there is some little room
to unfold it as Vedanta and explain its meaning,
will someone speak?”
Accepting Bharati's invitation,
our dear Thooran has arrived.
Let us leave Thooran near that yearning cuckoo
and stand a little farther away;
only when Tamil sounds shall we come close.

(Poet Periyasamy Thooran finishes his poem on
“Bharati's Cuckoo.”)

<!-- scan 172 -->

Chairman Kalaignar:

You have seen the Parasakthi I saw!
The traditionalist asks, “Why did we see her?”
but hides only the news he himself saw.
Taking honey-filled Tamil,
Bharathiyar, in the name of Parasakthi
seen by the deer of devotion,
gives valour reaching to the very sky.
Viswabharathi will explain it:

(Poet Viswabharathi finishes his poem on
“Bharati's Parasakthi.”)

Chairman Kalaignar:

I saw Anna —
as mother, as Tamil,
as the eldest child of the homeland,
as a form of love that knew no barrier.
Bharati saw Kannan.
Some say Krishna and Kannan are one;
some say they are not.
Ku. Sa. Ki. will light a lamp of inquiry in the darkness
and tell us without confusion:

(Poet Ku. Sa. Krishnamurthi finishes his poem on
“Bharati's Kannan.”)

Chairman Kalaignar:

Bharati never saw in person
Panchali, wife of Partha.
But there is one here who has seen her.
Look — he is Bhishma himself!

<!-- scan 173 -->

You who once lay beneath a rain of arrows,
come now and pour a rain of poetry!

(Poet Bhishmar finishes his poem on
“Bharati's Panchali.”)

Chairman Kalaignar:

Eyes that dance beneath artificial brows,
red paint on the lips,
fierce competition in half-skirts and other dress,
Ajanta sculptures piled upon the head,
walking like an exhibition object —
that is not the New Woman!
Then who is she, you ask?
There is a lady here to tell you.
To see the New Woman,
do not shift your seat;
stay exactly where you are:

(Poet Ira. Rasalakshmi finishes her poem on
“Bharati's New Woman.”)

Chairman Kalaignar's concluding address:

Since we are holding a festival
for Subramania Bharati of Pondicherry,
Subramania Kavirayar of Puthaneri did not come,
lest it appear he was arriving in competition!
So I shall go to the conclusion — forgive me.

Bharati honoured womanhood.
Looking at human beings trampled like earth,
he said, “Do not sell yourself.”

<!-- scan 174 -->

“Even if enemies offer me crores,
I will not touch it,”
he gave the cry of freedom.
He stood as a good ray of sunlight
to remove the black darkness of slavery.
He called the man who lies idle without serving “a tortoise.”
He lit lamp after lamp of poetry.
To that Bharati came an heir — Bharathidasan.
For a leader, a poet, or any scholar of distinction,
the lineage that remains after him
is itself the measure of worth.
Wherever we look,
we see Bharati's lineage;
we see descendants in Bharathidasan's path;
we laugh and rejoice with a Bharati-like radiance!

Greetings.
Long live!

## Translator notes

- This is a distinct Bharati *kaviyarangam* item, not a continuation of item 14. The adjacent placement and overlapping literary references are preserved without merging their identities.
- The opening `கடிதோச்சி மெல்ல எறிக` joke and the “nineteenth Avvai” passage are kept as stage rhetoric; English prioritises the source's teasing cadence over literal word-for-word equivalence.
- Embedded descriptions of Bharati's Kannan, Parasakthi, Panchali and New Woman are translated as Kalaignar's source framing, not externally harmonised with other editions or interpretations.
