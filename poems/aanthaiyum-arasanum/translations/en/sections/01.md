# Andhai and the King!

<!-- scan_page: 18 -->

M. Karunanidhi

In the age when bodies were buried in great urns,
on the southern land ringed by the sea,
Kozhiyur was another name for Uraiyur;
crying, “Long live noble Tamil!” the kings—

<!-- scan_page: 19 -->

drew their swords and stood guard in the forefront—
then shaped literature upon palm leaves!
Like the swaying tuft of the Cholas,
the house-cock of the plough-rich farmer
shakes its head! Torments its foe!

We say the dog is the cat's enemy,
the hunter the tiger's enemy,
the vulture the pigeon's enemy;
have you ever read that a rooster is an elephant's enemy?
There is literature that tells how, in Uraiyur,
one rooster brought its mighty feud with an elephant to an end;
and that tale has indeed tasted sweet,
like a thick-cut piece of sugarcane!
    Because valour had settled through all the soil,
    the people called it Uraiyur!
The place where a sword rests is called its urai—its sheath.
The place where sword-like warriors dwell is Uraiyur—
                                so it resounds.

★                                 ★

The valiant warrior who ruled that city,
friend of poets who poured out golden Tamil,
a poet who strayed from neither metre nor ornament—
Kopperuncholan, a great scholar of Tamil.
Boothanathar; Pullarrur Eyitriyanar;
the renowned Nathathan; Poththiyar;
Pisirandhai, sovereign of speech, praised by Poovarasu—
the heart of the garlanded king
was the lovely hall where
all these poets of surging language
came to dwell!

★                                 ★

Grown in honey, blossomed in honey,
soaked in honey, squeezing forth honey,
like a flower of honey......
born in Tamil, raised by Tamil,
threaded with Tamil, clothed in Tamil—
the worthy ones lived in friendship that never cooled!

<!-- scan_page: 20 -->

Karuvur Chathukka Boothanathar
gave in abundance the Tamil tongue,
shade-giving as a tree,
with meanings rare and rich.
The king whose ministers spoke of matters to come said,
“I shall be the fibre joined to a fragrant flower;
bind me to you in friendship,”
and embraced him;
Boothanathar swelled with joy!
    Like a waterfall bearing floating flowers,
    Pullalur Eyitriyanar,
    who sang Puram songs, became his friend.
“To see poets—to cherish them—
to sing of learning—is sweet at every hour,”
he would say; praising friendship,
he rejoiced!

*                                      *

Friendship is mixing poison,
trampling until the good heart aches,
speaking baseness beyond the eye's reach—
all these wonders happen today!
But in the adorned life of Tamils of old,
unsullied friendship shone nobly.

<!-- scan_page: 21 -->

Poets who saw, heard, tasted, breathed—
who offered Tamil as a bouquet—becoming
the king's friends was no wonder;
but a poet he had never seen became
the Chola guardian's friend—and a river
of honeyed love rose in flood
and ran between the two!
Under the shade of the fish-bearing Pandya flag
there gleamed a town;
Pisir was the ancient town,
the great poet Andhaiyar's birthplace.
His parents had given him the name Andhaiyar;
joining name and town, he became Pisirandhaiyar.
So that learned people might delight and rejoice,
he spread nurturing Tamil through the land.
Others spoke of him; the Chola heard,
and poured upon him an unfailing love
that surpassed the bond of kin.

    The king of verse had never seen the king of earth;
    the king of earth had never seen the king of verse;
    yet unseen, the two sovereigns came to love each other,
    and to the end their time passed so.

*                                      *

Then, in flourishing Kozhiyur,
an event of shameful nature arose—
                                        through counsel
that destroyed wisdom, words of fire from evil men;
the royal sons had eyes, yet lacked the power to awaken.
Whenever they had sprouted, upon shoulder and chest,
with tender hands giving comfort, he had raised them;
now they marched against their father, a mountain of light.
Seeing his own creation advance
to destroy his own life,
with a wounded heart he lifted
                                the sword of war.
Was having eyes itself a crime, to witness this sight?
“The Chola land's great fortune is gone,”
thought all the wise whose hearts were cool as the moon;
they stood pleading with both sides:
                                    “Let not
war be waged.”

<!-- scan_page: 22 -->

“Take your counsel and run with it
to flower-like women around whom bees dance!
Do not stop the festival of killing
celebrated by broad-shouldered warriors!”
So he refused them;
    son and father cut through the bond of love.
As the two armies moved to clash,
the great war-drum thundered there!
The bitter feud swelled;
the battlefront itself groaned!
    Determined to strive for peace to the very end,
    one poet set out.
Cutting through the ranks at the battlefront,
Pullarrur Eyitriyanar entered.
“Friendship is not for laughter;
it is for stepping forward and rebuking”—
to make that Tamil utterance true,
he held his head high and approached the garlanded king.
“In this Tamil land fenced by the roaring sea,
if the heads of your sons fall like seed-pods,
will such fragrant victory bring you joy?

<!-- scan_page: 23 -->

O king! After the sons you fathered are dead,
who then is left to live within the fortress
                                and rule Kozhiyur?
If your sons win, disgrace will surround you!
If your sons die, there will be no child
                        to rule Uraiyur.
This is the truth—understand it!” he said, melting with feeling.
The guardian, his heart burning,
poured water from his eyes like a waterfall
and left the battlefield.

*                                      *

“When honour is threatened, death is good”—
he came from a tradition of such a mind.
Dishonour had arisen—valour had perished—
heaven itself reproached him! “Is living now good?”
he asked,
a question wrapped in grief;
slowing his pace, he went north.

*                                      *

There is a custom called sitting facing north—a custom
kings take up to consume their own life.
Fasting without food, wasting and growing thin,
until the body falls upon the earth,
they remain there, accepting it as bliss—
this is what is called sitting facing north.

*                                      *

Kopperuncholan's northern fast,
in Tamilakam radiant with great fame,
made the hearts of the young, the old,
and those still like buds
feel that the land had been left without a protector;
grief flared everywhere.
The poets bound to him by friendship
would not go away; they remained nearby.

The great king who had understood the Three Parts—his end
came unfailingly near—he weakened.
“Pisirandhai, who has always lived in my heart—
will he come now to see me?”

<!-- scan_page: 24 -->

he asked those around him.
“He will not come,” they said—“he does not know
the news of your northern fast.”
“Andhaiyar's town is very far away,” they said.
“So coming here is impossible,” they said.
Hearing this,
the king of letters struck back with his words:
“My dear friend, who even takes my name for his own—
a spring of noble character—
when he hears that I am sitting facing north,
tomorrow—or today itself—he will come and join me;
not for a single moment will he forget me, I say.”
    Hearing the earth-king speak
    of love, without ever seeing him,
    for Andhai, king among poets,
    his intimate companions fell mute.

*                                      *

<!-- scan_page: 25 -->

The poet Poththiyar too had come,
to sit facing north beside his sacred friend!
The Chola stopped him and said:
“Your moon-faced wife is full with child—
when she has borne the child of babbling speech,
then come and stay beside me.”
Poththiyar agreed
                                and went!

*                                      *

The king, filled with love and righteousness—
his bone and skin grew thinner and thinner;
his eyes darkened,
his legs curled,
his hands fell still,
his hearing faded,
his speech ceased,
his breath faltered;
only thought wandered somewhere.
In the king's mind was the thought of Andhai—
“The radiant poet will come, will come—
he will give me sugarcane-sweet Tamil, will give it”—
thinking only this,
    he reached his end!
Pitch dissolved!
Music itself collapsed!
The Chola king, who knew no scorching word,
became a hero-stone, praised by the people!
Crossing many miles, the king of verse,
Andhai of the Pandya land, arrived.
He saw the stone of the dead king.
“I do not wish to live again,” he said.
“There is a place for me here,” he said;
before the stone of the king wearied by rule,
he stretched himself out and sat facing north—
and through countless ages they live on!
