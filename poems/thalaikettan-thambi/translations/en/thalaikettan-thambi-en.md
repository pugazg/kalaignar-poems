# The Younger Brother Who Asked for a Head

<!-- scan_page: 18 -->

Taking sugarcane, squeezing Tamil from it—in the flower,
taking the bee that comes bearing honey,
taking the bud, the gathering of women smiles:
“Even the food that ants carry away
was given by our king, generous Kumanan,” they say—
Kongu land strides in exultation.

*

Every poet who sings Tamil
comes singing Kumanan's praise—
words roar like the waves of the resounding sea;
the melody of the nectar called Tamil resounds;
a fragrant garden of flowers of verse—a gathering
of hearts where love glides.

With seal-stamped Tamil shaping verse,
there was a poet named Perunchithiranar—a great scholar!
A chaste wife, children, and the womb that bore the poet—
treasures like gold of the purest touch;
yet the joy he found upon this earth was poverty,
and he grew terribly thin through hunger day after day!
His wife wilted and withered like a flower;
his mother curled up like a worm;
from hunger, his child rolled the pot about like a cat!
The poet stood wringing his hands;
then he remembered generous Kumanan,
whose hand had reddened from giving, and set out.

*

“Cast away the thought,
‘After Pari and Kari, who remains to give?’
Scoop up the wealth I give!
O Kongu king who beats his chest proclaiming,
‘Kumanan is but another name for rain!’
I have one request—my family,” he said.
The king poured out gifts.
“I need gold to live.
On the road by which I came lived Veliman;
I walked there to receive what he would give.
Alas, king! A year has passed since he died—
his younger brother Ilaveliman is there now;
he refuses to give,
and speaks a cruel reply.
O guardian who rules wealth-pouring Mudiramalai!
To break the arrogance of that base Ilaveliman,
give me an elephant;
let it be tied to his guard-tree,
so that the low fellow must bow his head.
Give it to me,” he said.
Kumanan gave it.

Just as heroic Veliman had a younger brother, Ilaveliman,
King Kumanan too had one;
his name was Ilankumanan.
“Jewels, elephants, armies, palaces—all of them—
if you give them away just so poets may live,
our country, brother, will become a mendicant's monastery
without even a scrap of cloth!
I cannot allow this any longer;
divide my share for me right now,” he said.
“I have seen the trees of fruit-shedding Mudiramalai—
and I have seen your mouth, brother,
shedding red unripe fruit.
What jewels do I need?
What do I need in the palace?
Elephants, armies—take them all..
Only give me the love that says,
‘An elder has a younger brother!’”
So saying, generous Kumanan wept.
But the feeling of enmity that had put down roots
and grown in the younger brother would not subside.
The man whose heart had gone rotten said:
“Before the red-rayed one who ploughed across the sky returns,
bow to my country and go to the forest!”
So he ordered him away.

*

Kumanan left.

<!-- scan_page: 21 -->

Deer, honey-birds, peacocks, cuckoos and house-pigeons
showed the king a love greater
than the human race displays,
and so he lived happily in the forest;
but there was no poet to offer him sugarcane-sweet Tamil—
that alone was his unending sorrow; there was no other!
As treachery flared into flame,
the younger brother seized the sceptre into his own hand!
Was he content with that much?
Cruel friends stirred the resentment buried in his mind.
“I have been patient until this day—
my elder brother has prepared to make war on me.
Do not stop me;
for whoever cuts off my elder brother's head
and brings it here,
ten thousand gold pieces as reward!” he said.
“To cut off his head? What clan-destroying news is this!

*

His heart is as vast as a sea that beats in waves;
for every poet who fashions the art of Tamil,
he is a spring of giving!
Mudiramalai itself will melt away—
if the crowned king's head is cut off,
all our blood will freeze!”
So saying, they spat upon
the purse of gold the ruler displayed.

With the thought of stringing flowers of Tamil
and placing them upon the victorious king's shoulder,
a poet came;
his name was Sathanar.
Learning that Kumanan was living in the forest,
he grieved.

*

It was like finding a grove in the desert,
like meeting a breeze in the evening;
in the morning Kumanan saw the poet,
and feasted deeply on Tamil written upon the palm-leaf!
“On that day you did not come, rare poet;
today you have come to me in suffering.
Take my head to my younger brother,
give it to him,
receive its price,
and cure the disease of your poverty!”
said Kumanan—and drew his sword.
The poet stopped him—and left the forest!
Then he approached the younger brother,
ruined by his own words.

*

“Where?” he asked—and loosed his shafts of speech:
“Your elder had believed,
‘He who has a younger brother fears no army.’
You have cut your elder brother's throat;
you promised gold as reward
to whoever cut off his head.
Here is the sword that severed his head—
give me my reward,” he said.
The younger brother saw his elder's sword—
and saw red upon the sword-point.
“Have you fallen, brother!”
he screamed and wailed.
Poisoned heart—frenzied heart—
at the news that his elder had given his life,
it burst like a mountain split by an explosion—
the younger brother's now-pure heart
cried aloud.
“The ochre-robed Kumanan said in the forest,
‘Even if I must give my life,
I will give it only for precious Tamil.’
Sinner, through the wrong you committed
I learned those golden words.
Run—go and fall at that man's feet;
not merely your elder's life,
Tamil itself will come alive!
Come without delay;
touch the sword-point!
It is not blood—it is red colour,”
said the poet.
Why would Ilankumanan stand there any longer?

Like flies circling those who sell honey,
a gathering of poets who weave verse in lofty Tamil
circled King Kumanan.
At his feet Ilankumanan bowed and fell—
the generous king embraced him.
The man who had consented
to give his head as a gift to Tamil Sathanar
now gave his younger brother
forgiveness as another gift.
The younger brother gave up the royal parasol,
saying he no longer wanted it;
the elder brother gave it to Tamil!
